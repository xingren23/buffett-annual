## 问题

1976 年巴菲特在悼念本杰明·格雷厄姆时引用沃尔特·李普曼的话，称格雷厄姆是「种树以便他人乘凉」的人，并说两位都是这样的人。他请两人分享对伯克希尔的百年愿景。

## 回答要点

1. 巴菲特称格雷厄姆为他做了各种事，从不期待任何回报，也没有任何期待回报的暗示。
2. 格雷厄姆 1949 年的著作说服了他，此前八九年间投入并热爱的方法「全都是错的」。
3. 该书在亚马逊长期位居约第 300—350 名；哈珀柯林斯表示销量记录已追溯不到起点，但累计售出 730 万册。
4. 巴菲特称多数投资书排名会从数百名跌到 2.5 万—20 万名，没有第二本书能维持这种地位。
5. 百年愿景是：由股东拥有、其存在令社会满意而非不满，拥有无限资本与人才基础，可像那本书一样延续下去。
6. 芒格指出格雷厄姆一生投资收益过半来自一只成长股 GEICO，他晚年对此略感不好意思；买入被低估的伟大公司是极好的事。

## 关键引述

> **“Ben Graham did all kinds of things for me, and he never expected one thing in return.”**
>
> 「本·格雷厄姆为我做了各种各样的事，而他从不期待任何回报。」

> **“more than half of all the investment return that Ben Graham made in his whole life came from one stock, one growth stock: GEICO, Berkshire subsidiary.”**
>
> 「本·格雷厄姆一生全部投资收益中，超过一半来自一只股票、一只成长股：GEICO，伯克希尔的子公司。」

> **“buying one undervalued great company is a very good thing, as Berkshire has found out again and again and again.”**
>
> 「买下一家被低估的伟大公司是件非常好的事，伯克希尔已经一次又一次地发现了这一点。」

## 中文全文（逐段翻译）

> 与下方「英文原文」逐段对应，按发言顺序排列；术语与专名首次出现时保留英文。

**Warren Buffett**（02:18:41）：好，十号提问站？

**Audience Member**（02:18:42）：首先，谢谢你们让我们的生活变得更好。我叫 Bugomil Baranowsky，是纽约 Sicart Associates 的创始合伙人。我们管理跨代家族财富，所以才有此一问。巴菲特先生，1976 年您在悼念本杰明·格雷厄姆时写道：「沃尔特·李普曼谈到那些种树以便他人乘凉的人。」本·格雷厄姆就是这样的人。你们两位也都是这样的人。能否与我们分享您对伯克希尔的百年愿景？这个问题是问你们两位的。

**Warren Buffett**（02:19:20）：好。关于本·格雷厄姆，我想补充一点。本·格雷厄姆为我做了各种各样的事，而他从不期待任何回报。我的意思是，你说得出来的事他都做了，其中没有任何隐藏的——应该说，没有任何一丝他期待回报的暗示。我查过：他在 1949 年写了一本书，某种意义上是用非常有说服力的措辞告诉我，我此前八九年里投入并热爱的事业全都是错的。那本书一直——我时不时在亚马逊上看它的排名，你知道，亚马逊按销量给几十万本书排名。本·格雷厄姆的那本书一直就在那儿，大概第 300 名、350 名左右，一直如此。没有哪本书像它。前几天我给哈珀柯林斯写了封信，因为他们要出新的版本。我问他们卖了多少册。他们说记录追溯不了那么久远，但这本改变了我一生的小书已经卖出 730 万册，而且至今仍然比任何一本投资书都畅销。

投资书一本本出来，你知道，它们会有一阵子排在第 400 名、1000 名左右，然后突然就掉到第 25000 名，或者（笑）20 万名。而这本书——你能在多少个领域找到任何一本保持这种持续地位的书？你回头看看 1950 年的第一名、第二名或第三名，再看 51 年、52 年：它们没有延续下去。我的意思是，它们就是不延续。食谱书，也许有一两本能撑一阵，但除此之外什么都没有。而这本书活了下来，所有人都在不断推出新书，讲很多别的东西。但他们讲的任何东西，都不如他在 1949 年那本相对薄薄的小书里讲的那么重要。所以，你知道，我们对伯克希尔的愿景正是我们今天所说的：我们希望它是一家由股东拥有、并且其行为方式让社会乐于见到它存在、而不是不满它存在的公司。

我们将拥有无限的资本，我们会吸引到大量人才，我们有一个无人能敌的基础。没有理由它不能像本的书那样延续下去，也许还能成为其他人的榜样。如果真是这样，我们会非常高兴。查理？

**Charlie Munger**（02:23:06）：是的。关于本·格雷厄姆，真正有意思的一点是：他是一位极有天赋的老师，这是一个非常可敬的职业。而留存下来的正是这个。不过有个有趣的事实，他晚年对此有点不好意思：本·格雷厄姆一生全部投资收益中，超过一半来自一只股票、一只成长股——GEICO，伯克希尔的子公司。在他操作的年代，有很多差劲的公司价格太便宜，你可以从一只倒到另一只，赚点小钱。但他赚的大钱来自一只成长股；买下一家被低估的伟大公司是件非常好的事，伯克希尔已经一次又一次地发现了这一点。

**Warren Buffett**（02:23:58）：本在 49 年版的后记里正好指出了这个事实，并承认了它，但也从中吸取了一些好的教训。他说：「你知道，生活就是这样：你做好准备，你知道，一路上不要在每件事上都亏掉，然后机会就来了。」GEICO 之所以出现，是因为沃斯堡的一位银行家曾为利奥 Davidson 提供融资——我想那位银行家拿了四分之三。我是说，不是「利奥 Davidson」，是利奥 Goodwin，他创立了 GEICO，当时叫 Government Employees Insurance Company，缩写的由来你自己想。那笔交易几乎告吹。我记得交易金额大概是 150 万还是多少——125 万。它几乎因为交割净资产差了 2.5 万美元而告吹。

这是一门如今价值数百亿美元的业务。但他也指出了其中的讽刺。我的意思是，他很诚实，对他的方法的缺陷和长处都做到了彻底的理智上的诚实。而在某种程度上，你知道，查理和我一生中都看到了这一点。我的意思是，那种有准备的头脑、在需要行动时愿意行动，以及愿意无视世上每一个推销员——无视他们是必须的，而正是这一两件事让你做出正确的决定。如果你在配偶这件事上做了正确的决定，我的意思是，你就赢了这场游戏。你知道，这是一个极其重要的决定，而你有的是时间。我的意思是，比我小时候做这个决定的时间多得多。而且，你知道，我不知道是有三分之一还是多少比例的人把这件事搞砸了。你知道，这真的很有意思。该做的就是不断努力把事情想透，不要做太多蠢事，「然后，迟早你会遇上一个 lollapalooza（多重因素叠加的大机会）」，就像查理会说的那样。

## 英文原文（CNBC 官方逐字稿）

**Warren Buffett**（02:18:41）：OK, station ten?

**Audience Member**（02:18:42）：First of all, thank you for making our lives better. My name is Bugomil Baranowsky. I'm a founding partner of Sicart Associates in New York. We manage multi-generational family fortunes, hence my question. Mr. Buffet, in 1976 in your tribute to Benjamin Graham you wrote, "Walter Lippmann spoke of men who plant trees that other men will sit under." Ben Graham was such a man. You're both such people. Could you share with us your 100-year vision for Berkshire? It's a question to you both.

**Warren Buffett**（02:19:20）：Yeah. I would like to add one thing about Ben Graham. Ben Graham did all kinds of things for me, and he never expected one thing in return. I mean, just you name it, and he did it, and there wasn't any hidden — you know, the slightest hint, I should say, of anything he expected in return. And I checked: He wrote a book in 1949 that, in a sense, said to me, in very persuasive terms, that what I'd been spending the previous eight or nine years worked at, and loving, was all wrong. And that book has been — I check it every now and then on Amazon where it ranks, and, you know, Amazon ranks hundreds and hundreds and hundreds of thousands of books by sales. And Ben Graham's book has been up there, like, number 300, or 350, or something like that, forever. And there isn't book like it. I wrote Harper Collins a note the other day because they're bringing out another edition. And I asked them how many copies have been sold. And they said the records didn't go back far enough, but they had 7.3 million copies of this little book that changed my life and continues to outsell every investment book ever. Investment books come along and, you know, they're number 400, or 1000 or something for a while, and then all of a sudden, they're number 25,000, or (Laugh) 200,000. And this book — you know, in how many areas can you find any book that has had that sustained position? You go back and look at number one in 1950, or number two or number three, and you look at it in '51 and '52: They don't continue. I mean, they just don't continue. Cookbooks, maybe one or two of them, last for a while, but there is nothing. And this book lives on, and everybody keeps bringing out new books, and saying a lot of other things. But they aren't saying anything that's as important as what he said in 1949 in this relatively thin little book. So, you know, our vision for Berkshire is exactly what we said today: We want it to be a company that is owned by shareholders and behaves in a way that society is happy that it exists, and not unhappy. And we will have unlimited capital, we'll get lots of talent, and we've got a base that can't be beat. And there's no reason why it can't be perpetuated, just like Ben's book, and maybe be an example to other people. And, if so, we'll be very happy. Charlie?

**Charlie Munger**（02:23:06）：Yeah. One of the really interesting things about Ben Graham: He was a really gifted teacher, a very honorable profession. And that is what has lasted. However, an interesting fact that he was sheepish about in his old age, was that more than half of all the investment return that Ben Graham made in his whole life came from one stock, one growth stock: GEICO, Berkshire subsidiary. And at the time he operated, there were a lot of sort of lousy companies that were too cheap, and you could make a little money floating from one to another. But the big money he made was one growth stock, buying one undervalued great company is a very good thing, as Berkshire has found out again and again and again.

**Warren Buffett**（02:23:58）：And Ben wrote a postscript to the '49 edition pointing out exactly that fact, and acknowledging it, but also took some good lessons from it. "You know," he said, "that's the way life is: That you prepare, and you, you know, don't lose on everything along the way, and then something comes along." And GEICO came along because a banker in Fort Worth that had financed Leo Davidson, and I think the banker got three-quarters of it. I don't mean "Leo Davidson," Leo Goodwin, who founded GEICO then called Government Employees Insurance Company, and you can figure out the acronym. The deal almost fell apart. The deal was, as I remember, for maybe a million and a half or something like — a million and a quarter. And it almost fell apart because of a difference of $25,000 in the net worth delivered. This is a business that's, you know, worth tens of billions. But he pointed out the irony in that, too. I mean, he was honest, he was totally intellectually honest about the failings, and also the strengths, of his approach. And, to some extent, you know, Charlie and I have seen that in our lives. I mean, sort of the prepared mind, the willingness to act when you need to act, and the willing to ignore every salesman in the world — and it's imperative to ignore them, and it's one or two things that make the right decision. If you make the right decision on a spouse, I mean, you've won the game. You know, there's an enormously important decision, and you've got all the time in the world. I mean, you've got more time than you used to have when I was a kid to make that decision. And, you know, I don't know whether a third, or whatever percentage, blow that one. You know, it is really interesting. The thing to do is just keep trying to think things through and not do too many stupid things, "And, sooner or later, you have a lollapalooza," as Charlie would say.
