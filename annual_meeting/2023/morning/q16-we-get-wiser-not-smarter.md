## 问题

做企业永远要面对一个两难：做能赚钱的产品，还是做能加固竞争地位的产品。最好的情况是两者兼得，谷歌就是如此；但多数时候公司必须在短期利润和长期防御性之间选一个。亚马逊早期就是压低利润建飞轮，换取更强的网络效应，指望日后利润更稳固。你们投资时总讲护城河，对 CEO 平衡这个矛盾有什么建议？

## 回答要点

1. 伯克希尔不开投资者电话会、不做业绩指引，因此没有来自华尔街的压力，可以按自己的节奏犯错。
2. 1972 年买下喜诗糖果是分水岭；1966 年进入百货生意，买价墨迹未干就意识到做错了。
3. 巴菲特自认学不会生意的技术细节，但懂消费者行为：人们怎么比较第二辆车和一部手机。
4. 苹果持仓约 5%—7%，因公司回购而每年上升；他说自己完全不懂那部手机，但懂人们怎么花钱。
5. 判断一笔生意有没有意思只需十秒钟，投行画的未来预测图没有意义。
6. 「我们不会随时间变聪明，只是变得聪明一点点。」

## 关键引述

## 中文全文（逐段翻译）

> 与下方「英文原文」逐段对应，按发言顺序排列；术语与专名首次出现时保留英文。

**Warren Buffett**（01:50:13）：好，8 号提问点。

**Audience Member**（01:50:14）：你好。我叫阿达尔·弗洛雷斯（音），做股东大约 16 年了，从墨西哥瓜达拉哈拉来。我的问题问沃伦和查理。公司永远面对一个两难：是造能赚钱的产品，还是造能提升公司竞争地位的产品。最好的情况是两者兼得，谷歌就做到了。但多数时候，公司必须在短期利润和长期防御性之间选一个。比如亚马逊，早期专注打造它那个著名的飞轮，利润很薄，为的是获得更强的网络效应，指望将来利润更稳固。你们投资时总讲护城河有多重要。对 CEO 们，你们有什么建议，怎么平衡这个两难——本质上是短期利润对长期防御性？谢谢。

**Warren Buffett**（01:51:21）：嗯，答案是掌控自己的命运，这一点我们在伯克希尔做到了。我们感受不到来自华尔街的压力。你知道，我们不开投资者电话会，不必做承诺。我们有机会自己犯错，偶尔也找到一些很管用的东西。但我们清楚，这间屋子里的人，以及和他们一样的人，才是我们为之工作的人。我们不是在为一群只关心我们有没有达到市场预期的人工作。所以我们拥有可以运用的自由。我们想永久持有一家出色的企业。我们从许多出色的企业身上学到东西。但我们也确实是一路走一路学。查理和我常提到，买下喜诗糖果时我们学到很多——确实如此。但买下本·罗斯纳那家遍布美国东部的女装连锁店时，我们也学到东西。1966 年试着进入百货生意时，我们也学到东西：买价的墨迹还没干（笑声），我们就意识到自己干了件蠢事。

我的意思是，我们一直在学消费者如何行事。我学不会生意的技术层面。要是懂当然好，但那不是必需的。你知道，显然我们有一块苹果的生意，比我们的能源业务还大。我们也许只拥有 5%、6% 或 7%。但我们的持股比例每年都在上升，而我完全不懂那部手机。可我懂消费者行为。我知道人们怎么想「要不要再买一辆车」。我知道他们怎么走进不同的——我们拥有汽车经销商。我们一直在从旗下所有生意里学：人们对 Garanimals 和对别的东西反应有什么不同。所以喜诗算是一个突破。但我们只是一直在学，学人们如何行事，学一家好生意如何变成坏生意，学某些好生意如何长期保住竞争优势。所以，伯克希尔的人并没有什么公式。但我们也能在十秒钟里判断出一样东西有没有意思。

我的意思是，那些人打电话来，想给我们送各种演示材料，全是没用的东西——就是一帮人（笑声）拿着钱，专门画未来的预测图之类。要是他们知道未来……我们不知道未来，但我们确实懂某几类生意。我们知道什么价格是对的，也知道在消费者行为和对生意的威胁方面，我们大致能推演出什么。这就是我们一直做的事，也是我们会继续做的事。我们不会随时间变聪明，但跟着时间走，我们会变得聪明一点点。而且这活儿坐在办公室里拿着电话就能干，这一点我们喜欢。查理？

## 英文原文（CNBC 官方逐字稿）

**Warren Buffett**（01:50:13）：OK, station eight.

**Audience Member**（01:50:14）：Hello. My name is Adal Flores (PH), and I've been a shareholder for about 16 years. And I'm coming from Guadalajara, Mexico. My question is for Warren and Charlie. Companies have the eternal dilemma between building products that can make profits and increase their company's competitive position. In the best case, you can build products that have both characteristics at the same time, like Google did. But most of the time, companies need to choose between short-term profits and long-term defensibility. For example, Amazon was focused on building their famous Amazon flywheel with limited profits initially, in order to obtain stronger network effects, with the hope of getting more defensible profits in the future. When you invest, you constantly speak about the importance of building competitive moats. What advice would you give to CEOs about how to balance this dilemma, which is essentially short-term profits versus long-term defensibility? Thank you.

**Warren Buffett**（01:51:21）：Well, the answer is to control your destiny, which we've been able to do at Berkshire. We feel no pressure from Wall Street. You know, we don't have investor calls. We don't have to make promises. We get a chance to make our own mistakes, and occasionally find something that works well. But we recognize that the people in this room and people like them are the ones that we're working for. And we're not working for a bunch of people that care about whether we meet the core estimate or anything. So, we have a freedom that we get to use. And we're interested in owning a wonderful business forever. We learned from many wonderful businesses. But we do learn a lot as we go along. Charlie and I have often mentioned how we learned so much when we bought See's Candies, which we did. But we learned when we bought Ben Rosner's chain of women's dress shops spread all over the eastern part of the country. We learned when we tried getting into the department store business back in 1966. And as the ink was drying on our purchase price, we realized we'd done something (Laugh) dumb. I mean, we're learning all the time how consumers behave. I'm not going to be able to learn the technical aspects of businesses. It'd be nice if I knew it, but it isn't essential. And, you know, obviously we've got a business at Apple, which is larger than our energy business. And we may only own five points, 6% or 7%. But our ownership goes up every year, and I don't understand the phone at all. But I do understand consumer behavior. And I know how people think about whether to buy a second car. I know how they go out to different — we own auto dealerships. We're learning all the time from all of our businesses how people react to Garanimals versus selling them something else. And so, See's was a sort of breakthrough. But we just keep learning as to more about how people behave and how a good business can turn into a bad business, and how some good businesses can maintain their competitive advantage over time. And so, we don't have some formula, Berkshire people. But we can also tell in ten seconds whether it's something of interest. I mean, when I get these calls and we want to send decks and all that sort of thing, which is nonsense, I mean, it's a bunch of guys (Laugh) that get paid for drawing up these projections of the future and everything like that. If they knew the future, you know, we don't know the future, but we do know certain kinds of businesses. We know what the right price is, and we know what we think we can project out in terms of consumer behavior and threats to a business. And that's what we've been about and that's what we'll continue to be about. We don't get smarter over time, we get a little wiser, though, following it over time. And you can do it while sitting in the office with a telephone, too, which we like. Charlie?
