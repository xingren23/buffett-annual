## 问题

你们一直说择时是不可能的。但看历史记录：1969、1970 年退出市场，1972、1974 年回来；1987、1999、2000 年又做对了。今天市场在跌，你们手上又握着大量现金。你们是怎么把大的市场转折踩得这么准的？

## 回答要点

1. 答案是「没有择时」：巴菲特说自己从不知道下周一开盘会怎么走，也从不因为「市场会怎样」或「经济会怎样」作买卖决定；芒格在旁边只说了一个「No」。
2. 2008 年那 150–160 亿美元（Wrigley、高盛等）是在「糟糕的时点」花出去的，事后看买早了；2020 年 3 月的低点也完全错过。
3. 真正做对的是「付出的钱够不够值」：买完还盼着它跌好继续买；公司回购还能抬高我们的权益——「这是四年级就能学会的东西」。
4. 关键决定只有一个，发生在 1942 年 3 月，他 11 岁、道指 90 多点时：持有美国企业是件好事。如今道指三万四千点。
5. 出问题的是机构化：校产基金、养老金要「平衡配置」，每三个月听一批管理人汇报；「拿飞镖让猴子扔，扣掉管理费，我押猴子赢」。
6. 为什么没人这么说？说破了，这些说话的人 90% 以上的收入就没了。

**Warren Buffett**（01:36:04）：好，第二提问点。

**Rajid Erduwal**（01:36:08）：沃伦、查理，你们好。见到你们二位和这些出色的伯克希尔经理人真是太好了，谢谢你们所做的一切。我叫 Rajid Erduwal，来自新泽西。我的问题是关于择时。你们一直说市场是无法择时的，可看你们的记录，几次关键决策的时点都惊人地准：1969、1970 年退出股市，1972、1974 年市场很便宜时回来；1987、1999、2000 年又做了同样的事。今天我们握着一大笔现金，而市场在下跌。我的问题是：你们怎么把大的市场转折择得这么准？

**Warren Buffett**（01:37:01）：我们倒想先给你一份工作。（笑声）

**Rajid Erduwal**（01:37:09）：我接受。（笑声）

**Warren Buffett**（01:37:13）：有意思的是，我们显然完全不知道下周一开盘股市会怎么走，从来就不知道。查理和我从没——我想在我们共事的全部时间里——我待会儿可能会讲讲学习是怎么发生的——但我们从来没有做过一个决定，是两个人中谁说、或者想「我们应该因为市场要往哪走而买或卖」。

**Charlie Munger**（01:37:46）：没有。

**Warren Buffett**（01:37:47）：或者因为经济要往哪走。我们不知道。有意思的是，有时候我还因为 2008 年大家都在抛股票时我们保持乐观而受表扬。你知道吗，我们把净值的很大一部分花在了一个非常蠢的时点上。（笑）我不该说「我们」，是我。我们花了大约 150 亿、160 亿美元，那在当时对我们比现在重要得多，在三四週里花出去，Wrigley、高盛等等，事后看那个时点糟透了。我当时并不知道那是好时点还是坏时点，但事后看确实很蠢。我还给《纽约时报》写了篇《买美国》的文章。要是我有一点择时的感觉，等上六个月，低点大概在三月，事实上那天我可能就在 CNBC 上。可我完全错过了那个机会，2020 年 3 月也完全错过。我们不擅长择时。我们比较擅长判断「付出的钱够不够值」。

我们买任何东西时都不知道会怎样，我们总是希望它先跌一阵，好让我们多买点；甚至买完、钱花光了，我们还希望如果它便宜，公司会继续回购，从而抬高我们的权益。这些都是四年级就能学会的东西，但学校里不教。所以永远别把功劳记在我们头上。其实，把功劳全记在我们头上吧，出去跟所有人说我们多聪明，但我们并不聪明。（笑声）我们从来没有择过时，我们也从来没有对经济有什么洞见。我 11 岁那年，1942 年 3 月 11 日或 12 日，道指 90 多点时买了股票，早上是 101，收盘大概是 99，现在道指三万四千点，也许比周四少一千点。（笑声）那一个决定才是关键：持有美国企业是件好事。你要是让哈佛校产基金在我 11 岁时来找我（笑），或者通用汽车的养老金，他们说不行，我们得做平衡配置，也许股票配 60%，然后每三个月坐下来听一堆管理人汇报，他们其实只要拿几支飞镖随手一扔，说「我们五十年后、一百年后还在美国，股票会比债券好」，结果就会更好。

这么简单的一个游戏，人们却硬是把它弄得那么难，真是惊人。当然，如果他们告诉所有人这游戏这么简单，这些说话的人 90% 以上的收入就没了。指望人性去解释「为什么我们其实没有为你增加任何你自己做不到的价值」，实在要求太高了。或者说，我不太想用这个例子，你可以让猴子往纸上扔飞镖，扣掉管理费等等，我押猴子赢。我并不觉得猴子是更高级的物种，我也不希望它们搬到我隔壁来住（笑）。但事情就是这样。查理，你有什么让人高兴的话要说吗？（笑声）

**Warren Buffett**（01:36:04）：OK, station two.

**Rajid Erduwal**（01:36:08）：Hello, Warren and Charlie. It is great to see you both and the wonderful Berkshire managers. Our thanks for everything that you do. My name is Rajid Erduwal (PH), and I am from New Jersey. My question is on market timing. You have always said that it is impossible to time the markets. Yet if we look at your track record, you have had amazing timings with some of your key decisions. You got out of the stock markets in 1969 and 1970. You got back in 1972, '74, when the markets were really cheap. You did the same thing in '87, '99, 2000. And today we are sitting on a significant amount of cash when the markets are going down. My question is how do you time the big market moves so well?

**Warren Buffett**（01:37:01）：We'd like to offer you a job first. (Laughter)

**Rajid Erduwal**（01:37:09）：I will take it. (Laughter)

**Warren Buffett**（01:37:13）：The interesting thing is, you know, obviously we haven't the faintest idea what the stock market is going to do when it opens on Monday. We never have had. We have never made — Charlie and I — I don't think in all the time we've worked together — and I'll tell you something later on maybe about how learning takes place — but we have never — I don't think we've ever made a decision where either one of us has either said or been thinking, we should buy or sell based on what the market's going to do.

**Charlie Munger**（01:37:46）：No.

**Warren Buffett**（01:37:47）：Or for that matter, on what the economy's going to do. We don't know. And the interesting thing is, sometimes I get some credit some place for the fact that, you know, how wonderful it was that we were optimistic in 2008 when everybody was down on stocks and all that sort of thing. You know, we spent a big percentage of our net worth at a very dumb time. (Laughs) I shouldn't say we, it's I. We spent about 15 or 16 billion dollars — which was a lot bigger to us then than it is now — we spent it in the last few weeks, over a period of three or four weeks — between Wrigley and Goldman Sachs and generally — at a terrible time, as it turned out. I mean, I didn't know whether it was going to be a good time or a bad time, but it was a really dumb time. And I wrote an article for The New York Times, "Buy American," and all these things. Well, if I'd had any sense of timing and waited six months until — I think the low was in March — and in fact I think I was on CNBC maybe that day, or something. But I totally missed that opportunity. I totally missed, you know, in March of 2020. We have not been good at timing. We've been reasonably good at figuring out when we were getting enough for our money. And we had no idea when we bought anything — well, we always hoped it would go down for a while so we could buy more, and we hoped even after we were done buying and ran out of money that if it was cheap the company would keep buying, in effect, taking our interest up. I mean, that's stuff you can learn it in fourth grade. But it's not what's taught in school. And so never give us any credit. Well, actually, give us all the credit. I mean, go out and tell everybody how smart we are, but we aren't. (Laughter) We haven't ever timed anything. We've never figured out insights into the economy. I mean, when I was 11 years old, March 12th, I guess, 1942 — or March 11th — you know, I bought stock when the Dow was 90 — well, it was 101 in the morning — It was 99 at the end of the day I think — and, you know, now it's 34,000, or maybe it's 1,000 less than it was on Thursday. (Laughter) But, you know, it's one decision that it's a good thing to own American business. And, you know, if the Harvard endowment had come to see me as an 11-year-old, (laughs) you know, or General Motors' pension fund or something and, you know, they say, well, no, but we have to have a balance. And we have to maybe have 60%. And then we have to sit around every three months and listen to a bunch of managers. They'd have just done better if they'd just taken some darts and thrown them and just said, we're going to be in America 50 years from now and 100 years from now, and we'll do better in stocks than we will in bonds. It's amazing how hard people make what a simple game it is. But of course, if they told everybody what a simple game it was then 90% of the income or more of the people that were speaking would disappear. So, it's really a little too much of us to expect of human nature that people will explain why they really aren't adding any value to what you can do by yourself. Or actually, you're, you know — I hate to use the example — but you can have monkeys throwing darts at the page. And, you know, take away the management fees and everything, I'll bet on the monkeys. But I don't consider them a superior species. And I don't want them to move next-door instead of my next-door neighbor or (laughs) anything, but it's just the way it has to be. Charlie, do you have anything cheerful to say? (Laughter)
