## 问题

（本片段没有现场提问。芒格先谈为什么要拿几百亿美元买自己的股票，随后谈到指数基金来信要求把董事长与 CEO 分开、并要求在年会前单独会见，巴菲特接着回应。）

## 回答要点

1. 回购的算术很简单：在价格合适时买回自家股票，等于不付管理费、不付中介费地提高在「已经喜欢的生意」里的权益，芒格称之为「再多一点我们已经拥有的东西」。
2. 美国运通是活例子：1998 年持股 11.2%，靠公司持续回购，如今是 20%，伯克希尔没多掏一分钱。
3. 苹果同理：一季度又加了一点仓，且清楚公司自己也在回购——「不需要任何内幕消息，这就是该下的注」。
4. 拆开董事长与 CEO 是「我听过最荒唐的批评」：芒格的比喻是奥德修斯打赢特洛伊回来，有人挑剔他握矛的姿势，而挑的人从没经营过任何生意。
5. 指数基金为什么热衷治理：巴菲特认为它们真正担心的，是「某家机构拥有全国每家大公司 10% 的投票权」这件事被注意到；他强调伯克希尔对三百万股东一视同仁，不会给它们开小会。

## 关键引述

> **“If you do it at the right price, there's nothing better than buying in your own business.”**
>
> 「只要价格合适，没有什么比买进自己的生意更好的事。」

> **“the most ridiculous criticism I've ever heard”**
>
> 「我听过的最荒唐的批评。」

> **“we've gone from 11.2% to 20%”**
>
> 「我们从 11.2% 变成了 20%。」

## 中文全文（逐段翻译）

> 与下方「英文原文」逐段对应，按发言顺序排列；术语与专名首次出现时保留英文。

**Charlie Munger**（01:10:26）：这个嘛，往大处想：想象一下买回我们自己 600 亿美元的股票。我们喜欢这些生意，我们喜欢付的价格，没有管理费、没有成本、什么都没有，只是在我们已经拥有的东西里占更多份额。这不算完全浪费时间。

**Warren Buffett**（01:10:51）：是啊，你要是去看，关于回购，你能读到几十万字、也许上百万字，讲这是什么、那是什么。其实没那么复杂。（笑）假设你有个柠檬水摊，一个合伙人的权益要卖，摊子有钱买，我们这个小柠檬水摊，而他开的价格对留下来的两个人是划算的，你就会买。让我觉得有意思的是，你能做成多少事，而人们根本不在意。1998 年，二十多年前，我们持有大约 1.5 亿股美国运通，我不知道后来拆没拆股，反正按拆股调整，我们大概在 1998 年买进最后一股，那时拥有美国运通 11.2% 的股份，一家很棒的公司。从那以后，他们每季度给我们寄一张分红支票，我们也陆续兑现了一点现金。现在我们拥有美国运通 20%。这就是他们回购股票的结果。这笔恰好回报极好。

如果他们买贵了，这解决不了所有问题，但当你拥有一个喜欢的资产，而它把你持有的比例抬上去，那是件美妙的事。就像我说的，我们从 11.2% 到了 20%。你刷美国运通卡的时候，不管你刷什么，那 20% 的盈利里有一小部分归我们，而我们一分钱没多掏。再想象一下：你有一块 640 英亩的农场，每年耕种，赚一点钱，你也喜欢种地。二十来年后，它变成了 1100 或 1200 英亩。你会说，这是怎么发生的？这有什么问题吗？这不美国吗？这是不是对资本成本的合理使用？诸如此类。只要价格合适，没有什么比买进自己的生意更好的事。我提过苹果的例子：一家年赚一千亿美元的公司，它每回购一次，我们在里面的权益就涨 0.1%，等于多了一亿美元的盈利。

而要自己挣出一亿美元，那得干多少活（笑）。他们刚公布的第一季度，他们财年不同，刚公布的 3 月季，赚得更多、流通股更少。我们一季度其实又买了一点苹果，我们想让自己的权益更大一些；而且我们知道，只要他们继续回购，我们的权益还会更大，我们没有内幕消息，但这显然是该下的注。我们在市场上买的那些股票让我们感觉很好，他们用现金买掉别人的股份，我们也同样高兴。这是世界上最简单的事，可我却读到那么多说法。人们居然想不明白，如果你有一块农场，旁边那家伙也有一块，而你不断拿到他那块的一部分，还不用掏钱，只用自己的收成去付，你至少会觉得很不错。查理，你能解释这是为什么吗？

**Charlie Munger**（01:15:53）：嗯，眼下的场景里还有一件事让我在意。我们收到指数基金的各种建议，信里说我们的董事长和首席执行官是同一个人，他们从哪儿找了个教授，认为美国企业如果分设会运转得更好——如果能把沃伦一分为二，让两半各自工作。在我看来这是我听过的最荒唐的批评。那就像——就像奥德修斯打赢了特洛伊之战回来，有人跟他说：我不喜欢你打那仗时握矛的姿势。（笑声）而说这话的人从没经营过任何生意，什么也不懂。我对这套活动评价不高。（掌声）

**Warren Buffett**（01:17:10）：这个嘛，让我找找，啊，在这儿。我们在年报里写过，一份九页报告的第三段，我们说：「我们会一视同仁地对待所有人。」这也许是个疯狂的想法，但我们真的觉得，1960 年、1970 年、1980 年把积蓄交给我们、然后一直信任我们的人，理应得到跟那种管理规模疯狂膨胀、按管理资产收费的人同样的尊重和关注，后者知道，他们只需要拿出在华盛顿受欢迎的政策就行。他们唯一的念头是：华盛顿有时会说你们太大了，我们要对你动手。所以他们要确保自己做的事能让人叫好。所以我说：「我们会一视同仁。」我们有三百万股东，我们会一视同仁。结果 3 月 25 日，我写完那封信一个月后，就在第三段，你会以为他们至少读到那么远，他们持有 1.01 亿股 B 股，总该有人读到第三段吧。

总之，我们收到一封信，说「我们希望在伯克希尔 2022 年年度股东大会之前与你们会面，讨论伯克希尔在治理与可持续性方面的看法」。关于这个话题，我写的诚实的文字大概比任何一位公司掌门人都多。可他们凭什么认为我们应该跟他们见面，而不是跟你们每一位单独见面？（笑）（掌声）我在一个非常、非常共和党的家庭长大，但现在我觉得自己像个煽动民粹的人。我就是无法想象，算了，你们已经听到了。（笑声）有人拿钱干这个，公关行业里肯定有很多人，他们雇顾问，因为如果有顾问告诉他们董事长和 CEO 该不该是同一个人，看起来更体面，而这些人是收费的。然后他们在董事会上讨论。说到底，相信我：如果国会 90% 的议员出于某种原因认为董事长和 CEO 该是同一个人，这些指数基金（笑）就不会写那种信了，因为它们唯一要担心的是：为什么某家机构会拥有全国每家主要公司 10% 的投票权。

我喜欢指数基金这个想法。但看着激励机制和官僚体系把人带到哪里去，是件有意思的事。给我写信的那位大概是个很好的人，那是他的工作。总之，他们没得到单独会面。而你们来了这儿，我们非常感谢你们到场。（掌声）

## 英文原文（CNBC 官方逐字稿）

**Charlie Munger**（01:10:26）：Well, but think in the scheme of things, imagine buying in $60 billion worth of our own stock. We like the businesses. We like the price we're paying. No overhead, no cost, no nothing, just more interest in what we already owned. It isn't that we're totally wasting our time.

**Warren Buffett**（01:10:51）：Yeah, and if you look at it, there are — you can read hundreds of thousands — maybe millions of words — written on stock repurchases, and what this is and what that is and all this kind of thing. It's not very complicated. (Laughs) I mean, if you had a partner in a lemonade stand and they wanted to sell out — sell their interest — or two partners and one of them wanted to sell their interest — I mean — and the business had the money to buy it — our little lemonade stand — and they were offering at a price that was good for the other two people who are going to remain, you'd buy it. Now, the thing that's fascinating to me is what you can accomplish, and still — people don't pay any attention to it. We owned — in 1998, you know — this was more than 20 years ago — we owned about 150 million — I don't know whether they've split — whatever it is — if they've split, it's split-adjusted — but we owned 150 million shares of American Express. I think we bought our last share in 1998 or something like that. And we then owned 11.2% of the American Express company — wonderful company. And since then, they've sent us a check every quarter as a dividend. And so, we've taken some cash a little bit as they've gone along. And now we own 20% of American Express. Now, that's what's happened because they repurchased shares. That happens to have worked out extremely well. If they overpaid for the stock and all that — it doesn't solve every problem — but it's a wonderful thing if you've got an asset you like and they take your ownership interest up. And like I say, we've gone from 11.2% to 20%. And if you're using your American card — or whatever it may be — 20% of whatever earnings — contribute a little to our interest that used to be 11.2%, and we've done it without putting up any money. Now, imagine — imagine if you owned a farm, and you had 640 acres, and you farmed it every year, and you made a little money on it, and you enjoyed farming. And somehow, 20 or so years later, it had turned into 11-hundred or 12-hunred acres. I mean, you'd say, you know, how long has this been going on? You know, what could possibly be? — you know, is this un-American? — or whatever it may be. I mean, is it, you know, sensible use of the (unintelligible) cost of capital? Blah, blah, blah, blah, blah. If you do it at the right price, there's nothing better than buying in your own business. We owned — I mentioned and used Apple as an example of how our interest in Apple, you know — every time a company that earns a hundred billion a year — you know — it means that our interest in it goes up a tenth of a percent. You know, we've added another a hundred million to earnings. Well, I mean, it takes a lot of work (laughs) — a hundred million in earnings. And, you know, in the first quarter they just reported — they're on a fiscal year — but they just reported their March quarter — and, you know, they earned more money and they had fewer shares outstanding. And we actually bought a little more Apple, in the first quarter or so. We decided we wanted to own a greater interest. And on top of that, we knew that we would own an even greater interest if they kept buying in their shares, which — we didn't have any insider information or anything — but certainly, it would seem the way to bet. And, you know, we feel better because we bought the shares we bought in the market. And we feel (laughs) just as good as the fact — by the fact — they used their cash to buy out some of the other people. It is the simplest thing in the world, and then I read all this stuff. It is unbelievable how people can't figure out something that, you know, if they owned a farm and the guy next to them had a farm and somehow you were getting more of his farm all the time without putting up any money while you farmed your own farm — that at least, you know, you're using some of the earnings for that — you'd feel very good about it. Have you got any explanation for it, Charlie?

**Charlie Munger**（01:15:53）：Well, I have another thing that interests me in the present scene. We get all these suggestions from index funds, a letter saying we — the chairman and the chief executive officer are the same person, and that they have some professor somewhere that thinks that American business would work better if it had a separate — if Warren could split — we could split him in two and have each half work. And to me it's the most ridiculous criticism I've ever heard. It would be like — it would be like — Odysseus would come back from winning the battle in Troy and so forth, and some guy would say, I don't like the way you were holding your spear when you won that battle. (Laughter) And it's some guy that's never run any business and doesn't know anything. I don't think too much of this activity. (Applause)

**Warren Buffett**（01:17:10）：Well, let's see. Somewhere in here — I may find it at some point. Ah, here it is. We wrote in the annual report — in the third paragraph of a nine-page report — we said, "We're going to treat everybody the same." It may be a crazy concept we have, but we really feel that somebody that gave us their savings in 1960 or 1970 or 1980 and just left them with us, and trusted us, we feel that they're entitled to the same sort of respect and attention that somebody that, you know, is accumulating, like crazy, assets under management and gets paid based on assets under management, that knows that they just need to have policies that essentially are popular in Washington. The only thought they have is that Washington sometimes says that, you're getting too damn big and we're going to do something about you. So, they try to be very sure that they're doing things that people will cheer. So anyway, I say, "Well, we're going to treat you all alike." We've got three million people — or shareholders — out there. We're going to treat you all alike. And on March 25th, about a month after I wrote that letter — it's in the third paragraph, you'd think that they would get that far — that they had 101 million B shares — i mean, now, somebody ought to read to the third paragraph. But anyway, we get a letter that says, "We would like to meet with you in advance of Berkshire Hathaway's 2022 Annual Meeting of Shareholders to discuss Berkshire Hathaway's perspective on governance and sustainability." Well, I have written probably more on that that's been honestly written by the guy that runs the company. But why in hell would they think that we should meet with them and not you people all individually (laughs) that come here? (Applause) I grew up in a very, very, very Republican household, but I feel like, you know, some raving populist or something. But I just can't imagine — well, anyway. You've heard it. (Laughter) And, you know, somebody gets paid to — well, there's a lot of people I'm sure in public relations and they hire advisors, because it looks better if they have advisors that tell them whether the chairman and the CEO should be the same person or not, and those people get paid for it. And then they discuss it at their board meeting and then, you know — In the end, believe me, if 90% of Congress for some reason felt it was better to have the chairman and the CEO be the same person, the index funds (laughs) would not be writing those letters, because all they have to worry about is whether for some reason people start wondering why some institution should have 10% of the votes in every major corporation in the country. And I like the idea of index funds. But it is interesting to watch where incentives and bureaucracy, and whatever it may be, lead people. The guy that wrote me the letter's probably a very nice guy. That's his job. Well, anyway, they didn't get a special meeting. And you people are here, and we appreciate the fact you're here. (Applause)
