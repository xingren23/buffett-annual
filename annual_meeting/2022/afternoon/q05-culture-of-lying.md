## 问题

你说过，公司会得到它配得上的股东。今年信里你也提到，为长期持有的个人股东工作是你的一大满足。在指数基金影响力越来越大的情况下，管理层该怎么培养出伯克希尔这样的股东文化？

## 回答要点

1. 伯克希尔的答案很简单：股东结构是固定的（今天 147 万股 A 股，比一年前还少），「座位已经坐满了」，没有理由再招新人来替掉现有股东。
2. 教堂的比喻：座位有限、教区居民又很好，你不会再去招 50 个人，好把现有的 50 个人赶走。
3. 反过来说，几乎所有公司都在拉新股东——「这在我看来基本是疯了」；他会问那些每月开分析师会的公司：「你想赶走现有的哪一批？」
4. 「说谎的文化」从业绩指引开始：每月重复同一套教义（服务数要从 6.2 做到 7），一旦错了就没法改口；新任 CEO 接了班也不敢说出真相。
5. 一次说谎就停不下来：像从收银机里拿 5 美元——「第一次你会说我会放回去，然后就再也停不下来」。
6. 伯克希尔从不改数字：「一旦在顶部立错榜样，你就真有大麻烦」；而董事会重流程轻实质，「最终地基会崩塌」。

>

**Warren Buffett**（00:20:04）：好，第六提问点。

**Martin Wegand**（00:20:10）：我叫 Martin Wegand，住在田纳西州纳什维尔。巴菲特先生、芒格先生，谢谢你们毕生的教诲，也谢谢你们今年把我们请回奥马哈。（掌声）

**Warren Buffett**（00:20:30）：谢谢你。

**Martin Wegand**（00:20:32）：你提到过，公司会得到它配得上的股东。今年信里你也说，为长期持有的个人股东工作是你的一大满足。在指数基金这样的机构影响力越来越大的情况下，管理层该怎么培养出我们在伯克希尔拥有的这种股东文化？谢谢。

**Warren Buffett**（00:21:00）：这个嘛，幸运的是我们已经有这种文化，而我们更懂怎么保持它，而不是怎么建立它。这很有意思。今天我们发行在外的 A 股有 147 万股，比一年前还少。座位已经坐满了，你们就是在座位的股东。我们喜欢这批人。既然是固定数量的座位，我们凭什么还要去外面招人来替换你们？我们所能拥有的最理想的股东群体，就是今天这一批。如果我们有座教堂，我们会希望人们一周一周地回来。如果座位有限，而且已有一些很好的教区居民，我们就不会再去招五十、一百个人，然后不得不把现有的五十、一百个人赶出去。而我们认识的几乎每一家公司，都在招新人进来。他们到底有没有改善自己的股东群体？在我们看来这基本是疯了。我们不想要跟现在不同的人（笑声）。

我们不会为了赶走指数基金，就把你们这样的人赶走，而我们不想赶走你们这样的人。（笑）我就是不明白：假设你有一个街区，地块大小正好容得下十户邻居，而这十户都是极好的邻居，你为什么要跑到街上去跟一大堆人说「你为什么不买我旁边那家的房子」？（笑）这很怪。但有非常多的人靠干这个谋生，而且从不质疑。我倒想问任何一家每月开分析师会的公司：「你现在想赶走的是哪一批？」因为我不希望年底你的股份比现在更多。难道我该让位，好让另一个关心你下季度股价的基金来替代我？（笑）这是个非常、非常、非常怪的局面。而真正疯狂的是，这套流程发展成了跟分析师群体，可以说是金融的高级祭司，对话，有些公司一个月还不止一次。你想想，如果你在那家公司工作，每个月人们都在重复那些关于公司的话：「我们把每客户服务数做到 6.2 很重要，我们得做到 7」之类。

他们一个月一个月地重复，这就成了一段教义问答。CEO 或者他/她的代表说这些话，下个月你怎么能站出来说：「顺便说，我们之前真的错了，这才是我们该做的」？你不会说。而接任的新 CEO 面临一个可怕的问题：前任说最重要的事是达到盈利目标。而他多半是靠时不时作弊达到的。这个人把接力棒交给你，你能站出来说：「其实我们一直在稍微作弊，而做盈利预测对公司的成长真的很有害，我们以后只报实际结果，不编东西」？会计部门做不到，这不合人性；再说，你也当不上接班人。但你就是不能走进去说：「我们一直在维护这些神话，我们总能做到 8% 增长、我们能做这做那、最重要的是这个。」如果你每个月都在向人们宣讲这就是我们的立场，你就没法改。

然后把这话传给大众、传给分析师。这是一种彻底破坏性的政策。在 GAAP 之内，我可以在数字上玩很多把戏。我们在伯克希尔做过很多蠢事，但我们从来没有告诉任何人「数字必须是这个」，或者改动任何东西。一旦开始，就全完了，你停不下来。这就像从收银机里拿走 5 美元：第一次你会说「我会放回去」，干了几次就再也停不下来。其实干一次，你大概就再也停不下来。如果一件事有破坏性，该做的就是别开始。而预测盈利，我想不出比这更有破坏性的事。我有 36 万员工，他们中很多人知道我在不在撒谎，他们知道报上去的数字有没有被改。你这是在传递什么信息？伯克希尔内部就有过一个很戏剧化的例子。总之，一旦开始撒谎，你就有大麻烦，就是这么简单。

如果你开始跟团队说，你的工作就是股东关系，你的职责是每天出去告诉大家我们的股票是几千个选择里最值得买的，那是疯了。那你要告诉他们什么？他们只能看风往哪吹，琢磨该跟人说什么，然后出去说。而如果你是个人，你说过「我们今年每股赚 3.59 美元」，你就能做到 3.59，还能撑一阵。这些流程都有，但如果你有一种说谎的文化，流程就不管用了，它们会直接消失。查理和我大概在每一家我们进过的董事会里都见过。查理，你讲讲？

**Charlie Munger**（00:28:48）：我认为，我们走后，伯克希尔的文化还会持续很久。我认为它应该如此，也会运转得不错。美国其余的公司很不一样，而且我认为每过十年就更不一样，变得越来越怪。很快他们要把股东大会全放到线上，股东根本不会到场。这越来越怪。指数基金在投票上的分量越来越重。就像生活里其他一切，它在变，而且不总是往你喜欢的方向变。

**Warren Buffett**（00:29:54）：最终这会影响到选什么样的 CEO 等等。你不会任命一个上任就说「以前做的每件事都有点像欺诈」的接班人。如果我们季度末需要多记一笔销售、需要调整准备金——一旦开始撒谎，就全完了。我不知道有什么别的办法，只能尽一切努力不开始；如果你在顶层立了错误的榜样，你就真有大麻烦。我们从来没有让任何人改一个数字，将来也不会。如果他们改过数字，我们早就陷入各种麻烦，因为他们知道、我知道、下一个人也会知道，然后一路烂下去。这种事我们真的一次又一次看到。董事会的运作必须流程导向。我理解特拉华州在写法条时的难题，也理解法官面对这些问题时的处境，但组织对「流程」的重视能造成什么后果，实在惊人——因为它们会以为，只要被允许，什么都能做。

最终，地基会崩塌。

**Warren Buffett**（00:21:00）：Well, fortunately we have it, and we know more about how to keep it than to institute one. And it's very interesting. We have a 1,470,000 class A shares outstanding today. Fewer than we had a year ago. And those seats are filled. I mean you are the shareholders in place. We like the group we have. So why in the world, when we got a fixed number of seats, should we go out and recruit other people to replace you? You know? I mean the ideal shareholder group we can have is the group we have today. And, you know, if we had a church, we'd want the people to keep coming back week after week after week. If we had a limited number of seats, and we had some wonderful parishioners, we would not go out and recruit another 50 or 100 of them and have to throw out 50 or 100 of the ones we already had. We've got. And every company I know, virtually, you know, is wooing new people to come in. And whether they're improving the group they get or not, I mean it strikes us as basically crazy. We don't want anybody different (Laughter) than we have now. And, you know, we're not going to get rid of the index fund, so we have to get rid of people like you, and we don't want to get rid of people like you. (Laughs) And I just don't understand why if you had a neighborhood, and the size of the terrain or whatever it was would be such that you could have ten neighbors, and they were all great neighbors, why in the world would you go out and say to a whole bunch of people going up and down the street, you know, "Why don't you buy the house of the guy next to me?" You know, (Laughs) it is weird, but there's an awful lot of people that make their living by doing that, and they never really question. I would sort of ask any company that's making analyst presentations every month or something, "Which of the present ones are you trying to get rid of?" You know, basically, because I hope you're not going to have more shares outstanding at the end of the year than you have now. And am I supposed to, you know, get out of the way so (Laughs) some other fund that is thinking about what your stock is going to do next week replaces me? It is a very, very, very weird situation. And of course, the really crazy process that has developed is people talking to, we'll say, analyst group, you know, sort of the high priests of finance, you know, some companies are doing it more than once a month. Well, just imagine if you work for that company, you go to work for that company, and every month people are repeating these things about their company that, "It's important that we have more services per customer at 6.2 and we got to get to seven," or something like that. And they'd say that month after month after month, so it becomes a catechism. And CEO says it or his or her representative says it, and how do you go on the next month and say, "By the way, we were really wrong, and this is what we should be working on." You don't say that. And it's a terrible problem the new CEO has coming in after a previous CEO has said the important thing to do is to hit your earnings targets. Well, you know, he's been meeting them, in all probability, by cheating from some time to time. And this guy hands you the baton, and are you going to come out and say, "Well, we've really been cheating a little and it's really counterproductive to the development of the, you know, companies, not to make earnings projections and just to give you the results as they come, rather than making up a few things. And the accounting department, you know, they can't do it. It's not human nature, and besides, you wouldn't get appointed the successor. But you just don't go in and say, "We've been perpetuating these myths that we can always deliver 8% growth or we can do this or do that, or the most important thing is this." You can't go in and change that if every month you've been preaching to people that this is what we stand for, and just ask another question and carry this message out to the masses, to the analysts and all that. And it's a totally destructive policy. I mean, you know, I can, within gap accounting, I can play a lot of games with numbers. We've done a lot-ta dumb things at Berkshire. We have never told anybody that the number had to be this or that or to change anything. I mean once you start it, it's all over. You can't quit. It's like taking $5 out of the cash register. You know, the first time you take the five bucks out, you'll say, "Well, I'm going to put it back." And then do it a few times, and you'll never stop. In fact, do it once and you probably never stop. But if something is going to be destructive, the thing to do is not start it. And forecasting earnings, I can't imagine anything more destructive. I've got 360,000 people out there, and they know whether I'm lying or not. Many of them. And they know what they send in figures, and they get changed? You know, what message are you telling? We've got one dramatic illustration of that within Berkshire. And it's just, you know, if you start lying you've got a big problem. It's that simple. And if you start saying to your team that somehow you've got a job — you've got shareholder relations, your job is to go out and tell everybody that our stock is the best thing among thousands of choices to buy, every day. Well, that's crazy. So what do you tell them? Well, they try to, you know, see which way the wind is blowing and figure out what they have to tell people. And that they go out and tell them, and then if you're human, and you've said, "We're going to earn $3.59 a share," you can get to $3.59. And get there quite a while. And, you know, you can have all these processes, but if you have a culture of lying, the processes really don't — they just disappear. And Charlie and I have seen it, well, probably every time we've gone on a board. Charlie, tell them about it? (Laughs)

**Charlie Munger**（00:28:48）：Well, I think Berkshire's culture's going to last a long time after we're gone. And I think it should, and I think it'll prosper pretty well. The rest of corporate America is quite different, and it gets more different, I think, with each passing decade. And it's getting very peculiar. Pretty soon they're going to hold all the shareholders' meetings online, and the shareholders won't even come. And it's just it's getting very peculiar. And the index fund's getting more and more important than the voting. And it's like everything else in life. It changes, and not always in ways you like.

**Warren Buffett**（00:29:54）：And it ends up for selecting different CEOs and all kinds of things. I mean, you're not going to appoint a successor CEO that's going to come in and say everything that's been done before, you know, has been kind of fraudulent. You know? I mean if we needed to book an extra sale after the end of the quarter, if we needed to adjust the reserves, once you start lying, it's all over. And I just don't know any way around that, except to try every way you can to not — if you set the wrong example at the top, you've got a real problem. You know? And we've never told anybody to change a figure. And we never will. And if they had been changing figures, you know, we'd be in all kinds of trouble, because they know it and I'd know it and the next person would know it. And it just deteriorates. And we've really seen it time after time. The way boards operate, you know, it has to be process-oriented. I mean I understand the problems that Delaware has in writing a statute that judges face when they look at things, but it's just extraordinary what an emphasis on process can do to an organization, because they think they can do anything if it's allowed. And, you know, eventually the foundation crumbles.
