## 问题

马克·麦克尼古拉斯提问：伯克希尔自身有堡垒般的资产负债表，但部分运营公司在疫情中可能现金紧张。伯克希尔是否会考虑向运营公司输送现金，一是确保它们渡过疫情，二是让它们在竞争对手挣扎时增加市场份额？

## 回答要点

1. 巴菲特称已向少数公司注资，但不会无限期资助那些未来已根本改变的公司。
2. 他以航空业为例，即便大幅亏损也把资金撤出，不会为未来可能烧钱的公司提供资金。
3. 他回顾1965年从纺织业务起步、花了20年试图解决一个基本无解的问题，因此不会用股东的钱补贴任何公司。
4. 阿贝尔称管理层先关注员工安全，再跟进客户需求；多数企业随客户需求调整，很少需要资金，少数需要的已获注资。
5. 巴菲特强调伯克希尔几乎肯定能产生现金，绝不让大量资金明日到期，只持有国库券，从不依赖商业票据或银行信贷额度。
6. 他称要保持诺克斯堡式堡垒，不需要1300亿或1350亿美元，但需要大量随时可用的资金以应对1929年式极端情形。

## 关键引述

> **“So, we are not in the business of subsidizing any companies with shareholders' money.”**
>
> 「所以我们不会用股东的钱去补贴任何公司。」

> **“Our position will be to be — to stay a Fort Knox.”**
>

> **“we basically want to be in a position to get through anything”**
>

**Becky Quick**（00:16:35）：这有点接上下一个问题，来自伊利诺伊州芝加哥的 Mark McNicholas。他说伯克希尔自身有一张诺克斯堡级别的资产负债表，但旗下一些运营公司在疫情期间可能现金紧张。伯克希尔会不会考虑向这些运营公司输送现金，一是确保它们度过疫情，二是让它们在竞争对手挣扎时扩大市场份额？

**Warren Buffett**（00:17:27）：我们已经给几家汇了钱，也有能力这么做。但对于那些看起来前景已经——和一年前、甚至六个月前相比发生了剧烈变化的业务，我们不会无休止地汇钱。航空业这件事上我们就是这么决定的：我们从这门生意里把钱拿出来，哪怕亏得很厉害。对于我们认为未来会不断烧钱的公司，我们不会提供资金。

1965 年我们接手伯克希尔·哈撒韦时，就是从这样一家公司——纺织业务——起步的。我们折腾了 20 年，一直以为能解决一件没那么容易解决的事。所以我们不会做拿股东的钱去补贴任何公司的生意。如果人们想拿自己的钱这么做，随他们，但我们不会代表他们这么干。

不过我们已经预支过资金，也完全愿意预支。扩大市场份额等等，那可能会发生，但需要钱的公司，市场份额大概不是它们的头号问题。这么说吧。格雷格，你要……？

**Greg Abel**（00:18:19）：对。进入疫情时我们审视旗下不同公司、应对新冠危机，有意思的是：管理团队的第一关注点——也是对的——是员工，说到底是确保他们在我们所在的经营环境里安全、能继续运转。接着我们很快转向看客户处在周期的哪个位置，也就是业务底层需求是什么。值得称赞的是，我们的经理人大幅调整了业务，使之与客户底层的需求相一致。实际上他们跟着客户走，所以我们真正需要资金的业务很少。有一些需要，正如沃伦说的，我们已经把钱预付给了它们。但这些公司确实以与市场、也就是对其产品需求相符的方式做出了反应。

**Warren Buffett**（00:19:30）：是的。伯克希尔几乎肯定能产生现金，当然没有什么是百分之百确定的。正如格雷格提到的，伯克希尔·哈撒韦能源做了一些短期融资，而我们在伯克希尔母公司这边基本没有任何短期融资。我们永远不会让自己陷入有大量资金明天就要还的地步。那些靠商业票据大量融资、然后生意突然停摆的人，看看航空公司就知道了，它们需要钱，邮轮公司也需要钱。有些生意就是这样。

伯克希尔永远不会陷入那种靠钱才能运转的处境。而且正如我说，我们会把一些并非极不可能的情形考虑进去。我不打算把这些剧本讲出来，因为在某种程度上，你越展开说，越可能增加它们发生的概率。所以我们不太想多谈这个。我们的立场是保持诺克斯堡那样的堡垒。但我们并不需要，现在比季度末略高一点，我们不需要 1300 亿或 1350 亿，但我们需要大量随时可用的钱。这意味着我们只持有短期国库券。我们不买商业票据，也不依赖银行授信；少数几家子公司有授信，但基本上我们希望自己有能力穿过任何事情。

我们希望那不会发生，但你不能排除这种可能，就像 1929 年你不能排除另一种可能：你要一直等到 1955 年、或 1954 年底才回本。什么都可能发生，我们想为任何事做好准备，但我们同时想做成大事。如果价格有吸引力，正如格雷格说的，就在美联储出手之前，我们开始接到电话。那些电话没有吸引力，但电话确实在响。等美联储行动之后，其中几家公司已经能在公开市场上拿钱了，坦白说，那些条款我们根本不会给它们。

## 英文原文（CNBC 官方逐字稿）

**Becky Quick**（00:16:35）：That gets kind of to another question that came in from Mark McNicholas in Chicago, Illinois. He says Berkshire itself has a Fort Knox-like balance sheet, but some of its operating companies may be tight on cash during the pandemic. Would Berkshire consider sending cash to its operating companies to, one, ensure that they can get through the pandemic and, two, allow them to increase market share while their competitors struggle?

**Warren Buffett**（00:17:27）：Well, we've sent money to a few and — we're in a position to do that. We're not going to send money indefinitely to anything where it looks like their future is not — it's just changed dramatically from what it was a year ago or so, or even six months ago. You know, we made that decision in terms of the airline business. We took money out of the business, basically, even at a substantial loss. And we will not fund a company that — where we think that it's going to chew up money in the future. We started out with a company like that in our textile business at Berkshire Hathaway in 1965. And we went for 20 years, trying to think we could solve something that wasn't that solvable. So, we are not in the business of subsidizing any companies with shareholders' money. If people want to do that with their own money — but we're not going to do it on their behalf. But we have advanced money — we are perfectly ready to advance money. Gaining market share and all that. That may happen but the companies that need money, probably, market share is not their number one problem. I'll put it that way. Greg, would you —?

**Greg Abel**（00:18:19）：Yeah, well, yeah. It's interesting when we look at our different companies as we went into the pandemic, where we're addressing the COVID-19 crisis. Obviously, the first focus by our management team — and appropriately — was our employees, and effectively making sure they're safe in that the business environment we're in that, that they could continue to operate. Then we quickly moved to looking at where our customers were in the cycle, i.e., what was the underlying demand within the business. And to great credit to our managers, they very much have adjusted their businesses consistent with the underlying needs and demands of customers. So effectively, they're moving with the customer, meaning very few of our businesses have actually required funds. Some have, and as Warren said, we've advanced the funds to them. But the businesses have really reacted in a way where they're managing consistent with the — with where the market's at, i.e., their — the demand for their products.

**Warren Buffett**（00:19:30）：Yeah. Berkshire is almost certain to generate cash. I mean nothing's 100 percent certain but — and we're, as Greg mentioned, at Berkshire Hathaway Energy we had some short-term financing. We don't have short-term financing to any degree. We'll never get ourselves in a position where we have a lot of money that can come due tomorrow. And people that were financing heavily with commercial paper and then found their business stopped — well, you've seen what's happened to the airlines. I mean, they need money. Cruise lines need money. There's some businesses that, you know, it's just the nature of what they're in. Berkshire will never get in a position where it makes money. And we factor in — like I say — we factor in some things that are not ridiculously unlikely. And I'm not going to spell out scenarios because I, to some extent, you start — spreading — spelling out scenarios, you may increase the chance of them happening. So, it's not something that we really want to talk about a lot, but — Our position will be to be — to stay a Fort Knox. But we don't need — we don't need a hundred and — it's a little higher now than it was at quarter end — we don't need 130 or 35 billion, but we need a lot of money that's always available. And that means we own nothing but Treasury bills. I mean, we do not — we've never owned — we never buy commercial paper — we don't buy — we don't count on bank lines. One or two of our subsidiaries — a few of our subsidiaries have them but the — we've — we basically want to be in a position to get through anything. And we hope that doesn't happen, but you can't rule out the possibility any more than in 1929 you could rule out the possibility that, you know, you would be waiting until 1955 — or the end of 1954 — to get even. Anything can happen, and we want to be prepared for anything, but we also want to do big things. If the prices are attractive — as Greg said — there was a period right before the Fed acted — we were starting to get calls. They weren't attractive calls. But we were getting calls. And the companies we were getting calls from — after the Fed acted — a number of them were able to get money in the public market, frankly, at terms we wouldn't have given it to them.
