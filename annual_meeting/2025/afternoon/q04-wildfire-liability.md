## 问题

请谈谈你们的策略：如何保护公司免受西部电力子公司被归责的野火赔偿？

## 回答要点

1. 巴菲特先认错：2005 年买 PacifiCorp 时，他和 Walter Scott、David Sokol「三个骨子里是资本家的人，用自己的钱做决策」，却没有把公司按七个州拆开收购，而是整体承接了原有结构，**「那是个大错误」**。
2. Abel 讲操作层面：野火风险不会消失、而且大概率逐年上升，能做的不是消除而是**降低暴露**——设备加固、植被管理、断电预案（PSPS）等。
3. Becky 追问「断电导致医院停摆、有人死亡怎么办」，Abel 承认这是常态化的权衡，关键基础设施会逐案评估，并与客户群体保持反馈机制。
4. 巴菲特的边界最清楚：**「有些问题无解，我们不该拿股东的钱去解我们不知道答案的问题」**；一旦涉及州或联邦层面的政策选择，那是政治决策，「如果你手里没有笔（没有决定权），该做的事就是退出」。
5. Abel 补充底线：公用事业**不能充当「最后保险人」**，为所有野火损失无条件兜底——这正是 2020 年俄勒冈野火诉讼的核心争点。

## 关键引述

> **“There are some problems that can't be solved, and we shouldn't be in the business of taking your money, investors' money and tackling things that we don't know the solution for.”**
>
> 「有些问题是无解的，我们不该拿你们的钱、股东的钱，去处理我们不知道答案的问题。」

> **“If you're in something where you're going to lose, the big thing to do is quit.”**
>
> 「如果你身处一件注定要输的事情里，最重要的动作就是退出。」

> **“We can't just become the insurer of last resort.”**
>
> 「（Abel）我们不能就这样变成最后保险人。」

## 中文全文（逐段翻译）

> 与下方「英文原文」逐段对应，按发言顺序排列；术语与专名首次出现时保留英文。

**Becky Quick**（00:26:54）：这个问题来自犹他州 Layton 的 Matthew Tisak。他说：「请谈谈你们的策略：如何保护公司免受未来因野火而被归责于我们西部电力子公司的赔偿责任？」

**Warren Buffett**（00:27:09）：这是个很好的问题。我们过去犯过一些错误，就在 2005 年买 PacifiCorp 的时候。Walter Scott、David Sokol 和我，我们三个骨子里都是资本家，用的是自己的钱做决策。但我们犯了个错误：没有把它按我们买入的那七个州拆开来。它是作为一个聚合体卖过来的，不是按州分，而我们保留了原有的结构，这是个大错。这个国家的每一部分都需要电，而总会有些地方，私营电力公司在那儿经营是非常不明智的，这要怎么解决，在一个民主国家里，我们拭目以待。但就目前而言，事实就是这样。Greg？

**Greg Abel**（00:28:28）：关于野火的风险——也就是野火会不会发生——它们不会消失，我们清楚这一点，而且风险大概率逐年上升。那么我们能做什么，来降低它对我们的电网、我们的底层资产、以及不幸地由此产生的赔偿责任的影响？这是我们可以改变、可以管理的。我们无法消除风险，但可以降低它。我们在西部的团队就在做这件事，而我们实际上是在全部能源基础设施上推进，因为现实是野火已经烧到德州，全美各地都出现过各类野火，我们所有人都非常关注如何管理这个风险。我们的做法是先处理资产本身——怎么维护、往哪里投资。我们尽量确保它们要么不会引发火灾，要么能强化系统以承受灾害。我们从运营层面入手。然后更进一步——这一点 Warren 和我讨论过很多次——公用事业开始意识到，当我们遇到这些异常天气事件时（Warren 提到过内布拉斯加的暴风雨，但西部同样在发生或出现重大事件），我们已经非常擅长说：好，我们必须用不同方式管理系统，我们可能会主动断电，因为很可能要有事件发生。但有一件事我们此前没有处理，而这与 2020 年 PacifiCorp 那次重大事件高度相关：我们没能在火势逼近时给系统断电。因为我们的员工和整个管理团队一辈子都在「把灯点亮」，他们最不愿意做的就是关灯、让系统断电。那些事件之后，在我们认真审视今后如何管理资产、降低风险时，团队清晰地认识到：我们必须给这些资产断电。所以现在，当火灾逼近到一定英里数时，我们就断电，因为我们不想助长火势，显然也不想伤害任何用户，或者不幸地造成死亡。这确实是我们必须带着团队走的一步：我们管理的是另一类风险了，不再是「把灯点亮」，而是保护公众、确保火势不再蔓延。我们做到了这一步——这一点如果说错了请纠正我——但今天我们大概是唯一一家、或者说我们旗下的公用事业里唯一这么做的，我们坚信这是对的。Becky？

**Becky Quick**（00:31:46）：我可以追问一句吗？如果你关停系统，一家医院停了电、有人因此死亡，这不就让你暴露在另外的风险里了吗？

**Greg Abel**（00:31:55）：你知道，幸运的是这是我们经常要处理的事，因为我们的停电也会意外发生。所以，就关键基础设施而言——这是个很好的点——我们一直在重新评估，也从客户群体那里收到大量反馈。但这是我们比任何人希望的都更日常地在处理的事：灯灭了，我们必须确保医院不断电、应急部门能响应，等等。但那里确实有风险。所以我们会花大量时间去教育用户、我们的客户群体：好，会发生这样的事，我们需要了解你的特殊情况，以及我们怎样才能最好地应对。所以我们不会因此又背上另一项责任。围绕断电有很多工作。然后，说到最后一步，Warren 也提到了，在能源政策层面，我们必须和各州、和监管机构合作，确保这从来不是我们投资公用事业时承担或预见的风险，也不是任何投资其他能源公司的投资者所预见的。你本来是在承担与该资产相关的、非常确定的既定风险，换取非常确定的既定回报，而现在已经远远超出了那个范围。我们赚的不是那种回报，你也不可能赚到足够大的回报去承担这个风险。所以这不只是解决回报端的问题，我们真的必须解决风险端，这意味着要和监管机构合作、和州议员合作，找到正确的答案，而这会是一个持续的过程。这里没有银弹，但我们公用事业各团队每天都在努力降低这个风险，同时清楚野火的根本风险不会消失。

**Warren Buffett**（00:33:58）：有些问题是无解的，我们不该拿你们的钱、股东的钱，去处理我们不知道答案的问题。你可以把道理讲清楚，但在和各州或联邦政府打交道时，那是政治决策。如果你身处一件注定要输的事情里，最重要的动作就是退出。你可以尽可能好地陈述你的理由，但如果你最终手里没有那支笔（没有决定权），你知道，我们就没有任何理由拿你们的钱去做蠢事。我们可以尽力说明什么是明智的做法，但那是你们的钱。所以这一点很难讲。我对那些由政治决定、很多还要上法庭的事，有一堆疑问，你知道那不讲道理。我们清楚我们认为一个合理的制度该是什么样，我们应该把它讲出来，尽力争取我们的立场，因为那是对社会有利的。但正确的方案，比如州际公路，并不是让 48 个州各自决定怎么做、各自把工程包出去。你知道，有些问题无解，而我们不做「试图解决无解问题」的生意。但我们面对的麻烦当然是：为你工作的人，那就是他们的工作。所以他们想有继续做下去的理由，如果你在做管理，这些都是艰难的选择——但这就是为什么需要有管理者。

**Greg Abel**（00:36:29）：嘿，Warren。我能补充一点吗，因为你刚讲到了很重要的一点。实际上是，比如公用事业和野火：我们不能就这样变成「最后保险人」，不能不论发生了什么，都去覆盖任何成本、所有成本。我们现在的处境多少就是这样——我们最大的挑战，2020 年那场野火，当时有四场火在很棘手的时点发生。其中一场，我们一直主张是落雷引起的，而且不在我们的服务区内。火从外面烧进我们的服务区，而我们通过法院，实际上要为那场火负责，我们一直坚持我们不该负责。理应如此——我们没有助燃、没有引发，我们也不认为我们曾对它有贡献。再说，看看那里的风险，我们必须这样挺过去。我们会走完那场诉讼。关于那一场，我们很高兴地报告：五年之后，俄勒冈州林业部门已经出来说，我们确实有、并且成功控制和扑灭的那些火，对那场火没有贡献。而那第四场火——四场中最大的一场，占了 60% 的索赔——我们花了五年，才把这些信息真正送进法庭。这显然会决定我们今后的法律策略，但这些都是我们在处理的事，作为一家公用事业公司、作为行业一员，我们一直在从中学习。所以正如我说的，我们深度参与各州立法，确保责任归属有清晰定义、经济损失如何界定，最重要的是非经济损失如何界定。前提是：我们不能是最后保险人。我们不能够对一个州里发生的一切负责。

**Warren Buffett**（00:38:43）：如果我们想用自己的钱去做，我们可以做，但我们不会用你们的钱去做我们认为是蠢的事。如果我们做了，你们就该把我们赶走。用别人的钱做蠢事，比用自己的钱容易。这大体上是政府面临的问题之一，我们不想把它带到私营企业里来。但美国有一套明智的能源政策很重要，就像二战时我们学会极快地造船而不是造车很重要一样。我们找到了答案，我们把私营企业和政府的力量结合起来。但这在民主国家里有多可行？二战时该做什么是显而易见的，我们做到了。但当 3.3 亿人都在为各自的利益争论、并且往往由那些并不真正承担决策责任的人来做决定、他们的反应还停留在 20 年前时，事情就没那么清楚了。总之，这就是管理，我们会尽力而为。11 号站。

## 英文原文（实录摘录）

**Becky Quick**（00:26:54）：This question comes from Matthew Tisak in Layton, UT. He said, “Please discuss your strategy on how to protect our company from future liabilities due to wildfires blamed on our electric utility companies out West.”

**Warren Buffett**（00:27:09）：Well, that’s a very good question. We’ve made some mistakes in the past when we bought PacifiCorp in 2005. Walter Scott, David Sokol, and myself, three guys who were capitalists at heart, and we’re dealing with our own money. But we made a mistake by not carving it up into the seven states that we were buying. It came with an aggregation where it wasn’t state by state, and we kept the same structure, and that was a big mistake. Well, every part of the country is going to need electricity, and there are going to be places where privately held electric utilities would be very foolish to operate and how that gets resolved. In a democracy, we will find out. But those are the facts as they stand now, I would say. Greg?

**Greg Abel**（00:28:28）：The risk around the wildfires, i.e. do the wildfires occur, they’re not going away, and we know that, and the risk probably goes up each year. So what can we do to reduce the risk of it impacting our system and our underlying assets, and unfortunately, the liabilities with such events? We can change that and manage that. We can’t eliminate the risk, but we can reduce that. And that’s where we’ve got our teams in the West, but we really are approaching it across all our energy infrastructure, because the reality is the wildfires have now occurred in Texas. They’ve had a variety of them throughout the US, and we’re all very focused on how we manage that risk. How we manage it is we start by addressing the actual assets, how we’re maintaining them and where we invest them, or invest in them. So we try to make sure that they’re either not causing the fire or potentially even hardening the system as to what they can withstand. We start with the operational focus. We then take it even further, and this is something Warren and I’ve discussed many times, that the utilities started to recognise when we have these unusual weather events. And Warren touched on what’s been happening in Nebraska with storms, but they’re equally occurring or significant events occurring out West. But when we have those, we’ve gotten very, very good at saying, OK, we have to manage the system differently. We’ll potentially deenergize because there’s likely to be an event. But the one thing we hadn’t tackled, and this is very relevant to the one significant event we had back in 2020 in PacifiCorp, is that we didn’t deenergize the system as the fire was approaching. Because our employees and the whole management team have been all their whole lives to keep the lights on and the last thing they want to do is turn those lights off and have a system deenergized. After those events and as we really looked at how we’re going to move forward in managing the assets and reducing that risk, we have clearly recognized as a team that we have to deenergize those assets. So now, as we get fires encroaching at a certain number of miles, we deenergize because we do not want to contribute to the fire, nor obviously harm any of our consumers or contribute, unfortunately, if there’s a death. And that’s really where we had to take our team that we’re managing at different risks now. It’s not about keeping the lights on, it’s about protecting the general public and ensuring the fire does not spread further. So we’ve gone as far as that, and I’ll stand corrected on this one, but we’re probably the one utility or across our utilities that does that today, and we strongly believe that. Becky?

**Becky Quick**（00:31:46）：Can I just follow up on that? Doesn’t that open you up to other risks if you shut down your system, a hospital gets shut down, somebody dies?

**Greg Abel**（00:31:55）：You know, fortunately, that’s something that we do deal with a lot because we have power outages that occur by accident. So when we look at critical infrastructure because it’s an excellent point, and we’re constantly reevaluating it, and we do receive a lot of feedback from our customer groups as to how we manage that. But that is something we deal with on a more routine basis than we’d ever like. The lights go out, and we have to make sure the hospitals stay on, emergency units can respond and all that. But there is a risk there. So then we spend a lot more time educating the consumers and our consumer groups, our customers. OK, this is what will happen. We need to understand your unusual situations and how we can best tackle them, again. So we just don’t take on another liability. So there’s a lot around deenergization. Then, just to take it to the last step, and Warren touched on that, in general on energy policy, we have to work with our States and our regulators to ensure this was never a risk we took on or envisioned when we were investing in utilities, nor would any of the investors who’ve invested in other energy companies. You were in a very set return for taking on a very set defined risk associated with that asset, and this has gone well beyond that. We don’t earn the type of returns, nor can you earn a large enough return to take on this risk. So it’s not just about solving the return side. We really have to solve the risk side, which means we work with our regulators, we’re working with our state legislators to get to the right answer, and that will be an ongoing process. There are no silver bullets out there, but every day our teams across utilities are working hard to reduce that risk, recognizing the fundamental risk of the wildfires is not going away.

**Warren Buffett**（00:33:58）：There are some problems that can’t be solved, and we shouldn’t be in the business of taking your money, investors’ money and tackling things that we don’t know the solution for. You can present the arguments, but it’s a political decision when you are dealing with states or the federal government. If you’re in something where you’re going to lose, the big thing to do is quit. If you do present your case as well as you can, and everything else, but if you don’t hold the pen in the end, you know we don’t have any business taking your money and doing dumb things with us. We can do our best to explain what the intelligent things are, but it’s your money. So it’s very hard to tell on that. I have all the questions about politically determined decisions that are going to go to court in many cases, but you know that it doesn’t make sense. We know what we think a sensible system would be, and we ought to explain what we think it is and do our best to get our position because it’s pro-social. They have the right solution to have it. But the right solution, for example, in the Interstate, wasn’t to let 48 states each decide their own way of doing it, and award contractors the jobs. You know, there are some problems that can’t be solved, and we are not in the business of trying to solve insolvable problems. But then the problem we have, of course, is that the people who work for you, that’s their job. So they want to have reasons to keep going, and those are tough choices if you’re managing, but that’s why they have managers.

**Greg Abel**（00:36:29）：Hey, Warren. Can I just add, because you touched on something really important there, that effectively is, for example, with the utilities and the wildfires. we can’t just become the insurer of last resort. And that we’re going to cover any costs and all costs irrespective of what occurred. And that’s a little bit of the situation we’re in right now with our largest challenge, the 2020 wildfire, there were four fires occurring at a challenging time. One, we’ve always asserted was a lightning strike that was not inside our service territory. The fire burned into our service territory, and we became responsible for that fire, effectively, through the courts, and we’ve continued to hold firm that we’re not responsible for that. Rightfully, I mean, we didn’t contribute to it, and we didn’t initiate it, nor did we feel we ever contributed to it. Again, if you look at the risks that are there, we have to manage through things like that. We’ll get through that litigation. We’re happy to report, for example, on that one. After five years, the Oregon Forestry Department has come out and said the fires, the other fires we did have that we were able to manage and extinguish, did not contribute to that fire. And that fourth fires the largest fire of the four, it’s 60% of the claims. We’re five years into effectively getting that information into the courts. Now that will outline our legal strategy obviously going forward, but it’s things we’re dealing with, but we continue to learn from this as a utility in the industry. So we’re in very much each of the legislatures, as I said, making sure we get a clear definition where liability falls, what the economic damages be, but most importantly, what the non-economic damages be. And with the thought, we can’t be the insurer of last resort. We just can’t be responsible for everything that happens in a state.

**Warren Buffett**（00:38:43）：If we want to do it with our own money, we can do it, but we’re not going to do things with your money that we think are stupid. You ought to get rid of us if we do. It’s easier to do stupid things with other people’s money than it is with your own money. That’s one of the problems the government has, generally. We don’t want to bring it to private enterprise. But it is important that the United States have an intelligent energy policy, just as it was important during World War II that we learned how to make ships instead of cars extremely fast. We figured out the answer. We combined private enterprise with the power of government. But how feasible is that in a democracy? You know, it was clearly obvious during World War II what needed to be done, and we did it. But it’s not so clear when you have 330 million people all arguing their own self-interest, and of course deciding what will happen and having the people often who are making the decisions, reacting as they did 20 years earlier, when they don’t really bear the responsibility for the decision. Anyway, that’s management, and we’ll do our best. Station 11.
