## 问题

我在纽约做护士，自己买好医保都很困难。你和摩根大通、亚马逊的医疗合资（Haven）最终为什么结束？在医疗成本问题上，你现在怎么看？

## 回答要点

1. 先给数字：美国医疗支出**接近 GDP 的 20%**；1960 年代各国都在 5% 左右，之后曲线急剧分化——但「等式里只有 100 个百分点」这个算术事实没变。
2. Haven 的失败被归因于结构：三位发起人「都不认为自己知道答案，但都以为自己知道」，而医疗是一个**利益格局极其 entrenched（盘根错节）**的领域。
3. 他用「绦虫」（tapeworm）比喻医疗成本对美国企业的侵蚀——这个说法在本场次被 CNBC 选作标题之一。
4. 结论偏悲观：既得利益太深，靠一家公司或几家公司的联合体改不动。

## 关键引述

> **“We're spending close to 20% of GDP on health.”**
>
> 「我们在医疗上花的钱接近 GDP 的 20%。」

> **“We had three people who didn't think they knew the answer, but thought that...”**
>
> 「我们三个人都不认为自己知道答案，却都以为……」

**Warren Buffett**（01:42:05）：好，第 31 个。让我看看，Becky？

**Becky Quick**（01:42:09）：这个问题来自 Billy de Ross。他写道：「Buffett 先生，作为纽约州的一名护士，我多年来一直在为自己争取一份好的医疗保险而挣扎，即便我本身就在一线抢救生命。在纽约，要拿到保险意味着要穿过一套令人困惑的州营体系，感觉它就是设计来让人不知所措的。我很好奇，最终是什么导致了你们与摩根大通、亚马逊的医疗合资项目结束？考虑到你对价值和长期思考的坚持，你会不会考虑再重新审视一次美国的医疗保险改革？」

**Warren Buffett**（01:42:46）：我们在医疗上的花费接近——确切数字很难算——接近 GDP 的 20%。如果你回到 1960 年，当时有不少国家各自花 5% 左右，然后曲线开始急剧分化。但「等式里只有 100 个百分点」这个数学事实没变。所以我们和摩根大通、亚马逊做了那个实验。我们三个人都不认为自己知道答案，但都以为……就我而言，我用的说法是「它是经济体内的一条绦虫」，这条绦虫在全国每个地方都活着。医院喜欢它——医院里有头有脸的人跟各方打交道；医生不喜欢这套体系。各种各样的力量。但最终，摩根大通、亚马逊和 Berkshire，我们不可能对改变那 20% 产生任何影响。那可是 20%，总共只有 100 个百分点；有的国家只花 6% 或 7%，而且或许还在利用我们的体系占便宜，这也是事实。那是一个极其庞大的经济占比。它太根深蒂固，很难做出什么改变。我们为此花了些钱，做了一些工作，也对自己公司的体系有了不少了解，也看到了现行体系在多少人身上扎根——无论是医疗服务提供方还是所有人。这些人并不邪恶，他们只是在做自己的事、努力救人性命。但我们发现，无论是在加拿大、法国、英国还是哪里——他们确实会看我们的成本——我们的成本就是高得多。某种程度上我们在补贴世界其他地方，人们会跑到美国来做那些真正罕见或困难的手术之类。但我们没有取得任何进展。到了某个点，政府在其中介入太深，而健康对每个人都太重要了。我对 Jamie 和 Jeff 说：这条绦虫赢了。当一个国家 GDP 的 20% 流向某个行业时，社会就会出现问题：改变这个行业的热情程度、这个行业拥有的政治力量——这不意味着他们邪恶，只是所有人在那个位置上最后都会变成这样。所以我们的结论是：我们不知道答案。我们三个人有钱去做，但我们不知道如何改变 3.3 亿人对他们的医生、对他们的医疗、对他们「应得什么」的感受。它不会自己改变，而政府是唯一能改变它的主体；而能改变它的政府和人士，必须拿到 435 人加 100 人的多数。我父亲一生只在 1948 年输过一次选举，他是个非常坚定的共和党人；1950 年他卷土重来，击败了 1948 年赢他的那个人。他把医生们都拉到自己这边——医生们日子过得很好，而且 100% 相信自己做的事，他们每天都在帮助别人。疫情期间，人们为拯救他人做出的牺牲简直不可思议。你想想，在那样一个地方工作：人们成批成批地被送进来、即将死去，你还要设法维持自己的士气、继续工作。所以你不能否认这件事的重要性。但我们的成本和世界上任何国家都如此不同。这是一个巨大的因素，而我们是个非常富裕的国家，所以能做别的国家做不到的事。通过我们选出的代表、通过各种各样的机制，随时间推移，我们发展出了一套对任何重大变革都极强抵抗力的体系，而它在每个社区里都很重要。所以，我希望我们能给你一个答案，但我进场时本来就偏悲观，出来时更悲观了一点。不过我很高兴我们做了这件事，过程中也看清了自己的一些短板。所以 Berkshire 实际上「值回票价」，但我们没能杀死那条绦虫。在这个国家，试图在政府层面改变事情是个有趣的命题，因为进入政府并留下来的人存在自我筛选。某种程度上，他们一路走来必须做出自己并不喜欢的决定，然后学会接受、或者合理化它们，但归根到底还是围绕着他们自己。这个国家运转得比世界上任何国家都好，所以你不能说它失败；但你也不能否认，有些问题极难找到解法。其中一个又回到我前面提到的财政问题：因为花钱容易，削减别人的福利难。而你一旦当选，你会对自己说：如果我留下来，我能做更多好事。于是，如果你真按良心投票，你在这里让一点、那里让一点、再那里让一点，最后你在镜子里都认不出自己了。我在政治家庭长大，看着人们怎么行事——他们像人一样行事，这是你必须预期的，而我也像人一样行事。所以我们还是以戏剧性的方式继续前进。住在这里，比 100 年前、200 年前好太多了，这是戏剧性的。所以你不能说这套制度是失败的，但你可以说，要在其中做出重大改变非常困难。好，5 号站。

**Warren Buffett**（01:42:05）：OK, 31. Let’s see. Becky?

**Becky Quick**（01:42:09）：This question comes from Billy de Ross. He writes, “Mr. Buffett, as a nurse from New York State, I’ve spent years struggling to secure good health insurance for myself, even while working on the front lines to save lives. In New York, accessing insurance means navigating a confusing state-run system that feels like it’s designed to overwhelm. I’m curious what ultimately led to the end of your healthcare venture with JP Morgan and Amazon, and given your commitment to value and long-term thinking, would you ever consider taking another look at health insurance reform in the US?”

**Warren Buffett**（01:42:46）：We’re spending close, it’s hard to get the precise figure, but close to 20% of GDP on health. And if you go back to 1960, there were a number of countries that were each spending around 5% and then the lines began to diverge dramatically. But the mathematical fact that there are only 100 percentage points in the equation didn’t change. So we tried that experiment with JP Morgan and Amazon. We had three people who didn’t think they knew the answer, but thought that… In my case, I used the term that it was a tapeworm in the economy, the tapeworm was alive in every part of the country. The hospitals liked it. The hospitals had prominent people who worked with people. Their doctor didn’t like the system. I mean, all kinds of things. But in the end, JP Morgan, Amazon and Berkshire, we’re not going to have any effect on changing that 20%. Now that 20%, there are only 100 percentage points available, and one of the countries spends 6 or 7%, and perhaps uses our system to their advantage, which is also very true. That is an enormous percentage of the economy. It was too entrenched to really do much in the way of change, and we spent some money on it. We did some work and we learned a good bit about our own systems, and we saw the degree to which the present system was ingrained in so many people, whether the healthcare providers or whether everybody. And these aren’t evil people. I mean, they’re just going about something and trying to save lives. But we found that whether it was in Canada or France or Britain, or wherever might be. And they do look at our cost, but they were just far higher and to some extent, we were subsidizing the rest of the world, and people would come to the United States to do the really unusual or challenging aspects of health, in terms of operations and that sort of thing. But we made no progress. There comes a point where the governments, that I mean, it’s so involved in the situation and health is so important to everybody. What I said to Jamie and Jeff, I said, well, the tape worm won. There are problems in society when you get 20% of your GDP going into a given industry. The degree of enthusiasm for changing that industry, the political power that the industry will have, and that doesn’t mean they’re evil, it’s everybody they just ends up there. So we came to a conclusion, we didn’t know the answer. Three of us had the money to do it. And we didn’t know how to change of 330 million people felt about their doctor, felt about their health care, what they felt entitled to. It won’t change by itself, and the government is the only one that can change it, and the only people and governments that can change it are getting the majority of 435 people and hundred people. My dad lost one election in his life in 1948, and he was a very strong Republican. In 1950, he went back and beat the guy who beat him in 1948. And he got the doctors behind him, and they’re very well, and they believe 100% in what they’re doing. They’re helping people every day. And during the pandemic, the sacrifices made by people to save other people are just incredible. Can you imagine working in something where they’re bringing in people that are, you know, going to die by the dozens and dozens and dozens, and you try to somehow keep your own morale up and keep working with them. So you can’t argue about the importance of it. But our costs are so different from any country in the world. It’s a huge element, and we’re a very rich country, so we can do things other countries can’t do. And through our elected representatives, and all varieties of things over time. We develop a system that is enormously resistant to any kind of major change, and it’s important in every community that it’s in. So, I wish we had an answer for you, but I was somewhat pessimistic going in, and I was a little more pessimistic going out, but I’m glad we did what we did, and we learned something about our own failings in the process. So Berkshire, in effect, got his money’s worth, but we didn’t kill the tapeworm. Trying to change things in government is an interesting proposition in the country because you get self-selection in terms of the people who go into government and continue in it. And to some extent, they have to make decisions that they don’t like as they go along, and they learn to accept them or rationalize them or whatever it may be, but it’s still about them. This country has worked out better than any country in the world, so you can’t argue as a failure, but you can’t argue that there are certain problems that are terribly tough to figure out ways to solve. And of course, one of them gets back to the fiscal problem, I mentioned before, because it’s easy to spend money, and it’s hard to cut people’s receipts and if you get elected, you’re going to say to yourself, well, I can do more good if I stay in. Then, if I really vote my conscience on this sort of thing, you give away a little bit here and a little bit there and a little bit there. And finally, you don’t recognize yourself in the mirror anymore. I grew up in a political family, and I watched how people behaved, and they behave like human beings, which is what you have to expect, and I behave like a human being. So we still managed to keep moving forward in a dramatic way. It’s so much better to live here than it was 100 years ago, or 200 years ago. It’s dramatic. So you can’t say the systems of failure, but you can say that it’s very difficult to make major changes in it. OK. Station 5.
