## 问题

伯克希尔是美国第二大公用事业供应商。路透社 2025 年调查称其燃煤机组是全美最脏的，而且目前没有明确的煤电退出、全面转向可再生能源的计划。我 17 岁，你怎么向我解释？

## 回答要点

1. Abel 以爱荷华州为例：伯克希尔能源（BHE）在当地的煤电占比已大幅下降，可再生能源比例很高，但**仍保留部分燃煤机组用于保障供电可靠性**。
2. 他的核心论点是「可靠性优先」：在储能与输电还没到位的阶段，过早上马退出会造成停电风险。
3. 他没有承诺具体退出时间表，只强调会持续投入可再生与电网改造。
4. 这段是全场少见的、被现场年轻人当面追问 ESG 记录的片段，回答偏技术、避开了「最脏机组」的定性。

## 关键引述

> **“The risk around the wildfires, they're not going away, and we know that, and the risk probably goes up each year.”**
>
> 「（Abel）野火风险不会消失，我们知道这一点，而且风险大概率逐年上升。」

> **“Some coal plants are still needed to keep electricity flowing.”**
>
> 「（CNBC 章节标题）仍需要部分燃煤机组来维持供电。」

**Kansas Lomire**（01:35:27）：您好，Buffett 先生、Abel 先生。我叫 Kansas Lohmeier，是 Elkhorn South 高中的高三学生，在奥马哈出生、长大。我的问题是问 Greg Abel 的。Berkshire Hathaway 是美国第二大公用事业供应商，路透社 2025 年的一项调查发现，它的燃煤机组是全美最脏的。目前没有明确的煤电退出、全面转向可再生能源的计划。我 17 岁，考虑到这些，对像我这样要承受 Berkshire 这类公司造成的气候变化后果的年轻人，你有什么要说的？

**Greg Abel**（01:36:24）：谢谢你的问题和你的意见，因为重要的是理解 Berkshire Hathaway Energy，也要理解它是怎么运营的。也许可以用爱荷华州作为起点，因为我想那是报告里提到的州之一。我要早点说的一点是，在收购能源公司时——回到我们收购 MidAmerican，1999 年收购、2000 年 Berkshire 买下 MidAmerican——我和我们团队非常清楚的一点是，我们在公用事业里做什么，实际上由两条驱动。第一，我们绝对必须满足联邦法律的要求；但最重要的是，我们必须认识到，我们在这些州里是在执行公共政策。这是个很有意思的对话，当年我回到爱荷华——报告也把那列为重大问题。那是 2000 年代初，爱荷华第一次出现作为公用事业将面临电力短缺的情况。我们没有足够的电，于是和我们当时的州长进行了一次重要讨论，真的坐下来问：「你希望我们 MidAmerican 往哪儿走？作为一个州，你想要什么样的资源？」当时我们主要是一个以煤为基础的州，我们当然也认识到这一点，而且我们个人把它视为一种风险，但我们需要和州里就今后如何管理这件事达成对话。有意思的是，2000 年代初那次对话中——同样是在州领导层的带领下——明确决定我们要「长期有电」，即不能让客户停电。我们讨论了资源类型，我记得有一次非常清晰的讨论：我们希望在多种能源之间保持平衡，当时就是煤和天然气。就在那时，我们决定在爱荷华建设美国最大的风电项目。所以我们着手建三种资源：一座燃煤电厂、一座燃气电厂，以及 MidAmerican 拥有的第一个风电项目。这非常符合州里的意愿，但我们也在那里打下了一些重要的基础，因为我们开始界定可再生能源、无碳资源的重要性，但它必须与州的意愿一致。自那以后，我们在爱荷华投入了 160 亿美元用于可再生能源。同样，这与我们州想要我们做的——也就是底层政策——高度一致。这个决定不是我们做的，也不是我们想花 160 亿就花 160 亿；这是与州长、议员和监管机构共同完成的。同时，我们有机会关停了 10 台燃煤机组中的 5 台。正如报告强调的，我理解人们希望现在就把另外 5 台也关掉。但想想，我们投了 160 亿、关掉 5 台，这对客户来说是非常好的结果：我们得以维持电价，而我们的电价是全国最低之列。所以这件事做得非常高效。但现实是，我们仍然需要那 5 台燃煤机组来维持系统稳定。我们不能出现西班牙—葡萄牙那样的情况。所以我们绝对尊重这些意见，绝对尊重这个流程，我们会继续与各州合作，确定他们想走的路。我们努力确保有良好、平衡的结果，因为我们理解其他人的诉求所带来的挑战。所以我想你会继续看到，我们的公用事业会执行与其利益相关方、客户需求相一致的政策，同时始终遵守任何联邦标准的要求。谢谢你的意见。

## 英文原文（实录摘录）

**Kansas Lomire**（01:35:27）：Hello, Mr. Buffett and Mr. Abel. My name is Kansas Lohmeier. I am a junior at Elkhorn South High School and was born and raised in Omaha. My question is directed to Greg Abel. Berkshire Hathaway is the second-largest utility provider in the United States, and a 2025 Reuters investigation found that its coal fleet is the dirtiest in the nation. There are currently no concrete plans to phase out coal and fully transition to renewable energy. I’m 17 years old, considering that, what do you have to say to young people like me who will live with the consequences of climate change due to companies like Berkshire?

**Greg Abel**（01:36:24）：Thank you for both your question and your comments because it is important to understand, say, Berkshire Hathaway Energy, but also how they operate and maybe using Iowa, at least, as a starting example, because I think that was one of the States cited in the report. One of the important things that I’d say early in is acquiring our energy companies, and I go back to when we acquired MidAmerican, we acquired it in 1999, Berkshire purchased MidAmerican in 2000. But one thing that became very clear to me and our teams was that what we do within our utilities is really driven on two fronts. One, we absolutely have to meet the requirements in the law that’s laid out federally, but most importantly, we had to recognise that we implement public policy across these states. And that was an interesting conversation when I went back to Iowa, and again, the report cited that as a significant problem. It was early in the 2000s when, for the first time in Iowa, we were going to as a utility, be short of power. So we didn’t have the energy, and we entered into a significant discussion with our governor at the time, and really sat down and said, “Where do you want us to go as MidAmerican? And what resources do you want as a state?” At that time, we were predominantly a coal-based state, and we recognised that obviously and fundamentally, we personally viewed it as a risk, but we needed to have that conversation with our state as to how we would manage that going forward. The interesting thing was that as we had that conversation in the early 2000s, again with the leadership of our state, it was clearly decided we wanted to continue to be long power, i.e. not be short for our customers. We discussed the type of resource, and I remember a very clear conversation around we wanted to stay balanced across a variety of energy sources, and at that time, it was really coal and natural gas. And at that time, we made the decision to build the largest wind project in the US, in Iowa. So we undertook an effort to build three resources: a coal plant, a gas plant, and a what was the first wind project we owned in MidAmerican. And again, it was very consistent with what the state wanted, but we also laid some important groundwork there because we started to define the importance of renewable energy, non-carbon resources, but it has to be consistent with what the state wanted and we’ve gone on over since that period of time to deploy $16 billion into Iowa associated with renewable energy. Again, very consistent with what our state wanted us to do, i.e. the underlying policy. We don’t get to make that decision and spend 16 billion. It’s done in conjunction with our governors, legislators, and regulators, and at the same time, we’ve had the opportunity to retire five of the 10 coal units. Now, as the report highlighted, I understand people would like those other five coal units retired at this time. But to think we deployed 16 billion to retire five, and it’s a very good outcome for our customers. We’ve been able to maintain our rates, which are some of the lowest in the country. So it’s been done very efficiently, but the reality is we still need those five coal units to keep the system stable. We cannot have a Spain-Portugal situation. So we absolutely respect the input. We absolutely respect the process, and we’ll continue to work with each of our states to identify the path they would like to chart. And we work hard to ensure there are good, balanced outcomes because we recognize the challenges associated with other folks’ desires. So I think you’ll continue to see our utilities implement policy consistent with the needs of their stakeholders or customers, and at the same time, always respecting what’s required by any of the federal standards. So, thank you for your comments.
