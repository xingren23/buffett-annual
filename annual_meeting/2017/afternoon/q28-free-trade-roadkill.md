## 问题

美国企业为压低成本关闭本土工厂、把岗位迁往海外，是否该考虑纯经济之外的因素？对员工与社区负有什么义务？若子公司 CEO 请求批准搬迁，除经济账外还会问什么？

## 回答要点

1. Fruit of the Loom 在 1955 年（当时叫 Union Underwear）几乎全部本土生产，「如果今天还全在美国生产，它根本活不下来」；德克斯特鞋业同理
2. 贸易双向都极有利：1970 年美国出口仅占 GDP 的 5%，如今约 12%；人均 GDP 近 6 万美元，实际值是巴菲特出生时的六倍
3. 需要「总教育家」——逻辑上是总统——向公众解释自由贸易的总体好处
4. 同时需要政策照顾「路杀」：无法给新贝德福德那位 55 岁的纺织工人重新培训
5. 投资者可以通过分散配置自保，工人往往做不到；「我们有一个富裕的社会可以做到这一点」
6. 芒格：失业保险正是为此设立，但资本主义在调整过程中总会伤到人，「这无法避免」；并以俾斯麦论香肠与立法的名言收尾

## 关键引述

> **“if it was all domestic now, it wouldn't exist”**
>
> 「如果今天还全部在美国生产，它根本活不下来。」

> **“nobody should be roadkill in this sort of society”**
>
> 「在这样的社会里，不应该有人成为路杀。」

> **“There's no way to avoid it.”**
>
> 「这无法避免。」

**Warren Buffett**（01:59:29）：第十一站。

**Audience Member**（01:59:30）：下午好。我是惠特尼·蒂尔森，来自纽约的股东。我的问题与前面关于裁员的几个问题相关。也许唯一比裁员更让美国工人愤怒的事，就是关掉一个工厂、把岗位搬到海外。你问俄亥俄或密歇根的任何一个人，他们都能讲出一些公司在这个州经营了几十年、享受了当地纳税人出资的教育体系和基础设施。然后来了几位高薪顾问，告诉公司把生产迁到墨西哥或中国就能降本增效。于是啪的一下，好的美国岗位消失了。我的观察是：今天大多数投资者和美国企业界都在「股东价值最大化」的祭坛前朝拜，而这其实是「不惜一切把股价推到最高」的暗语。可这样做的时候，企业的行动让数以百万计的工人往好里说是恐惧和被抛下，往坏里说是被美国企业深深伤害。

这让太多人愤怒，我认为它在考验战后以自由贸易为基础的经济秩序，甚至在考验我们民主制度的强度。我还要说，它在这次大选中起了决定性作用。所以我的问题是：在做这类决策时，企业是否该考虑纯经济之外的因素？它们对员工和所在社区负有什么义务？最后，如果一位伯克希尔子公司的 CEO 来找你，请求批准关掉美国工厂、迁到海外省钱，除了经济账，你会问他什么问题？谢谢。

**Warren Buffett**（02:01:09）：嗯，事实是（掌声），在某些情况下，原本在美国的生产确实被世界其他地方的生产取代了。早年 Fruit of the Loom 还叫 Union Underwear，1955 年由格雷厄姆-纽曼公司买下，我当时在场，那时它大概全部在美国生产。事实是，如果今天还全部在美国生产，它根本活不下来。德克斯特鞋业也是一样。那是一家很好的公司，有技术熟练的工人。但到最后，如果我们按成本定价卖鞋，它们就竞争不过世界各地的鞋。我要说，贸易，无论出口还是进口、无论规模多大，对美国、对世界都极其有利，事实上也确实如此。更高的生产率总体上会让世界受益。

但成为「路杀」，无论是新贝德福德那个最终被裁掉的纺织工人，还是德克斯特那个失去工作的鞋厂工人，都不好受。你没法一辈子对自己说：「我这么做是为了更大的善，是为了鞋子和内裤便宜 5%，而美国公众其实永远不会知道。」所以在我看来，需要两件事。第一，我们是一个极其繁荣的国家，人均 GDP 接近 6 万美元，按实际购买力算是出生时的六倍，难以置信。这份繁荣因贸易而增强：1970 年我们只出口 GDP 的 5%，现在大约 12%。我们在做自己最擅长的事。但我们需要一位「总教育家」，逻辑上就是总统（我不是指这一任，而是任何一位在任多年的总统），必须能向美国公众解释自由贸易总体上带来的好处。

第二，我们必须有政策去照顾在这个过程中成为「路杀」的人。因为对我来说，如果我的生活被一件对 3.2 亿人有极微小好处的事毁掉，而我本来是想好好过日子的，那毫无意义。我们有资源照顾这些人。投资者我不担心。几年前我写过：投资者可以通过分散配置，让贸易总体上有利于自己，不会被某个行业的状况打死。但工人在很多情况下做不到。你没法给新贝德福德那位 55 岁、可能连英语都不太会说的纺织工人重新培训。如果他们被一件对社会有益的事毁掉，他们就是被毁掉了，除非政府出台政策照顾这样的人。我们有一个富裕的社会可以做到这一点，也有一个会因自由贸易而受益的社会。我认为我们应该同时实现两个目标：既不让有人成为路杀，又让 3.2 亿人得到自由贸易的好处。（掌声）查理？

**Charlie Munger**（02:04:57）：嗯，我不反对。我们设立失业保险正是为了这个原因。但我担心，资本主义制度在调整和改进的过程中，总会伤到一些人，这无法避免。

**Warren Buffett**（02:05:13）：对。资本主义对资本也很残酷，如果你待错了行业。就像我说的，那种结果可以靠分散投资化解。资本主义对那种运气不好、把一项技能磨练了几十年的人也很残酷。但一个非常富裕的社会（如果这件事总体上有利于社会），是有能力照顾这些人的。比如新税——几天前通过的法案把我的税降低了 17%。政府真的需要这样吗？

**Charlie Munger**（02:05:49）：我不会急着把那笔钱花掉。

**Warren Buffett**（02:05:50）：不会。而且（（笑）），但那就是……不，我同意。谁知道这个法案最后会怎样。但这件事就这样发生了。我不认为——如果你在奥马哈问一千个正走向购物中心的人，我的税是不是因为这项法案被大幅削减了，我不认为有多少人会有丝毫概念，从报道和整个过程看都是如此。我们的人均 GDP 大概是 5.7 万、5.8 万美元，四口之家就是 23 万美元。但在这样的社会里，不应该有人成为路杀——

**Charlie Munger**（02:06:30）：嗯，记住俾斯麦说的：有两样东西谁都不该去看，一个是做香肠，另一个是做法律。（笑）

**Warren Buffett**（02:06:41）：对。不过我要说，总得有人去看。（笑）总之，我们到了 3 点半这个神奇的时刻。我们 3 点 45 分重新集合，举行正式的股东大会，那可能要花些时间。欢迎你们留下来看，也欢迎你们去购物——我甚至可能稍微倾向于后者。随你们高兴。好。（掌声）

## 英文原文（CNBC 官方逐字稿）

**Warren Buffett**（01:59:29）：Station 11.

**Audience Member**（01:59:30）：Good afternoon. I'm Whitney Tilson, a shareholder from New York. My question is related to the ones asked earlier about job cuts. Perhaps, the only thing that makes American workers angrier than layoffs is to shut down an operation entirely and move the jobs overseas. Ask anyone in Ohio or Michigan, and they'll tell you stories about companies that have been operating in those states for decades, benefitting from the educational system, infrastructure and so forth, things that were paid for by local taxpayers. But, then, some high-paid consultants came along and showed the company how it could reduce its costs by relocating production to Mexico or China. And poof, the good U.S. jobs disappeared. My observation is that most investors and those in corporate America today worship at the altar of maximizing shareholder value, which is code for doing whatever is necessary to boost the share price as high as possible. But in doing so, companies are taking actions that make millions of workers feel, at best, fearful and left behind and, at worst, deeply harmed by corporate America. It makes so many people so angry that I think it's testing the post-World War II economic order, which is rooted in free trade, and even the strength of our democracy. I'd argue that it was decisive in our last election. So my question to you is, do you think that businesses should consider factors outside of pure economics when making these types of decisions? What obligations, if any, do they have to their employees and communities in which they operate? And lastly, if a Berkshire CEO came to you and asked for your approval to close a U.S. operation and relocate it overseas to save money, what questions would you ask beyond the economics of this decision? Thank you.

**Warren Buffett**（02:01:09）：Yeah. Well, the truth is that — (applause) — in certain cases, production that would otherwise — that had formerly been in the United States has definitely been supplanted by production that comes from other parts of the world. Originally — I was there when Fruit of the Loom was called Union Underwear and bought by Graham-Newman Corp in 1955, I believe. And it was probably all domestic then. And the truth is if it was all domestic now, it wouldn't exist. We had the same thing happen with Dexter Shoe. And it was a wonderful company and skilled workers. And in the end, if we sold the shoes at a price that yielded what they cost us, they were not competitive with shoes from around the world. Trade, I would argue — both ways, export, import — massive trade should be — and is, actually — enormously beneficial both to the United States and the world. I mean, it will — it — greater productivity will benefit the world in a general way. But to be roadkill, to be the textile worker in New Bedford that was put out of a job eventually, to be the shoe worker in Dexter — at Dexter to be — was put out of work, you know, is — I mean, it would be no fun to go through life and say, "I'm doing this for the greater good and so that shoes or underwear will sell for five percent less," or something, "and the American public will actually never know." So what you need is two things, in my view. You've got an enormously prosperous country. You've got almost $60,000 of GDP per capita. It's unbelievable — six times what it was when I was born, in real terms. So we've got the prosperity. And that prosperity is enhanced by trade. We were only exporting five percent of our GDP back in 1970, and that's — I think it's around 12 percent or something like that now. We're doing what we do best. But we need an educator-in-chief, logically the president — I don't mean this specific president. I mean any president who's been around for decades — has to be able to explain to the American public the overall benefits of, essentially, free trade. And then, beyond that, we have to have policies that take care of the people that become the roadkill in the process. Because it doesn't make any difference to me if — as far as I'm concerned, if my life is miserable because I've been put out of business by something that's good for 320-some million people in some infinitesimal way, and it's messed up my life when I've tried to live it in a proper way. So we have got the resources to take care of those people. The investors, I don't worry about. I wrote about this a few years ago. The investors can diversify their investments in such a way that, overall, trade probably benefits them and they don't get killed by a specific industry condition. But the worker, in many cases, can't do that. You're not going to retrain some 55-year-old worker in New Bedford who may not even speak English in our textile mill or something. I mean, they — If they get destroyed by something that's good for society, they get destroyed, unless government puts in some policies that takes care of people like that. And we've got a rich society that can do that. And we got a society that will benefit by free trade. And I think we ought to try to hit both objectives of making sure that there is not roadkill and that, at the same time, we get — 320 million people — get the benefits of free trade. (Applause) Charlie?

**Charlie Munger**（02:04:57）：Well, I don't quarrel with that. And we have unemployment insurance for that exact reason. But I'm afraid that a capitalist system is always going to hurt some people as it modifies and improves. There's no way to avoid it.

**Warren Buffett**（02:05:13）：Yeah. Well, capitalism is brutal to capital if you're in the wrong businesses. And, like I say, you can diversify those results. Capitalism is brutal to people that have the bad luck to be skilled or develop their skills for decades. But a rich — a very rich society can actually — if it's beneficial to society overall, it can take care of those people. I mean, it just — you know, the new tax — The bill that was passed a couple days ago reduces my taxes, you know, by 17 percent. You know, and is that needed by the government or anything of the sort?

**Charlie Munger**（02:05:49）：I wouldn't start spending the money.

**Warren Buffett**（02:05:50）：No. And — (laughter) — but that was the will, I mean, of the — No, I agree. I don't think — who knows what happens with the bill? But I'm just — to have that happen, and I don't think — I think if you polled a thousand people in Omaha that were walking to a shopping center as to whether my tax bill had been cut by some very large sum because of what passed, I don't think many people would have the faintest idea what happened, in terms of the coverage of it and all of that that took place. So I — we've got — we do have — it's probably more like 57- or $58,000 of GDP per capita — family of four, $230,000. But nobody should be roadkill in this sort of society —

**Charlie Munger**（02:06:30）：Well, remember what Bismarck said: There are two things that nobody should have to watch. One is the making of the sausage. And the other is the making of legislation. (Laughter)

**Warren Buffett**（02:06:41）：Yeah. Well, I would say that somebody ought to watch. (Laughter) Anyway, we've hit the magic hour of 3:30. We'll reconvene at 3:45 to do — have a formal shareholders meeting. And that may take a while. So, you're welcome to stay and watch that. Or you're welcome to shop. And I might even have a small preference of that. But go — do whatever you wish. OK. (Applause)
