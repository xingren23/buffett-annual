## 问题

随巴菲特逐年捐出 A 股，超级投票权会逐步消失。公司是否已梳理潜在的大额卖方名单，以便像 2012 年 12 月那样通过私下协商回购 A 股？

## 回答要点

1. 每年捐出约 28 亿美元，只相当于苹果一天的成交额，占伯克希尔市值不到千分之七，「不是重要的市场因素」
2. A 股流动性并不像外界担心的那样差，市场能吃下；知道少数持有人手上有 8000 至 10000 股 A 股
3. 2012 年买入约 12000 股 A 股，是因为认为能显著提升内在价值，并按当时市价付给卖方
4. 回购的常规上限是账面价值的 120%；若是规模很大的大宗、且董事们认可，124% 也可能接受
5. 基金会拿到股票后必须花掉，会陆续卖出，因此资本结构不会出问题
6. 巴菲特偏好相当比例的投票权长期集中在真正信仰这种文化的人手里，但承认这部分会持续减少

## 中文全文（逐段翻译）

**Warren Buffett**（00:16:43）：格雷格？

**Gregg Warren**（00:16:48）：沃伦，你对名下持股的安排已经相当公开——大部分集中在 A 股，长期将主要捐给比尔及梅琳达·盖茨基金会和四家家族慈善机构。你每年对这些机构的承诺涉及把 A 股转换出去，而 A 股的投票权远高于 B 股。因此，你遗产所掌握的投票控制权会随时间下降，这一层超级投票权股份会在过程中消失。虽然 B 股股东的投票影响力会上升，但不足以对伯克希尔的事务产生重大影响。考虑到这一点，也考虑到让伯克希尔长期回购并注销 A 股的重要性，我想问：公司是否已在现有股东中梳理出一份潜在未来卖方的名单？鉴于 A 股流动性有限，与这些卖方私下协商交易（像你 2012 年 12 月谈成的那笔），对双方都是最有利的。

**Warren Buffett**（00:17:45）：嗯，这仍然取决于伯克希尔的价格。就我每年捐出的量而言，过去两年大约每年 28 亿美元。你知道，那只是苹果一天的成交额。按伯克希尔的市值算，我捐出去的这些占市值不到千分之七。所以它不是重要的市场因素，也没那么缺乏流动性。我知道少数大持有人，手里可能有 8000 股、10000 股 A 股。但市场现在吃得下。当年我们买下那一大块（我记得是 12000 股 A 股），是因为我们认它会显著提升伯克希尔的内在价值，而我们按当时的市价付给卖方。我们对这件事的接受上限是 120%。谁知道呢，如果某天出现一个 124% 的大宗、规模很大，董事们认为这仍是很可观的折价、可以接受，我们很可能就买了。

但就市场的有序流动而言，不会有什么问题，就像过去也没出过问题——我每年 7 月都捐，过去两年都是如此。有些基金会会持有一阵，但我给他们的钱他们必须花出去，所以他们可能会累积相当金额的 B 股，然后卖掉。确实，在我去世后的一段时间里，遗产和后来的信托里仍会有很多投票权，但那会随时间减少。我看不出我们的资本结构会有什么问题。我喜欢相当一部分投票权集中在真正信仰这种文化的人手里——那些人会去考虑：如果有人带着某个方案上门、承诺股价立刻涨 20%，该不该答应。但最终这部分会减少，而且一直在减少。伯克希尔股票有一个很好的市场。

如果我们能以低于内在价值的价格买到，而有人愿意卖一大块（也许当时股价是账面价值的 122%、124%），我会拿起电话打给董事，看他们是否愿意改变规则。我们这么做过一次。如果合理，我相信他们会同意；如果不合理，我相信他们会拒绝。所以我不认为我们在股票大宗交易上有什么问题，也不认为持有者卖出会有什么问题，更不认为我们判断是否该回购会有什么问题。查理？

**Charlie Munger**（00:21:05）：没什么可补充。

## 英文原文（CNBC 官方逐字稿）

**Warren Buffett**（00:16:43）：Gregg?

**Gregg Warren**（00:16:48）：Warren, plans for your ownership stake, which is heavily concentrated in Class A shares, are fairly well known, with the bulk of the stock going to the Bill and Melinda Gates Foundation and four different family charities over time. Your annual pledges to these different charities involve the conversion of Class A shares, which hold significantly greater voting rights than the Class B shares. As such, the voting control held by your estate will diminish over time, with a whole layer of super-voting shares being eliminated in the process. While the voting influence of Class B shareholders are expected to increase over time, it will not be large enough to have a big influence on Berkshire's affairs. With that in mind, and recognizing the great importance on having Berkshire buy back and retire Class A shares in the long run, I was just wondering if the firm has compiled a pipeline of potential future sellers from the ranks of the company's existing shareholders. Given the limited amount of liquidity for the shares, privately negotiated transactions with these sellers, like the one you negotiated in December of 2012, would end up being in the best interest of both parties.

**Warren Buffett**（00:17:45）：Well, again, it would depend on the price of Berkshire. So, in terms of what I give away annually, you know, it's — the last two years, it's been about 2.8 billion per year. That can be — you know that's one day's trading in Apple. I mean, the amount I'm giving away is, in terms of Berkshire's market cap, I mean, you know, you're down to seven-tenths of one percent of the market cap. So, it's not a big market factor, and it really wouldn't be that illiquid. So, I know a few big holders that, you know, might have 8- or 10,000 shares of A. But the market can handle it now. When we bought that block of — I think it was 12,000 shares of A, I mean, we bought it because we thought it increased the intrinsic business value of Berkshire by a significant amount. And we paid the seller what the market was at the time. And, you know, we're open to that up to 120 percent. And who knows? If it came along at a time and it was 124 percent or something, it was a very large block and the directors decided that that was OK, it still was a significant discount, we might very well buy it. But in terms of the orderly flow of the market or anything like that, there will be no problems just as there haven't been, you know, when I've given away — I do it every July — when I've given away the last two years. Some of the foundations may keep it for a while. But they have to spend what I give them. And they may build up a position in B for, you know, a fairly significant dollar amount. But they're going to sell it. And it is true that for a period after I die, there'll be a lot of votes still in the estate and later in a trust. But, you know, that will get reduced over time. I see no problem with our capitalization over time. You know, I like the idea of a fair number of votes being concentrated with people that believe in the culture strongly and, you know, would be thinking about whether they'd get a 20 percent jump in the stock if somebody came along with some particular plan. But, eventually, that's going to get diminished. It continues to get diminished. And I think, in terms of — you know, there's a very good market in Berkshire shares. And if we can buy them at a discount from intrinsic business value and somebody offers some sum — a big piece — and it may be at a hundred — stock may be selling at 122 percent, 124 percent, you know, I would pick up the phone and call the directors and see if they didn't want to make a change. And we did it once before. And if it made sense, I'm sure they'd say yes. And if it didn't make sense, I'm sure they'd say no. So, I don't think we have any problem in terms of blocks of stock or anything. I don't think people that own it have a problem selling it. And I don't think we have a problem in terms of evaluating the desirability of repurchasing it. Charlie?

**Charlie Munger**（00:21:05）：Nothing to add.
