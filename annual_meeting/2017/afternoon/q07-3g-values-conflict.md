## 问题

卡夫亨氏投资经济上成功（账面 150 亿、2016 年市值 280 亿），但 3G 裁掉 2500 人，「那是私募股权公司干的事」。随 3G 偏离伯克希尔的原则，如何防止它同时损害价值与价值观？

## 回答要点

1. 伯克希尔自己的纺织业务几十年间裁掉约 2000 人，最终仍未救活；百货公司则被沃尔玛、亚马逊取代
2. 两三百年前美国 80% 的劳动人口在农场；若无生产率提升，今天仍要 80% 的人种地养活全国
3. 卡夫亨氏以更少的人做约 270 亿美元的生意，「正是美国企业两百年来一直在做的事」
4. 差别在速度：3G 做得非常快，遣散条件厚道，但不愿两人干一人的活
5. 巴菲特自陈在比阿特丽斯的登普斯特经历过这个过程，「我更愿意把日子花在别的事情上」
6. 芒格：3G 没有道德过错，但会引发对谁都没好处的政治反弹；并以弗里德曼「为什么不发勺子」的笑话作结

## 中文全文（逐段翻译）

> 与下方「英文原文」逐段对应，按发言顺序排列；术语与专名首次出现时保留英文。

**Warren Buffett**（00:25:42）：卡罗尔？

**Carol Loomis**（00:25:44）：这个问题来自乔治·贝纳罗亚，算是接着刚才 3G 那场讨论再加一层。他说：「我是一位非常愉快的长期股东，但对伯克希尔的卡夫亨氏投资有一个担忧。这笔投资在经济上做得不错：账面价值 150 亿美元，2016 年市值 280 亿。但 3G 的基因跟我们很不一样。我们不靠买下公司、解雇员工来赚钱。3G 在卡夫亨氏裁掉了 2500 人。那是私募股权公司干的事，而我们不是私募股权公司。我们的价值观为我们效力了五十多年。风险在于，随着 3G 不断偏离我们的原则，最终会同时损害我们的价值与价值观。我们如何防止这件事发生？」

**Warren Buffett**（00:26:58）：嗯，这很有意思。我刚才提到那是非常渐进的过程。但那大概也是更好的决定。我们在纺织业务上（伯克希尔本身），随着时间裁掉了 2000 人，有些人退休、离职，诸如此类。可它还是没救活。还有霍奇柴尔德-科恩那家百货公司（幸好我们卖给了别人），最终他们还是把店关了，因为百货公司，至少那一家、其实还包括我们在巴尔的摩的竞争对手，都做不下去。沃尔玛带来了新的做法，现在亚马逊又带来新的做法，改变了人们以为了解的东西。我们提到 CTB 做的家禽设备，那其实是很多种农业设备。CTB 开发的农业设备，思路往往是比市面上现有的更高效——也就意味着农场上用的人更少。

两三百年前，美国 80% 的劳动人口在农场干活；如果没有人发明让农业更高效的东西，我们今天还得让 80% 的人种地来养活全国，那我们就会生活在远为原始的状态里。汽车行业越来越高效，任何行业都在设法更高效。沃尔玛比百货公司更高效。这在美国会继续下去，而且最好继续下去，否则我们的孩子不会比我们过得更好。我们的孩子会比我们过得好，因为美国确实在不断变得更高效，人们确实在不断想出新办法。当卡夫亨氏发现他们做那么多生意（大概 270 亿美元的收入），可以用更少的人完成时，他们做的正是美国企业两百年来一直在做的事，也正是我们过得好原因。只是他们做得非常快。在遣散费等方面，他们给得相当厚道。

但他们不想让两个人干一个人能干的活。坦率地说，我不喜欢经历这个过程，因为我面对过。我在内布拉斯加州比阿特丽斯的登普斯特公司面对过，那确实需要改变，但改变对很多人来说是痛苦的。有过一两次经历之后，我更愿意把日子花在别的事情上。但我认为，美国必须变得更高效，因为这是我们提高人均消费的唯一途径——提高人均生产率。查理？

**Charlie Munger**（00:29:49）：嗯，你说得完全对。我们不想回到自给自足的农耕时代。我年轻时在内布拉斯加西部农场干过一周，我恨透了那活儿。（笑）我也不怀念那些整天坐在电梯里、摇着小曲柄的电梯操作员。但另一方面，正如你说的，对必须经历这些的人来说非常难受，那我们为什么还要一次又一次地亲自去做这种事？过去我们在不得不做的时候做过，因为那些生意正在死去。我认为 3G 没有任何道德上的过错，但我也看到会引发一些政治反弹，那对谁都没好处。

**Warren Buffett**（00:30:47）：米尔顿·弗里德曼——我想是他——常讲一个故事，大概是杜撰的：某个共产主义国家有个巨大的工程，成千上万的工人在工地上挥铁锹挖土。他们身后停着几台大型挖掘机，闲置着，而这些机器能用工人二十分之一的时间干完活。经济学家就问当地的党员干部：为什么不用这些机器，用十分之一、二十分之一的时间把活干完，而非要这么多人挥铁锹？那人回答：「是啊，可那样工人就失业了。」弗里德曼说：「那你为什么不发给他们勺子？」（笑）

## 英文原文（CNBC 官方逐字稿）

**Warren Buffett**（00:25:42）：Carol?

**Carol Loomis**（00:25:44）：This is a question from George Benaroya. And it adds a layer to the discussion about 3G a little bit ago. He says, "I am a very happy, long-term shareholder. But this is a concern I have regarding Berkshire Hathaway's Kraft Heinz investment. "This investment has done well in economic terms. The carrying value is 15 billion, and the market value was 28 billion in 2016. "But the DNA of 3G is quite different from ours. We do not make money by buying companies and firing people. 3G fired 2,500 employees at Kraft Heinz. That is what private equity firms do, but we are not a private equity firm. "Our values have worked for us for over four — 50 years. There is a risk that as 3G continues to deviate from our principles, they will, eventually, harm both our value and our values. How do we prevent that from happening?"

**Warren Buffett**（00:26:58）：Well, that's interesting. I mentioned earlier that it was very gradual. But it would've been, probably, a better decision. We fired 2,000 people over time — and some retired and left and all of that — but at the textile operation [Berkshire Hathaway]. You know, it didn't work. And at Hochschild Kohn, the successor — we fortunately sold it to somebody else — but eventually, they closed up the department stores because department stores, at least that particular one and a good many, actually, including our competitors in Baltimore, could not make it work. Walmart came along with something — and, now, Amazon's coming along with something — that changed the way people thought they knew. You — We mentioned our poultry with CTB, which is a lot of different farm equipment. The farm equipment, often, that CTB develops, the idea is that it's more productive than what already is out there, which means fewer people are employed on farms. We had 80 percent of the American public — population, working population — working on farms a couple of hundred years ago. And if nobody had come up with things to make it more productive — farming — we'd have 80 percent of people working on farms now to feed our populace. And it means that we'd be living in a far, far more primitive way. So there — you know — if you look at the auto industry, it gets more productive. If you look at any industry, they're trying to get more productive. Walmart was more productive than department stores, and — That will continue in America. And it better continue or we won't live in — or our kids won't live any better than we do. Our kids will live better than we do, because America does get more productive as it goes along. And people do come up with better ways of doing things. The — When Kraft Heinz finds that they can do whatever amount of business — $27 billion worth of business or something — and they can do it with fewer people, they're doing what American business has done for a couple hundred years and why we live so well. But they do it very fast. They're more than fair, in terms of severance pay and all of that sort of thing. But they don't want to have two people doing the job that one can do. And I, frankly, don't like going through that, having faced that. I faced that down at Dempster in Beatrice, Nebraska. And it really needed change. But the change is painful for a lot of people. And I just would rather spend my days not doing that sort of thing, having had one or two experiences. But I think that it's absolutely essential to America that we become more productive because that is the only way we have more consumption per capita, is to have more productivity per capita. Charlie?

**Charlie Munger**（00:29:49）：Well, I — you're absolutely right. We don't want to go back to subsistence farming. I had a week of that when I was young on a western Nebraska farm. And I hated it. (Laughter) And I don't miss the elevator operators who used to sit there all day in the elevator, run the little crank, you know. So, on the other hand, it — as you say it's terribly unpleasant for the people that have to go through it and why would we want to get into a — the business of doing that over and over ourselves? We did it in the past when we had to, when the businesses were dying. I don't see any moral fault in 3G at all, but I do see that there's some political reaction that doesn't do anybody any good.

**Warren Buffett**（00:30:47）：Milton Friedman, I think it was, used to talk about the time — probably apocryphal — he would talk about the huge construction project in some communist country. And they had thousands and thousands and thousands of workers out there with shovels digging away on this major project. And, then, they had a few of these big, earth-moving machines behind — which were idle — and which could've done the work in one-twentieth of the time of the workers. So the economists suggested to the local party worker or whoever it was that, you know, why in the world didn't they use these machines to get the job done in one-tenth or one-twentieth the time instead of having all these workers out there with shovels? And the guy replied, "Well, yeah. But that would put the workers out of work." And Friedman said, "Well, then, why don't you give them spoons to do it instead," you know? (Laughter)
