## 问题

过去 10 年伯克希尔的内在价值以什么速度复合增长？未来 10 年能以什么速度增长，理由是什么？

## 回答要点

1. 内在价值的严格定义是「从现在到审判日之间的现金流，按当时合适的利率折现」，而合适利率本身三四十年间变化极大
2. 2007 年 5 月至今，巴菲特估计内在价值复合增速约 10%；若利率环境不变，维持这个速度「几乎不可能」
3. 若只能索取一项关于未来的数据，巴菲特不会问 GDP 或总统人选，而会问未来 20 年平均利率
4. 以日本为例：25 年前没人相信其利率能维持，今天仍在看同一件事，说明利率路径极难预测
5. 伯克希尔出现糟糕结果的概率与出现惊人结果的概率都极低；10% 的假设前提是利率略高于过去七年
6. 芒格：规模所限，未来回报率不会像过去辉煌，「这话我们一直在说，现在正在被验证」

## 关键引述

> **“A lot of other people are trying to be brilliant. And we're just trying to stay rational.”**

> **“we do have more of a shareholder orientation than the S&P 500 as a whole”**

## 中文全文（逐段翻译）

> 与下方「英文原文」逐段对应，按发言顺序排列；术语与专名首次出现时保留英文。

**Warren Buffett**（01:12:04）：好，卡罗尔？

**Carol Loomis**（01:12:06）：这个问题来自奥地利的弗朗茨·特拉姆贝格（音）。关于内在价值——沃伦可能会修正我这里的定义——它既不是公司的会计价值，也不是股票市值，而是估算出来的真实价值。问题是：「过去 10 年，伯克希尔的内在价值以什么速度复合增长？未来 10 年你认为能以什么速度复合增长，也请说明理由。」

**Warren Buffett**（01:12:50）：嗯，内在价值只能在事后计算。纯粹的定义是：从现在到审判日之间将产生的现金，按当时看来合适的利率折现。而这个利率在过去三四十年里变化极大。如果你取 10 年，回到 2007 年 5 月，那时有些不太愉快的事要来。但我可以说，我们大概做到了 10% 左右的复合增速。而如果利率环境不变，我认为要维持这个水平会很难，实际上几乎不可能。这是第一位的变量。如果你让我回答这个问题，而我可以先向你索取一项统计数据，我不会问 GDP 增速，不会问谁当总统。一百万种可能里，我会问未来 20 年的平均利率是多少，十年期国债或随便你选哪个期限。

如果你假设目前的利率结构就是未来 10 年、20 年的平均水平，那我要说，做到 10% 会非常困难。反过来说，如果把利率的各种概率都摆上来，我会说 10% 也许是有点理想化，但很可能做得到。如果你说「这样的利率不可能长期维持」，我会请你去看看日本：25 年前，我们也看不出他们的利率怎么能维持下去，而今天我们还在看同样的事。所以我一点也不认为预测利率路径是容易的。不幸的是，要好好回答你的问题，就必须预测它。我要说，伯克希尔出现糟糕结果的概率，大概和你能找到的任何标的一样低；出现惊人结果的概率，也大概一样低。

所以我的最佳猜测是 10% 上下，但这假设未来 10 年、20 年的利率比过去七年略高——不是大幅更高，而是略高。查理？

**Charlie Munger**（01:15:49）：毫无疑问，以我们今天的规模，未来的回报率不会像过去那么辉煌。这话我们一直在说，现在正在被验证。（笑）

**Warren Buffett**（01:16:10）：查理，你想以这句话收尾吗？还是——（笑）

**Charlie Munger**（01:16:14）：嗯，有一点沃伦说得对：我们这批企业的平均投资价值，好于标普的平均水平。所以我不认为你们股东面临什么糟糕的问题。

**Warren Buffett**（01:16:33）：我还要说——我很确定——我们比整个标普 500 更以股东为导向。这家公司的文化是：决策像私人所有者那样做。坦率地说，这是我们拥有而许多公司没有的奢侈。今天很多公司时不时会面临做某些事的压力。我见到每一家上市公司的 CEO 都会问一个问题：「如果这家公司全归你所有，你会有什么不同做法？」答案通常是这个、那个，还有几件事。而如果你问我们，答案是：我们现在做的，就是如果全部股票都归我们自己所有时会做的事。我认为长期看，这是个小小的加分项。查理，还有吗？（掌声）

**Charlie Munger**（01:17:41）：我认为我们还有一个优势。很多人都在努力变得绝顶聪明，而我们只想保持理性。（笑，掌声）这是很大的优势。想变得绝顶聪明是危险的，尤其是在赌博的时候。

## 英文原文（CNBC 官方逐字稿）

**Warren Buffett**（01:12:04）：OK, Carol?

**Carol Loomis**（01:12:06）：This question is from Franz Tramberger (PH) of Austria. And it concerns intrinsic value, which is neither — Warren may rather — he may amend this, my definition here, but — which is neither a company's accounting value nor its stock market value, but is rather its estimated real value. So the question is, "At what rate has Berkshire compounded intrinsic value over the last 10 years? And at what rate, including your explanation for it please, do you think intrinsic value can be compounded over the next 10 years?"

**Warren Buffett**（01:12:50）：Yeah. Intrinsic value, you know, can only be calculated — or gains — you know, in retrospect. But the intrinsic value pure definition would be the cash to be generated between now and Judgment Day, discounted at an interest rate that seems appropriate at the time. And that's varied enormously over a 30 or 40-year period. If you pick out 10 years, and you're back to May of 2007, you know, we had some unpleasant things coming up. But we've — I would say that we've probably compounded it at about 10 percent. And I think that's going to be tough to achieve, in fact almost impossible to achieve, if we continued in this interest rate environment. That's the number one — if you asked me to give the answer to the question, if I could only pick one statistic to ask you about the future before I gave the answer, I would not ask you about GDP growth. I would not ask you about who was going to be president. I would — a million things — I would ask you what the interest rate is going to be over the next 20 years on average, the 10-year or whatever you wanted to do. And if you assume our present interest rate structure is likely to be the average over 10 or 20 years, then I would say it'd be very difficult to get to 10 percent. On the other hand, if I were to pick with a whole range of probabilities on interest rates, I would say that that rate might be — it might be somewhat aspirational. And it might well — it might be doable. And if you would say, "Well, we can't continue these interest rates for a long time," I would ask you to look at Japan, you know, where 25 years ago, we couldn't see how their interest rates could be sustained. And we're still looking at the same thing. So I do not think it's easy to predict the course of interest rates at all. And unfortunately, predicting that is embedded in giving a good answer to you. I would say the chances of getting a terrible result in Berkshire are probably as low as about anything you can find. Chance of getting a sensational result are also about as low as anything you can find. So if I — I would — I — My best guess would be in the 10 percent range, but that assumes somewhat higher interest rates — not dramatically higher — but somewhat higher interest rates in the next 10 or 20 years than we've experienced in the last seven years. Charlie?

**Charlie Munger**（01:15:49）：Well, there's no question about the fact that the future, with our present size is, in terms of percentages of rates of return, is going to be less glorious than our past. And we keep saying that. And now we're proving it. (Laughter)

**Warren Buffett**（01:16:10）：Do you want to end on that note, Charlie? Or would you care to — (Laughter)

**Charlie Munger**（01:16:14）：Well, I do think Warren's right about one thing. I think we have a collection of businesses that on average has better investment values than, say, the S&P average. So I don't think you shareholders have a terrible problem.

**Warren Buffett**（01:16:33）：And I would say we probably — well, I'm certain — we have — we do have more of a shareholder orientation than the S&P 500 as a whole. I mean, for — you know, the — This company has a culture where decisions are made for — as an owner, as a private owner would make them. And frankly, that's a luxury we have that many companies don't have. I mean, they're under pressures today, sometimes, to do things. One of the questions I ask the CEO of every public company that I meet is, "What would you be doing differently if you owned it all yourself?" And the answer, you know, is usually this, that, and a couple of other things. If you would ask us, the answer is, you know, we're doing exactly what we would do if we owned them all — all the stock ourselves. And I think that's a small plus over time. Anything further, Charlie? (Applause)

**Charlie Munger**（01:17:41）：I think we have one other advantage. A lot of other people are trying to be brilliant. And we're just trying to stay rational. And — (laughter and applause) — it's a big advantage. Trying to be brilliant is dangerous, particularly when you're gambling.
