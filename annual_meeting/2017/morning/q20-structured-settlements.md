## 问题

定期赔付年金业务的加权平均利率为 4.1%，在当前利率环境下并不诱人。这些负债的久期是否足够长，使这一资金成本仍有吸引力？还是合约主要签在利率更高的年代？

## 回答要点

1. 这类合约主要是「结构化赔付」：把一次性的百万元级人身伤害赔偿，转为伤者余生按月领取
2. 伯克希尔替其他保险公司签发，法院与家属倾向指定伯克希尔，因为要确保三四十年后承诺方仍在
3. 专挑长久期，每周约做 2000 万至 3000 万美元业务，平均久期取 15 年上下即得出 4.1%
4. 定价始终按当时利率调整，前提是赚到的收益高于赔付内含成本；目前规模约六七十亿美元
5. 相当比例尚未开始付款，期限拉得很远；须跟踪领取人是否在世，故预留了费用额度
6. 若利率长期维持当前水平，4.1% 会随新业务加入而略微下降，固定收益投资将出现小幅亏损

## 关键引述

>
> 「我们一直在寻找期限更长的情形。」

> **“the test is whether we earn, over time, a return above that which we're paying to the injured party”**
>
> 「检验标准是：长期看，我们赚到的回报能否高于付给伤者的成本。」

>
> 「这个数字会随时间上升。」

## 中文全文（逐段翻译）

> 与下方「英文原文」逐段对应，按发言顺序排列；术语与专名首次出现时保留英文。

**Warren Buffett**（02:02:29）：乔纳森？

**Jonathan Brandt**（02:02:34）：能不能谈谈你们的定期赔付年金业务？这些合约的加权平均利率是 4.1%，在当前利率环境下听起来并不特别有吸引力。这些负债的久期是否足够长，使这个资金成本仍有吸引力？还是说这些合约主要是在利率更高的时候签下的？

**Warren Buffett**（02:02:54）：嗯，那些合约主要是所谓的「结构化赔付」。当某个年轻人遭遇严重车祸之类，也许是法院建议、也许是真心为伤者着想的家人建议，他们可能把一笔一次性的大额赔偿转成伤者余生按月领取的款项。赔偿可能是 100 万、200 万美元，大概要向保险公司索赔。我们替其他保险公司签发这类年金。有时法院会指定、或者强烈暗示应由伯克希尔来签发，因为这涉及某个人三四十年后的生活，法院、律师、家属都希望非常非常确定：做出承诺的那一方到时候还在。伯克希尔在这方面有优先地位。回到你的问题，乔纳森：我们一直在寻找期限更长的情形，历来如此。我们必须对死亡率做假设，然后决定用什么利率来做。4.1% 显然是很多年里大量合约的混合结果。

我们每周大概做 2000 万到 3000 万美元这类业务，专挑长期限。如果平均久期取 15 年之类，就得出那个数字。我们始终按当时的利率调整定价。做这件事时，我们假设自己赚到的收益会高于这些结构化赔付内含的成本。这块业务目前规模大概六七十亿美元，我们会继续做。顺便说，这六七十亿里可能有相当大比例还没开始付款（早期那部分款项可能在别人手里），而且期限肯定拉得很远。所以十年、二十年后我们还会在这门生意里。我们有一定天然优势，因为人们比信任任何其他公司都更信任我们来履行这些付款。检验标准是：长期看，我们赚到的回报能否高于付给伤者的成本。这个赌我们愿意下。

但如果利率长期维持当前水平（假设我们把钱放在固定收益工具里），我们会有一点损失。顺便说，我们在里面还留了费用额度，因为我们最终要按月付款给这些人，还得跟踪他们是否还在世。你不能指望某位已故者的亲属，在每月支票还在寄来时，主动及时通知你人已经走了。这个数字会随时间上升；如果利率维持现状，随着新业务加入，4.1% 会略微下降。

## 英文原文（CNBC 官方逐字稿）

**Warren Buffett**（02:02:29）：Jonathan?

**Jonathan Brandt**（02:02:34）：Could you please talk about your periodic payment annuity business? The weighted average interest rate on these contracts is 4.1 percent, which doesn't sound particularly attractive given the current interest rate environment. Is the duration of these liabilities long enough to make that an attractive cost of funds? Or were these contracts executed primarily when rates were higher?

**Warren Buffett**（02:02:54）：Well, those contracts — these are what are called structured settlements, primarily. And when somebody young has a terrible auto accident or whatever it may be — perhaps urged by the court, urged by family members who really do have the interest of the injured party at heart, or — they may convert what could be a large sum settlement, probably against the insurance company — you know, maybe a million dollars, maybe $2 million — into periodic payments for the rest of the life of the injured party. And we issue those for other insurance companies. In fact, sometimes the court directs that Berkshire — or hints strongly — that Berkshire should be the one to issue those, because you're talking about somebody's life 30 or 40 or 50 years from now. And the court, or the lawyer, or the family, they want to be very, very sure that whoever makes that promise is going to be around to keep it. And Berkshire has a preferred position in that. We look — to get to your question, Jonny — we look for taking the longer maturity situations. We always have. And we have to make assumptions about mortality, and we have to make — and then we have to decide at what interest rate we'll do it. The 4.1 is a mix of a lot of contracts over a lot of years, obviously. We write maybe 30 million of these, 20 to 30 million a week, looking for the long maturities. And so, if you take an average of 15 years, or something of the sort, that's how we come up with that sort of a figure. We adjust them to interest rates at all times. And when doing that, we're making an assumption that we're going to earn more money that — than is inherent in the cost of these structured settlements. It's a business we've — I think we've got six or seven billion up now. And we'll keep doing them. And incidentally, probably a significant percentage of the six or seven billion, we're not yet paying anything on. Somebody else may have the earlier payments. And they're certainly weighted far out. So it's a business that we'll be in 10 or 20 years from now. We've got some natural advantage, because people trust us more than any other company to make those payments. And the test is whether we earn, over time, a return above that which we're paying to the injured party. And that's a bet we're willing to make. But if interest rates continued at present levels for a long time — we would, assuming we kept the money in fixed-income instruments — we would — we'd have some loss in that. We've got an allowance in there for the expenses, incidentally, because we do make monthly payments to these people, eventually. And we have to keep track of whether they're still alive or not. Because you cannot count on the relatives of somebody that's deceased when a check is coming in every month to notify you promptly that the person has become deceased. But it's — it'll — That number will go up over time. If interest rates stay where they are, that 4.1 will come down a little bit as we add new business.
