## 问题

苹果的价值是 iPhone、GEICO 是低成本车险、3G 是改善运营，那伯克希尔对世界的价值是什么？既然实行「放手到近乎弃权」，多数子公司独立运营也会一样好，伯克希尔到底创造了什么？

## 回答要点

1. 巴菲特认可「放手到近乎弃权」的描述，但认为这种放手恰恰让子公司运营得更好，好于作为标普 500 成员被维权投资者盯着
2. 伯克希尔的资本实力是另一重价值：合理项目随时拿钱，不必担心银行是否放贷（如 2008 年）、不必在意华尔街是否鼓掌
3. 接手日常事务可替上市公司 CEO 省出至少 20% 的时间，用于琢磨怎么把生意做好
4. GEICO 从车险市场份额 2% 多做到 12%；竞争对手因利润压力收缩新业务时，伯克希尔选择踩油门
5. 「如果 GEICO 是上市公司，做这个决定会比现在难」——因为只想着五年、十年后的事
6. 芒格：伯克希尔想「为世界做个好榜样」——保持清醒、保持诚实；「查理和我几乎什么都不干」

## 关键引述

> **“delegation just short of abdication”**
>
> 「放手到近乎弃权。」

> **“Charlie and I don't do hardly anything.”**
>
> 「查理和我几乎什么都不干。」

> **“Stay sane. Be honest.”**
>
> 「保持清醒，保持诚实。」

## 中文全文（逐段翻译）

> 与下方「英文原文」逐段对应，按发言顺序排列；术语与专名首次出现时保留英文。

**Warren Buffett**（01:56:13）：好，卡罗尔？

**Carol Loomis**（01:56:17）：这个问题来自加州硅谷地区的一位股东，他不愿具名，说不想出风头，但从照片看像是千禧一代。他说：「每位伯克希尔股东都知道伯克希尔的市值，但我问的是伯克希尔对世界的价值。比如，苹果对世界的价值是 iPhone；GEICO 的价值是低成本车险；3G 的价值」——我得说，这里有些股东会有不同意见——「是改善运营。但伯克希尔呢，我就是不知道。在管理子公司上，正如芒格先生那句名言，你们实践的是『放手到近乎弃权』。所以亲力亲为不是答案。这意味着伯克希尔的大多数子公司，如果保持独立，也会做得一样好。所以我的问题是：伯克希尔对世界的价值是什么？」

**Warren Buffett**（01:57:35）：嗯，这个问题——在他把我们的做法准确地描述为「放手到近乎弃权」这一点上，我同意他。但我要说，这种「弃权」在很多情况下恰恰让这些企业运营得更好，比它们作为标普 500 成员、可能成为维权投资者或想在短期内搞点动静的人的目标要好。所以我认为我们的「弃权」对这些公司有相当正面的价值。当然，这要一家一家看。今天在座大概有 50 位经理人。他们自然不会在电视上说什么贬低的话。但你可以把他们拉到角落里私下问一个问题：在伯克希尔这种「放手」模式下，他们的生意能不能运营得更好？他们同时还享有伯克希尔的资本实力：任何合理的项目都能立刻拿到钱，不必担心银行是否还在放贷（像 2008 年那样），也不必担心华尔街是否鼓掌），他们的生意能不能运营得更好。

所以我认为，我们这种袖手旁观的风格，确实在许多公司里创造了显著价值。我们当然不会靠打电话告诉他们「我们发明了更好的路博润添加剂生产方式」来增加价值，也不会说我们能把 GEICO 管得比托尼·奈斯利更好。但在资本配置上我们有非常客观的判断，也能把经理人解放出来。我敢说，我们至少能替一位上市公司 CEO 省出 20% 的时间（原本要见分析师、接电话、应付银行等等），让他们把所有时间都用来琢磨怎么把生意做到最好。所以我认为我们给这场聚会带来了东西，哪怕我们只是把脚翘在桌子上坐着。查理？

**Charlie Munger**（02:00:19）：对。我们想为世界做个好榜样。如果伯克希尔没有一点点教化的意味，我们大概不会办这么大的股东会。我仔细观察这件事很久了。我要说，我们想做的就是树立一个正当的榜样：保持清醒，保持诚实。对。（掌声）所以我为伯克希尔感到骄傲，至于我们卖不卖可口可乐，我不太担心。（笑）

**Warren Buffett**（02:01:00）：我还要说，GEICO 是一家运营得极其出色的公司，即便它是上市公司也会极其出色。但它从车险市场 2% 多一点做到了 12%。其中一部分原因（一小部分；真正的关键是 GEICO 和托尼·奈斯利），是当至少两家大竞争对手在八九个月前说，如果不收缩新业务就无法完成利润目标时，我们决定踩油门。这是个更好的商业决定。但如果 GEICO 是上市公司，做这个决定会比现在难。因为我们只想着 GEICO 五年、十年后会在哪里；如果这需要让新业务的成本在短期内压低我们的盈利，我们接受。别人承受的压力不同。我不是在评判他们的做法，因为他们的利益相关方和 GEICO 与伯克希尔、伯克希尔与股东之间的关系不一样。在这个案例上，我认为我们的制度更优。

但这不是因为我们更努力——查理和我几乎什么都不干。（笑）

## 英文原文（CNBC 官方逐字稿）

**Warren Buffett**（01:56:13）：OK. Carol?

**Carol Loomis**（01:56:17）：This question is from a shareholder in California, in the Silicon Valley area, who didn't want his name mentioned because he said he wasn't looking for publicity, but whose picture makes him appear to be a millennial. "Every Berkshire shareholder knows about the stock market value of Berkshire, but my question is about the value of Berkshire to the world. "For instance, the value of Apple to the world has been iPhones. The value of GEICO is cost-effective auto insurance. The value of 3G," and I will tell you that there are some shareholders who would be arguing about it here, but "the value of 3G is improved operations." "But about Berkshire, I just don't know. In managing Berkshire's subsidiaries, as Mr. Munger once famously said, you practice 'delegation just short of abdication.' So, hands-on management can't be the answer. "That means the majority of Berkshire's subsidiaries would do just as well if they were to stay independent companies. So that's my question. What is the value of Berkshire to the world?"

**Warren Buffett**（01:57:35）：Yeah, well, the — I would say the question about — I'm with him to the point where he says that our — which he accurately describes as "delegation to the point of abdication." But I would argue that that abdication, actually, in many cases, will enable those businesses to be run better than they would if they were part of the S&P 500 and the target, perhaps, of activists or somebody that wants to get some kind of a jiggle in the short term. So I think that our abdication actually has some very positive value on the companies. But that, you know, you'd have to look at it company by company. We've got probably 50 managers in attendance here. And naturally, they're not going to say anything, probably, on television or anything where they knock a certain thing. But get them off in a private corner and just ask them whether they think their business can be run better with a "management by abdication" from Berkshire, but with also all the capital strengths of Berkshire, that when any project that makes sense can be funded in a moment without worrying whether the banks are still lending, like in 2008, you know, or whether Wall Street will applaud it or something of that sort. So I think our very — our hands-off style, actually, I think can add significant value in many companies, but we do have managers here you could ask about that. We certainly don't add to value by calling them up and saying that we've developed a better system, you know, for turning out additives at Lubrizol, or running GEICO better than Tony Nicely can run it or anything of the sort. But we do take a — we have a very objective view about capital allocation. We can free managers up. I would say that we might very well free up at least 20 percent of the time of a CEO in the normal public — who would have — otherwise have a public company — just in terms of meeting with analysts, and the calls, and dealing with banks, and all kinds of things that, essentially, we relieve them of so that they can spend all of their time figuring out the best way to run their business. So I think we bring something to the party, even if it — even if we're just sitting there with our feet up on the desk. Charlie?

**Charlie Munger**（02:00:19）：Yeah. We're trying to be a good example for the world. I don't think we'd be having these big shareholders meetings if there weren't a little bit of teaching ethos in Berkshire. And I've watched it closely for a long time. I'd argue that that's what we're trying to do, is set a proper example. Stay sane. Be honest. Yeah. (Applause) So I'm proud of Berkshire, and I don't worry too much if we sell Coca-Cola. (Laughter)

**Warren Buffett**（02:01:00）：We — I would say, you know, GEICO is an extraordinarily well-run company and it would be extraordinarily well-run if it were public. But it has gone from 2-and-a-fraction percent of the auto insurance market to 12 percent. And part of the reason, a small part — the real key is GEICO and Tony Nicely — but part of the reason is that when other — at least two of our competitors — and big competitors — said that they would not meet their profit objectives if they didn't lighten up their interest in new business, eight or 10 months ago, I think our business decision to step on the gas is a better business decision. But I think that GEICO, as a public company, would have more trouble making that decision than they do when they're part of GEICO [Berkshire]. Because we are thinking about nothing but where GEICO's going to be in five or 10 years, and if that requires having new— we want new business cost to penalize our earnings in the short-term. And other people have different pressures. I'm not arguing about how the —how they behave, because they have a different constituency than GEICO has with Berkshire and what Berkshire has with its shareholders, in turn. And I think in that case, our system's superior. But it's not because we work harder. Charlie and I don't do hardly anything. (Laughter)
