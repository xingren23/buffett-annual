## 问题

股东援引富国银行销售操作调查报告的结论——分散化架构给了社区银行高管太大自主权——追问：伯克希尔同样高度分权、同样给子公司高管极大自主权，巴菲特凭什么确信不会重演同类风险？

## 回答要点

1. 伯克希尔比任何同规模公司都更分权，靠的是行为准则而非规章，每两年给经理人发一封通函：「我们需要的声誉一盎司也不多要，伯克希尔的声誉握在你们手里」
2. 36.7 万名员工，一定有人在干错事；关键是经理层是否在纠错，以及消息能否传到奥马哈并促成行动
3. 富国银行三个错误：一是激励制度按「每客户服务数」设指标，激励了错误行为；二是 CEO 知情后没有立即行动；三是用 1.85 亿美元罚款的数额来衡量问题严重性
4. 所罗门对照：1991 年 4 月 28 日高管已知情却拖延上报，5 月 15 日莫泽再度提交虚假国债投标，几乎拖垮公司
5. 伯克希尔靠热线与匿名信获得线索，一年约 4000 通，由内部审计负责人贝基·阿米克过筛，严重者直接上报巴菲特
6. 芒格：更谨慎地挑选掌权者、建立信任文化，比不断加厚合规部门更有效；「总有一天我们会被人从背后打一闷棍」

## 中文全文（逐段翻译）

> 与下方「英文原文」逐段对应，按发言顺序排列；术语与专名首次出现时保留英文。

**Carol Loomis**（00:15:49）：现在，问第一个问题。关于富国银行——它是伯克希尔最大的股票持仓，去年年底市值 280 亿美元。这个问题来自一位不愿具名的股东。他说：「去年富国银行被销售操作丑闻缠身之后，公司的独立董事委托进行了一次调查，并聘请一家大型律所协助。调查结论相当严厉，已经以《富国银行销售操作报告》的名义公布，网上可以查到。报告的结论是，公司问题的主要根源在于（我引用原文），『富国银行分散化的公司架构，让社区银行的高级管理层获得了太大的自主权』，引文结束。巴菲特先生，伯克希尔同样是高度分散的架构，也给子公司高管极大的自主权，您凭什么确信伯克希尔不会面临同样的风险？」

**Warren Buffett**（00:17:01）：没错。伯克希尔的运营方式，比任何规模相近的公司都要分散，这一点毫无疑问。我们主要依靠行为准则，而不是一大堆规章。每年年会上你们都会看到所罗门那段的描述，原因就在这里。也正因如此，我很少给经理人发通函，但每两年会发一封，大意是：我们需要的钱已经够了，当然多多益善，但那不是必须；而我们需要的声誉，一盎司也不能多要；伯克希尔的声誉握在你们手里。查理和我相信，只要建立起正确的文化，而这种文化在某种程度上会自动筛选出你得到的董事和经理人，那么在行为上，结果会比发一本一千页的手册更好。但无论如何，问题总会出现。我们有 36.7 万名员工。奥马哈都会区大约有 36.7 万个家庭，就在我们说话的当下，一定有人在干错事，这是毫无疑问的。真正的问题是：经理们是不是在操心、在设法发现并纠正不当行为？如果他们没做到，消息能不能传到奥马哈？我们会不会采取行动？富国银行犯了三个非常严重的错误，但其中一个把其余的都盖过去了。几乎任何生意都有激励制度，激励制度本身没有问题，但你得极度小心你激励的是什么。你不能去激励坏行为；万一激励指向了坏行为，你就必须有一套识别机制。富国银行显然把激励制度建立在交叉销售上，建立在「每客户服务数量」上。公司每个季度的投资者演示材料，都在强调每客户服务数。这成了整个组织的主要焦点。毫无疑问，员工的报酬、考核和晋升至少部分取决于这个数字。结果，它激励了错误的行为。我们自己也犯过类似的错误，任何公司在设计制度时都会犯错。但那是错误，迟早会被发现。至于我们怎么发现，我后面会讲。最大的错误在于，我并不清楚富国银行的信息是如何层层上报的，但在某个时点，如果存在重大问题，CEO 一定会听到风声。就在那一刻，一切都取决于他，因为 CEO 必须采取行动。你们看到的所罗门事件就是这样：大约 4 月 28 日，所罗门的 CEO、总裁和总法律顾问坐在一个房间里，听一个叫约翰·梅里韦瑟的人讲述了他们手下保罗·莫泽的恶劣做法。莫泽在欺骗美国财政部，这是极其愚蠢的行为。他这么做有一部分是出于怨恨，因为他不喜欢财政部，财政部也不喜欢他，于是他提交了虚假的美国国债投标。

4 月 28 日前后，CEO 和这些人都知道出了大问题，必须向纽约联邦储备银行报告。CEO 约翰·古特弗罗因德说他会去报，结果没去。他显然因为这事不愉快而拖延了。5 月 15 日又有一场国债拍卖，莫泽再次提交了一批虚假投标。到这一步就完了：最高管理层事先知情，而一个纵火犯在被警告之后又点了一把火。之后一切急转直下。CEO 一旦知道，就必须立刻制止。他们还犯了第三个错误，但相比第二个微不足道：他们完全低估了事情曝光后的冲击。当时的罚款是 1.85 亿美元。在银行业，因为房贷操作之类的事情被罚几十亿的比比皆是。他们用罚款数额来衡量问题的严重性，以为 1.85 亿意味着这比涉及 20 亿的做法要轻，这完全错了。但核心问题是，他们在知情之后没有行动。制度糟糕已经够糟了，不行动更糟。在伯克希尔，我了解子公司不当行为的主要渠道是热线。我们一年大概收到 4000 通热线报告。大多是无聊的，比如隔壁同事口臭之类（笑），但总有几件是严肃的，内部审计负责人贝基·阿米克会全部过一遍。很多是匿名的，可能大部分都是。其中一部分她会转回子公司，可能也是大部分。但任何看起来严重的事，我都会知道，而这已经不止一次促成了行动。我们为此花过真金白银去调查，有时派出专门的调查人员。就像我说的，它揭出过一些母公司绝不会容忍的做法。我认为这是个好系统，但不是完美系统。富国银行肯定也有内部审计，肯定也有热线。我不了解事实，但我敢打赌有大量信息通过热线进来；至于他们把信息送到正确的人手里用的是什么流程，我不知道，也不知道在什么时候是谁做了什么。但如果他们收到了（我相信他们收到了）却置之不理，或者只是往下退给下面的人，那就是一个巨大、巨大、巨大的错误。查理，你一直在跟踪这件事，你怎么看？

**Charlie Munger**（00:25:37）：嗯，先说我的怀疑：当某家律所认为它知道怎么修好这种事的时候，我是不信的。如果你所处的行业里，一大批人所受的激励很可能导致大量不当行为，那你当然需要庞大的合规部门。每一家大型经纪公司都有庞大的合规部门。如果我们有那样的业务，我们也会有个大合规部门，对吧，沃伦？

**Warren Buffett**（00:26:04）：当然。

**Charlie Munger**（00:26:05）：当然。但这并不意味着每个人都该用越来越多的合规去解决自己的问题。这些年来我们的麻烦更少，靠的是更谨慎地挑选掌权的人，靠的是一种信任的文化。我认为我们的麻烦是更少，而不是更多。

**Warren Buffett**（00:26:23）：但麻烦还是会时不时找上门。

**Charlie Munger**（00:26:25）：是的，当然。总有一天我们会被人从背后打一闷棍。

**Warren Buffett**（00:26:28）：查理说一盎司的预防——他崇拜的本·富兰克林说「一盎司预防抵得上一磅治疗」，查理说富兰克林还说轻了，一盎司预防抵得上不止一磅治疗。而我要说，一磅及时施行的治疗，抵得上一吨拖延的治疗。问题不会自己消失。古特弗罗因德最初把那件事称为「一张交通罚单」，他就是这么对所罗门的人说的。结果它几乎拖垮了一家公司。另有一位 CEO 把他遇到的问题称为「一次发球踩线」，结果给机构造成了难以想象的损害。所以你必须立即行动。坦率地说，我想不出比热线和写给我的匿名信更好的办法。我收到过匿名信，过去六七年里大概有三四封带来了重大改变。极少情况下是有署名的。几乎总是匿名，但署不署名没有区别，因为显然不会有人因为提醒我们出了问题而遭到报复。

但我可以告诉你们，就在我们坐在这里的时候，伯克希尔有人（相当一些人），可能正在做错事，通常很轻微，比如偷点小钱之类。但当它变成富国银行那种销售操作时，你就能看到会造成什么样的损害。

**Carol Loomis**（00:15:49）：Now, my first question. It's about Wells Fargo, which is Berkshire's largest equity holding — 28 billion at the end of the year. And this question comes from a shareholder who did not wish to be identified. "In the wake of the sales practices scandal that last year engulfed Wells Fargo, the company's independent directors commissioned an investigation and hired a large law firm to assist in carrying it out. "The findings of the investigation, which were harsh, have been released in what is called the Wells Fargo Sales Practices Reports." You can find it on the internet. "It concludes that a major part of the company's problem was that, and I quote, 'Wells Fargo's decentralized corporate structure gave too much autonomy to the community banks' senior leadership,' end of quote. "Mr. Buffett, how do you satisfy yourself that Berkshire isn't subject to the same risk, with its highly decentralized structure and the very substantial autonomy given to senior leadership of the operating companies?"

**Warren Buffett**（00:17:01）：Yeah, it's true that we at Berkshire probably operate on as — we certainly operate on a more decentralized plan than any company of remotely our size. And we count very heavily on principles of behavior rather than loads of rules. It's one reason at every annual meeting you see that Salomon description. And it's why I write very few communiqués to our managers, but I send them one once every two years and it basically says that we've got all the money we need. We'd like to have more, but we're — it's not a necessity. But we don't have one ounce of reputation more than we need, and that our reputation at Berkshire is in their hands. And Charlie and I believe that if you establish the right sort of culture, and that culture, to some extent, self-selects who you obtain as directors and as managers, that you will get better results that way in terms of behavior than if you have a thousand-page guidebook. You're going to have problems regardless. We have 367,000, I believe, employees. Now, if you have a town with 367,000 households, which is about what the Omaha metropolitan area is, people are doing something wrong as we talk here today. There's no question about it. And the real question is whether the managers at — [audio drops out] — are in a better — are worrying and thinking about finding and correcting any bad behavior, and whether, if they fail in that, whether the message gets to Omaha, and whether we do something about it. At Wells Fargo, you know, there were three very significant mistakes, but there was one that dwarfs all of the others. You're going to have incentive systems at any business — almost any business. There's nothing wrong with incentive systems, but you've got to be very careful what you incentivize. And you can't incentivize bad behavior. And if so, you better have a system for recognizing it. Clearly, at Wells Fargo, there was an incentive system built around the idea of cross-selling and number of services per customer. And the company, in every quarterly investor presentation, highlighted how many services per customer. So, it was the focus of the organization — a major focus. And undoubtedly, people got paid and graded and promoted based on that number — at least partly based on that number. Well, it turned out that that was incentivizing the wrong kind of behavior. We've made similar mistakes. I mean any company's going to make some mistakes in designing a system. But it's a mistake. And you're going to find out about it at some point. And I'll get to how we find out about it. But the biggest mistake was that — and I don't know — obviously don't know all the facts as to how the information got passed up the line at Wells Fargo. But at some point, if there's a major problem, the CEO will get wind of it. And that is — at that moment, that's the key to everything, because the CEO has to act. That Salomon situation that you saw happened because of — on April, I think, 28th, the CEO of Salomon, the president of Salomon, the general counsel of Salomon, sat in a room and they had described to them, by a fellow named John Meriwether, some bad practice, terrible practice, that was being conducted by a fellow named Paul Mozer, who worked for them. And Paul Mozer was flimflamming the United States Treasury, which is a very dumb thing to do. And he was doing it partly out of spite, because he didn't like the Treasury and they didn't like him. So he put in phony bids for U.S. Treasurys and all of that. So on April 28th, roughly, the CEO and all these people knew that they had something that had gone very wrong, and they had to report it to the Federal Reserve Board in New York — the Federal Reserve Bank of New York. And the CEO, John Gutfreund, said he would do it, and then he didn't do it. And he undoubtedly put it off just because it was an unpleasant thing to do. And then on May 15th, another Treasury auction was held, and Paul Mozer put in a bunch of phony bids again. And at this point, it's all over, because the top management had known ahead of time, and now a guy that was a pyromaniac had gone out and lit another fire. And he lit it after they'd been warned that he was a pyromaniac, essentially. And it all went downhill from there. It had to stop when the CEO learns about it. And then they made a third mistake, actually, but again, it pales in comparison to the second mistake. They made a third mistake when they totally underestimated the impact of what they had done once it became uncovered, because they — there was a penalty of 185 million. And in the banking business, people get fined billions and billions of dollars for mortgage practices and all kinds of things. The total fines against the big banks, I don't know whether the total's 30 or 40 or a billion or whatever the number may be. So, they measured the seriousness of the problem by the dimensions of the fine. And they thought $185 million fine signaled a less offensive practice than something involved 2 billion, and they were totally wrong on that. But the main problem was they didn't act when they learned about it. It was bad enough having a bad system, but they didn't act. At Berkshire, we have — the main source of information for me about anything that's being done wrong at a subsidiary is the hotline. Now, we got 4,000 or so hotline reports — or that come — we get communications on the hotline — perhaps 4,000 times a year. And most of them are frivolous. You know, the guy next to me has bad breath or something like that. I mean it's — (laughter) — but there are a few serious ones, and the head of our internal audit, Becki Amick, looks at all those. People — a lot of them come in anonymous, probably most of them. And some of them, she refers back to the companies, probably most of them. And — but anything that looks serious, you know, I will hear about, and that has led to action — well, put it, more than once. And we've spent real money investigating some of those. We put special investigators, sometimes, on them. And, like I say, it has uncovered certain practices that we would not at all condone at the parent company. I think it's a good system. I don't think it's perfect. I don't know what — I'm sure they've got an internal audit at Wells Fargo, and I'm sure they've got a hotline. And I don't know the facts, but I would just have to bet that a lot of communications came in on that, and I don't know what their system was for getting them to the right person. And I don't know who did what at any given time. But that was — it was a huge, huge, huge error if they were getting — and I'm sure they were — getting some communications and they ignored them, or they just sent them back down to somebody down below. Charlie? You've followed it. What are your thoughts on it?

**Charlie Munger**（00:25:37）：Well, put me down as skeptical when some law firm thinks they know how to fix something like this. If you're in a business where you have a whole a lot of people under incentives very likely to cause a lot of misbehavior, of course you need a big compliance department. Every big wirehouse stock brokerage firm has a huge compliance department. And if we had one, we would have a big compliance department too, wouldn't we, Warren?

**Warren Buffett**（00:26:04）：Absolutely.

**Charlie Munger**（00:26:05）：Absolutely, but doesn't mean that everybody should try and solve their problems with more and more compliance. I think we've had less trouble over the years by being more careful in whom we pick to have power and having a culture of trust. I think we have less trouble, not more.

**Warren Buffett**（00:26:23）：But we will have trouble from time to time.

**Charlie Munger**（00:26:25）：Yes, of course. We'll be blindsided someday.

**Warren Buffett**（00:26:28）：Charlie says an ounce of prevention — he said when Ben Franklin, who he worships, said, "An ounce of prevention is worth a pound of cure," he understated it. An ounce of prevention is worth more than a pound of cure. And I would say a pound of cure, promptly applied, is worth a ton of cure that's delayed. It — problems don't go away. John Gutfreund said that problem, originally, was — he called it a traffic ticket. He told the troops there at Salomon it was a traffic ticket. You know, and it almost brought down a business. Some other CEO, that they described the problem that he'd encountered as a foot fault. You know, and it resulted in incredible damage to the institution. And so you've got to act promptly. And frankly, I don't know any better system than hotlines and anonymous letters to me. I get anonymous letters. And I've gotten three or four of them probably in the last six or seven years that have resulted in major changes. And very, very occasionally they're signed. Almost always they're anonymous, but it wouldn't make any difference, because there were — will be no retribution against anybody, obviously, if they call our attention to something that's going wrong. But I will tell you, as we sit here, somebody is doing — quite a few people — are probably doing something wrong at Berkshire, and usually, it's very limited. I mean maybe stealing small amounts of money or something like that. But when it gets to some sales practice like was taking place at Wells Fargo, you can see the kind of damage it would do.
