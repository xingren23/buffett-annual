## 问题

煤炭占 BNSF 运量与收入的比重已从十年均值 24% 降至 18%。长期看煤炭还能贡献多少？经加拿大太平洋港口出口的增长能否抵消国内需求下滑？

## 回答要点

1. 煤炭长期下滑没有疑问，但单一年份高度取决于天然气价格：气价 3.15—3.20 美元时烧煤更便宜，跌到 2 美元则全部转向天然气
2. 电厂不是一夜建成，因此下滑速率无法预测；十年内煤炭占收入的比重会显著下降
3. 「如果你被绑在煤炭上，你就有麻烦了」——BNSF 正在寻找煤炭以外的增长来源
4. 芒格给出相反的长周期判断：极长期看所有碳氢化合物都会被用掉，包括全部的煤，「我从不介意把它们留给下一代」
5. 储能是关键变量：艾奥瓦州风电几年内可接近覆盖客户全部用电，但风电只有约 35% 的时间在吹
6. 今年 BNSF 煤炭运量明显回升，但去年基数极低、库存仍偏高；巴菲特判断十到二十年后运量远低于现在

## 中文全文（逐段翻译）

**Warren Buffett**（01:44:45）：格雷格？

**Gregg Warren**（01:44:47）：沃伦，我的问题也关于伯灵顿北方。尽管现政府相信他们能让煤炭业回来，市场力量仍在推动这个行业衰落。美国 90% 的煤炭消费来自发电，而天然气既更便宜也更清洁，风电和太阳能随着规模扩大、成本下降，也重塑了部分市场。这给伯灵顿北方带来了问题：去年煤炭只占运量和收入的 18%，而此前十年这两项指标的平均值都是 24%。其中一部分原因是过去两个冬天积压了大量煤炭库存，现在似乎终于消化得差不多了。那么长期看，你对煤炭对 BNSF 的贡献有何预期？我知道铁路目前也承运一些经加拿大太平洋沿岸港口出口的煤炭，但那里的增长足以抵消国内需求的下降吗？还是 BNSF 需要更依赖多式联运等板块来弥补流失的煤炭运量？

**Warren Buffett**（01:45:45）：嗯，答案是：煤炭会随着时间下降，这一点没什么疑问。具体到某一年，则很大程度上取决于天然气价格。现在需求比去年上升了不少，因为天然气价格在 3.15 或 3.20 美元，而在很多情况下，公用事业用煤发电比用天然气便宜不少；如果气价是 2 美元，那就全是天然气了。但长期看，我脑子里几乎可以确定，煤炭在铁路收入中的占比会下降。速度有多快——电厂不是一夜之间建成的——所以无法预测速率。如果天然气足够便宜，你会看到大规模转回天然气。所以煤炭占收入的比重会显著下降，十年之内肯定相当明显，逐年怎么走谁也说不准。我们正在寻找煤炭以外的增长来源。如果你被绑在煤炭上，你就有麻烦了。查理？

**Charlie Munger**（01:47:25）：嗯——放到极长期看，我认为所有碳氢化合物都会被用掉，包括所有的煤。所以归根结底，这些碳氢化合物是人类的巨大资源，我不认为我们有什么好的替代品。我从不介意把它们留给下一代，我不喜欢开采得太快。这一点上我常常是孤家寡人。人们认为这些碳氢化合物都会变成搁浅资产、整个世界都会变，而我认为我们迟早会用掉每一滴碳氢化合物——包括作为化工原料。这些事我认为非常难预测。而且我一点也不确定——我最终预期天然气供应会相当紧张。

**Warren Buffett**（01:48:33）：储能技术一旦变化，局面会大不相同。几年之内，我们在艾奥瓦州用风电发出的电量，将几乎等于客户的全部用电量。但风只有大约 35% 的时间在吹，有时候又吹得太猛。要 24 小时、每周七天稳定供电，储能是真问题，即便我们在艾奥瓦很快就能基本自给。煤炭——今年我们在 BNSF 上的煤炭运量增长不少，但去年非常低，正如你说的，库存涨上去后又降了一些，目前仍偏高。但在我看来（查理在这件事上更看长期），十到二十年后我们的煤炭运量会比现在少得多。另一方面，其他长距离运输品类的前景还不错，铁路运输大宗商品的长途成本相当低。我认为这是门好生意，只是煤炭那一块会萎缩。

## 英文原文（CNBC 官方逐字稿）

**Warren Buffett**（01:44:45）：Gregg?

**Gregg Warren**（01:44:47）：Warren, my question also relates to Burlington Northern. Despite the current administration's belief that they can bring the coal industry back, market forces continue to lead to the industry's demise. While 90 percent of U.S. coal consumption is driven by electricity generation, natural gas has been both cheaper and cleaner burning, and renewable electricity generation has remade parts of the market as wind and solar have gained scale and become cheaper alternatives. This has created problems for Burlington Northern, with coal shipments accounting for just 18 percent of volume and revenue for the railroad last year, down from an average of 24 percent for both measures the previous 10 years. While some of this was due to large buildup of coal supplies the past couple of winters, which finally seem to be working their way out, what are your expectations for the contribution coal can make to BNSF longer term? And I know that the railroad currently handles some export shipments going through Canada's Pacific Coast ports, but will there be enough growth there to offset domestic demand? Or will BNSF need to rely more heavily on segments like intermodal to offset lost coal volumes?

**Warren Buffett**（01:45:45）：Yeah, the answer is coal's — coal is going to go down over time. I don't think there's much question about that. The specifics of any given year relate very importantly to the price of natural gas. I mean, right now there are — there — Demand is somewhat up — fair amount up — from last year because natural gas is at 3.15 or 3.20, and the utilities can produce electricity, in many cases, quite a bit cheaper with coal than with natural gas. Whereas, with a $2, it would all be — it would be natural gas. But over time, coal is — in my mind — is essentially certain to decline as a percentage of the revenue of the railroad. The speed at which it does, you know, it — you don't build — create generation plants overnight. And so it — You can't predict the rate. And if natural gas is cheap enough, it's going to be a — you'll see a big conversion back to natural gas. So coal is a — coal is going to go down, as a percentage of revenues, significantly. You know, certainly over 10 years it'll be quite significant, and who knows exactly, year by year. We are looking for other sources of growth than coal. If you're tied to coal, you got problems. Charlie?

**Charlie Munger**（01:47:25）：Well — you go out over the extremely long term, I think that all hydrocarbons will be used, including all the coal. So I think that, in the end, these hydrocarbons are a huge resource for humanity, and I don't think we've got any good substitute. And I've never minded saving them for the next generation. I don't like using them up very fast. So, I'm often on a road on my own on this one. And people think that all this hydrocarbons are going to be stranded and the whole world's going to change. I think we're going to use every drop of the hydrocarbon sooner or later. We'll use them as chemical feed stocks. It's — I regard all these things as very hard to predict. And I'm not at all sure that — I would eventually expect natural gas to be pretty short in supply.

**Warren Buffett**（01:48:33）：A change in storage would make a big difference. We will produce, within a few years, as much electricity in Iowa — or virtually as much — electricity in Iowa from wind as our customers use. But the wind only blows about 35 percent of the time or something like that. And sometimes it blows too hard. But the storage, you know, having it 24 hours a day, seven days a week, is a real problem, even if we've got the capability of producing, like I say, a self-sufficient amount, essentially, in Iowa before very long. Coal — our shipments of coal are up fairly substantially this year on the BNSF. But they were very low last year, and as you said, stockpiles grew and have come down somewhat. They're still on the high side. But in my mind — Charlie's got a longer-term outlook on this — in my mind, we're going to be shipping a whole lot less coal 10 or 20 years from now than we are now. On the other hand, I think there's some decent prospects in other long hauls. I mean, it's a pretty cheap way to move bulk commodities long distance. Rail is. And I think it's a good business, but the coal aspect of it's going to diminish.
