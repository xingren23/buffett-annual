## 问题

以泰德·威廉姆斯的「好球带」作比：两位看潜在标的时，一家公司要具备哪些特质，才算落在你们的好球带里、值得挥棒？

## 回答要点

1. 巴菲特：无法精确定义，「看到就知道」；核心是能往前看 5 年、10 年、20 年，确认当下的竞争优势还在
2. 第二个条件是信得过、并且渴望融入伯克希尔文化的经理人；剩下的只是价格问题
3. 1972 年买喜诗糖果付 2500 万美元（扣除账上现金），当时税前利润约 400 万美元，累计取出的税前利润已近 20 亿美元
4. 判断依据是人们不会为低价糖果买单：情人节送糖时「我挑了报价最低的那家」说不出口
5. 芒格坦承当年又年轻又无知，若对方多要 500 万美元，巴菲特就不会买，「我们因为显得比实际更聪明而得到了太多赞誉」
6. 早期收购便宜烂公司的痛苦经验，让两人此后极擅长避开修不好的生意；「猪耳朵做不出丝绸钱包」

## 关键引述

> **“we sort of know it when we see it”**
>
> 「我们大概是看到就知道。」

> **“I took the low bid.”**
>
> 「我挑了报价最低的那家。」

> **“We learned we could not make a silk purse out of a sow's ear.”**
>
> 「我们明白了，猪耳朵做不出丝绸钱包。」

> **“our early stupidity helped us”**
>
> 「我们早年的愚蠢帮了我们。」

## 中文全文（逐段翻译）

> 与下方「英文原文」逐段对应，按发言顺序排列；术语与专名首次出现时保留英文。

**Warren Buffett**（00:30:31）：好，第一站，这位股东。

**Audience Member**（00:30:34）：沃伦、查理，你们好。我叫布莱恩·马丁，来自伊利诺伊州斯普林菲尔德。在 HBO 的纪录片《成为沃伦·巴菲特》里，你有个很好的比喻，把投资比作打棒球、要知道自己的好球带。泰德·威廉姆斯知道他的好球带是正中直球。两位看潜在投资标的的时候，一家公司具备哪些特质，才算落在你们的好球带里、值得你们挥棒？

**Warren Buffett**（00:31:05）：嗯，我没法完全按你想要的方式给它下定义，但我们大概是看到就知道。这种生意往往能让我们因为这样或那样的理由，往前看 5 年、10 年、20 年，并断定它当下的竞争优势能撑过这段时间。它还要有一位信得过的经理人，不仅契合伯克希尔的文化，而且渴望加入这种文化。剩下就是价格问题。主要的一点是：我们买一家公司，本质上就是现在拿出一大笔钱，赌这家公司未来能交付什么。我们对这个预测越有把握，感觉就越好。可以回到第一笔（那不是我们买的第一家出色企业，但算是个分水岭），喜诗糖果，一家不大的公司。1972 年我们看它的时候，问题是：人们还会不会继续吃它、送它，而不是买别的糖果？会不会变成谁出价低就买谁的？

而且我们很喜欢当时的经理人。我们付了 2500 万美元（扣除账上现金），那时它税前利润约 400 万美元。到今天，从它身上取出的税前利润大概快到 20 亿美元了。这一切的前提，是我们判断人们不会单纯去买更便宜的糖果。如果你在情人节走到太太或女朋友面前（我希望这两个人是同一个（笑）），说「亲爱的，这是一盒糖，我挑了报价最低的那家」，这话说下来效果就差点意思了。我们对喜诗糖果的判断是：它是特别的——2017 年也许未必，但我们确信它在 1982 年、1992 年是特别的。幸运的是，我们判断对了。我们现在还在找更多的喜诗糖果，只是要大得多。查理？

**Charlie Munger**（00:33:46）：嗯，不过也要说清楚，那时候我们又年轻又无知。

**Warren Buffett**（00:33:53）：现在我们又老又无知。对。（笑）

**Charlie Munger**（00:33:55）：这倒是真的。事实是，当年用稍高一点的价钱买下喜诗糖果会非常明智。可如果对方真开了那个价，我们就不会买了。所以我们因为显得比实际更聪明，得到了太多赞誉。

**Warren Buffett**（00:34:11）：对，说准确点，如果当时多要 500 万，我就不会买。查理是愿意买的。幸好没走到必须那样决定的地步。他会往前推，而我大概会退缩。幸亏当时来了那么一位卖家——其实是喜诗夫人的孙子，对吧查理？他是拉里·喜诗的儿子，我没记错吧？或者是拉里·喜诗的兄弟。他对这门生意没兴趣，他更感兴趣的是姑娘和葡萄。他差点改主意，后来确实改了主意，答应卖。我不在场，但里克·盖林告诉我，查理进去讲了一个小时，讲有糖果公司不如有姑娘和葡萄的道理（笑）。各位，这是真事。那人就卖给我们了，所以——（笑）遇到这种急事我就把查理请出来。他——（笑）

**Charlie Munger**（00:35:24）：我们很幸运：早年养成了买烂公司、因为便宜就买的习惯，这让我们积累了大量经验，去修那些修不好、正一路滑向深渊的生意。那段修无可修的经历太痛苦了，所以此后我们非常擅长避开这类东西。所以我要说，我们早年的愚蠢帮了我们。

**Warren Buffett**（00:35:52）：对，对。我们明白了，猪耳朵做不出丝绸钱包。

**Charlie Munger**（00:35:55）：不，我们学到的是——

**Warren Buffett**（00:35:56）：所以从那以后，我们就出门找丝绸了。

**Charlie Munger**（00:35:58）：可你得试很久、失败很久、被摁在里面反复摩擦，才真的懂这个道理。

## 英文原文（CNBC 官方逐字稿）

**Warren Buffett**（00:30:31）：OK. Station 1. The shareholder.

**Audience Member**（00:30:34）：Hi, Warren and Charlie. My name is Bryan Martin and I'm from Springfield, Illinois. In the HBO documentary, "Becoming Warren Buffett," you had a great analogy comparing investing to hitting a baseball and knowing your sweet spot. Ted Williams knew his sweet spot was a pitch right down the middle. When both of you look at potential investments, what attributes make a company a pitch in your sweet spot that you'll take a swing at and invest in?

**Warren Buffett**（00:31:05）：Well, I'm not sure I can define it in exactly the terms you would like, but the — we sort of know it when we see it. And it would tend to be a business that, for one reason or another, we can look out five, or 10, or 20 years and decide that the competitive advantage that it had at the present would last over that period. And it would have a trusted manager that would not only fit into the Berkshire culture, but that was eager to join the Berkshire culture. And then it would be a matter of price. But the main — you know, when we buy a business, essentially, we're laying out a lot of money now based on what we think that business will deliver over time. And the higher the certainty with which we make that prediction, the better off — the better we feel about it. You can go back to the first — it wasn't the first outstanding business we bought, but it was kind of a watershed event — which was a relatively small company, See's Candy. And the question when we looked at See's Candy in 1972 was, would people still want to be both eating and giving away that candy in preference to other candies? And it wouldn't be a question of people buying candy for the low bid. And we had a manager we liked very much. And we bought a business that was — paid $25 million for it, net of cash, and it was earning about 4 million pretax then. And we must be getting close to $2 billion or something like that, pretax, that was taken out of it. But it was only because we felt that people would not be buying, necessarily, a lower-price candy. I mean it does not work very well if you go to your wife or your girlfriend on Valentine's Day — I hope they're the same person — (laughter) — and say, you know, "Here's a box of candy, honey. I took the low bid." You know, it doesn't — it loses a little as you go through that speech. And we made a judgment about See's Candy that it would be special and — probably not in the year 2017 — but we certainly thought it would be special in 1982 and 1992. And fortunately, we were right on it. And we're looking for more See's Candies, only a lot bigger. Charlie?

**Charlie Munger**（00:33:46）：Yeah, well, but it's also true that we were young and ignorant then. And —

**Warren Buffett**（00:33:53）：Now we're old and ignorant. Yeah. (Laughter)

**Charlie Munger**（00:33:55）：And yes, that's true, too. And the truth of the matter is that it would have been very wise to buy See's Candy at a slightly higher price. You know if they'd asked it, we wouldn't have done it, so we've gotten a lot of credit for being smarter than we were.

**Warren Buffett**（00:34:11）：Yeah, and to be more accurate, if it had been 5 million more, I wouldn't have bought it. Charlie would have been willing to buy it, so, yeah. Fortunately, that we didn't get to the point where we had to make that decision that way. But he would've pushed forward when I probably would've faded. It's a good thing that a guy came around — actually the seller was the — well, he's the grandson of Mrs. See, wasn't he, Charlie? He was Larry See's son. Am I correct? Or Larry See's brother. But he was not interested in the business. And he was interested in — more interested in girls and grapes, actually. And he almost changed his mind. Well, he did change his mind about selling. And I wasn't there, but Rick Guerin told me that Charlie went in and gave a — an hour talk on the merits of girls and grapes over having a candy company. (Laughter) This is true, folks. And the fellow sold to us, so that — (laughter) — I pull Charlie out in emergencies like that. He's — (Laughter)

**Charlie Munger**（00:35:24）：We were very lucky that, early, the habit of buying horrible businesses because they were really cheap. It gave us a lot of experience trying to fix unfixable businesses as they headed downward toward doom. And that early experience was so horrible, fixing the unfixable, that we were very good at avoiding it, thereafter. So, I would argue that our early stupidity helped us.

**Warren Buffett**（00:35:52）：Yeah, yeah. We learned we could not make a silk purse out of a sow's ear.

**Charlie Munger**（00:35:55）：No, we learned —

**Warren Buffett**（00:35:56）：So, we went out looking for silk after that.

**Charlie Munger**（00:35:58）：But you have to try it for a long time and fail and have rub — have your nose rubbed in it to really understand it.
