## 问题

女性进入劳动力市场让人才倍增。但标普 500 董事中女性不到 21%、CEO 只有 5%。怎么看？

## 回答要点

1. 两个妹妹「跟我一样聪明、性格更好」——但完全没有我当年的机会
2. 1942 年《纽约时报》招聘广告按男女分类——「反而极大地帮助了我」
3. 芒格：「过去是一个非常奇怪的国家」——世界真的变了
4. 「在伯克希尔从未见过公开的性别歧视」——但人性的怪癖可能有
5. 「一切一直在改善，会继续改善。」

## 关键引述

> **“the past is a very strange country”**
>
> 「过去是一个非常奇怪的国家」

## 中文全文（逐段翻译）

> 与下方「英文原文」逐段对应，按发言顺序排列；术语与专名首次出现时保留英文。

**Warren Buffett**（01:53:42）：好，安德鲁。

**Andrew Ross Sorkin**（01:53:55）：这个问题来自 Impactive Capital 管理合伙人劳伦·泰勒·沃尔夫。她说：「沃伦，您最近说让您对美国乐观的因素之一是女性进入劳动力市场、人才实际上等于加倍。但在领导岗位上，标普 500 公司董事中女性不到 21%，CEO 只有 5%……」

**Warren Buffett**（01:54:29）：是的。再说一次（掌声）就像我过去指出的，我有个妹妹今天在场。我有两个妹妹，她们和我一样聪明，性格还比我好——认识我们的人都能作证。但她们当年得到的机会，跟我完全没法比。

你看这份 1942 年的《纽约时报》——那时女性只能当护士、教师、售货员或速记员。这在我小时候的奥马哈，对我其实是巨大的好处：我碰到的老师好得多，因为教书是对女性开放的职业。我小学时一个男老师都没有，查理在邓迪上学时我想也没有。

我们有一个巨大的人才库，被挤进极少的几个出口，因此我们从市场体系的产出里，得到的好老师好过我们应得的。

再说一次，我们的经理人自己经营公司。但在这次管理层变动之前，过去五六年我大概只任命过六七个 CEO——我们变动不多。我要说，我任命的其中一半是女性，这与按能力本该出现的结果大致相符。当然存在某种「人才管道」问题，但那会随时间解决，不能永远拿它当借口。我对我们选出的 CEO 感觉很满意。

我希望我们所有 CEO 都长生不老。我们雇的一位女性几乎做到了：B 太太活到 104 岁，103 岁才退休。这对其他经理人是个教训：如果你过早退休（笑）后面会发生什么可说不准。

这确实让我乐观，让我对人类乐观，当然也对我们国家乐观。因为如果你看第十九修正案之前、之后很长一段时间，直到今天，进步是显著的。我对未来更乐观，因为我认为将来会更多按才干来选人，而不是按性别、种族或继承。如果一个体系里所有企业都传给长子之类，我认为那个社会的进步会远不如一个凭才干的体系。查理？

**Charlie Munger**（01:57:53）：嗯，我们确实活在不同的年代。有句老话，过去是一个非常奇怪的国家。那里的行为完全不同。我高中只有一两个男老师。世界真的变了。在伯克希尔，我从未见过任何公开的性别歧视。

**Warren Buffett**（01:58:43）：可能也有些。——

**Charlie Munger**（01:58:45）：不不，我相信我们也有——

**Warren Buffett**（01:58:47）：哦，肯定有——

**Charlie Munger**（01:58:48）：——人性的所有怪癖。

**Warren Buffett**（01:58:49）：当然。

**Charlie Munger**（01:58:50）：但总体上，一切一直在改善，你不同意吗？

**Warren Buffett**（01:58:47）：对。

**Charlie Munger**（01:58:48）：而且我认为会继续改善。

## 英文原文（CNBC 官方逐字稿）

**Warren Buffett**（01:53:42）：OK, Andrew.

**Andrew Ross Sorkin**（01:53:55）：This question — we got a couple like this one — comes from Lauren Taylor Wolfe. She's a managing partner at Impactive Capital. "Warren, you've recently said that one of the things that makes you optimistic about America is women entering the workforce and the quote 'doubling of the talent that's effectively employed in that workforce.' When it comes to positions of leadership, however, women make up less than 21 percent of boards of S&P 500 companies, and an even smaller 5 percent of the CEOs. "What can Berkshire do, and what is Berkshire specifically doing, as a major investor in many of these large companies, to advance gender equality, both at the board level and among senior leadership?"

**Warren Buffett**（01:54:29）：Yeah. Well, again — (applause) — you know, as I've pointed out in the past, one of my sisters is here. And I have two sisters that are absolutely as smart as I am. And they have better personalities, as anybody that knows both of us — or all of us — can attest. And they didn't remotely have the same opportunities I had. And you have this 1942, or — New York Times — and you know, women could be nurses or teachers or retail clerks or stenographers. And that actually worked enormously to my advantage when I was a kid in Omaha in the '30s, because I had way better teachers, because they were — that was a job open to women, I didn't have a single male teacher in grammar school, and Charlie didn't when he went to Dundee, I don't think, either. And we had this huge talent pool that was being funneled into very few opportunities, and therefore we got better than we deserved in terms of a market system producing it. The — Again, our managers run their companies. But I've probably named — before we made this management change — I probably named only six or seven CEOs in the last five or six years. We don't change that much. But I would say that half of them that I've named have been women, which is about what you would — what should turn out to be the case in terms of ability. Now, there is a certain pipeline problem, but that gets cured with time, and you can't use that forever as an excuse. And you know, I feel very good about the decisions we made for CEOs. I prefer all our CEOs to live forever. And one woman almost did that, that we hired. Mrs. B. [Nebraska Furniture Mart founder Rose Blumkin] lived to be 104. She retired at 103. And that's a lesson to our other managers, that if you retire prematurely, you know — (laughter) — no telling what'll happen. But it is absolutely true. It does make me bullish. It makes me bullish on the human race. But it's certainly on our country. Because if you look at what happened, you know, before the 19th Amendment, and then after the 19th Amendment for a long time, and continuing to this day, but it's — that — there's been significant improvement. And I do feel more optimistic about the future, because I think there will be more selection by merit, rather than, you know, by gender or by race or by inheritance. I think that if you had a system where all businesses got passed on to the eldest son or something, I think it — I think that society would make a lot less progress than one that's merit-based. Charlie?

**Charlie Munger**（01:57:53）：Well, we did live in a different age. There's an old saying that the past is a very strange country. People behave quite differently there. And it was just totally different. And it was ridiculous that — I cannot remember — I had one or two male teachers in my high school. But almost none. And the world has really changed. And within Berkshire, I've never seen any overt discrimination anywhere on the grounds of gender.

**Warren Buffett**（01:58:43）：There probably has been some, though. I mean —

**Charlie Munger**（01:58:45）：No, no, I'm sure that we have our —

**Warren Buffett**（01:58:47）：Oh, there has —

**Charlie Munger**（01:58:48）：—share of all the peculiarities of human nature.

**Warren Buffett**（01:58:49）：Sure.

**Charlie Munger**（01:58:50）：But it's generally, it's — everything has always improved, wouldn't you agree with that?

**Warren Buffett**（01:58:47）：Yeah.

**Charlie Munger**（01:58:48）：And I think it'll keep improving.
