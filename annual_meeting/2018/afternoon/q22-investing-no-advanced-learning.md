## 问题

今天要成功是否仍回到《聪明的投资者》第 8 章？怎么看 CFA 等证书？

## 回答要点

1. 巴菲特上过三所商学院，每所都遇到一两个好老师——「我们不反商学院」
2. 格雷厄姆的方法「不可扩展」——600 万美元的基金
3. 芒格：格雷厄姆晚年承认其方法不一定在所有条件下管用
4. 「你以前知道的永远不够」——「独腿人踢屁股比赛」的比喻
5. 「听到理论被形容为『优雅』时要当心」
6. 芒格用过时白内障手术解释为什么金融老师教错东西

## 关键引述

> **“What you formerly knew is never enough.”**
>
> 「你以前知道的永远不够。」

> **“You're like a one-legged man in an ass-kicking contest.”**
>
> 「你就像踢屁股比赛里的独腿人。」

**Audience Member**（01:45:04）：你好，我是密苏里州圣路易斯的布雷迪·里奇（音）。1996 年起就是股东。

**Warren Buffett**（01:45:09）：太好了。

**Audience Member**（01:45:10）：沃伦，你和查理过去批评过商学院及其教学。关于价值投资，在《格雷厄姆-多德都市的超级投资者》中，你们展示了不同背景投资者的回报，核心教训是「遵循理念是关键」。今天要成功，是不是仍然回到《聪明的投资者》第 8 章？你们怎么看 CFA 等项目和证书？

**Warren Buffett**（01:45:39）：最后一部分是什么？

**Charlie Munger**（01:45:41）：嗯，他在说——我们怎么看——

**Warren Buffett**（01:45:43）：对，商学院等等。

**Charlie Munger**（01:45:45）：——商学院等等。

**Warren Buffett**（01:45:46）：我没听到最后一——

**Charlie Munger**（01:45:48）：他们更好了——

**Warren Buffett**（01:45:49）：哦，他向我挑战打桥牌。（笑）我上过三所商学院，每所都找到一两个真正让我受益匪浅的老师。所以我们一点也不反商学院。我们确实觉得三四十年前的「教士团」——有效市场理论那些——偏离得太远了。

**Charlie Munger**（01:49:08）：嗯，你发现格雷厄姆时他不按常规出牌，而且非常聪明。当然对你很有吸引力。然后你发现它管用、坐着就能赚大钱——（笑）——你立刻就皈依了。

**Warren Buffett**（01:49:47）：现在仍然吸引我。（笑）

**Charlie Munger**（01:49:53）：但世界变了。格雷厄姆去世前就承认，他寻找被低估公司的那套方法不一定在所有时期、所有条件下都管用。对我们确实如此。我们逐渐转向在好公司被低估时买入，而不是买被低估的烂公司。效果相当好。格雷厄姆本人也超越了他玩的那套游戏。

**Warren Buffett**（01:50:58）：对。

**Charlie Munger**（01:50:59）：你以前知道的永远不够。如果你不学会不断修正以前的结论、变得更好——我总用同一个比喻——你就像踢屁股比赛里的独腿人。（笑）

**Warren Buffett**（01:51:22）：如果有人有别的比喻，发给我。（笑）格雷厄姆有一点很重要——不可扩展。大钱做不了。我在格雷厄姆-纽曼公司工作时，他是所有分析师的导师、那个时代最杰出的头脑。但基金只有 600 万美元……

**Charlie Munger**（01:52:44）：我终于想通了为什么公司金融老师总教一些错的东西。我年轻时眼睛有问题，去看了一个非常有名的眼科医生。我发现他在做一个完全过时的白内障手术——有了更好的方法他还在用刀割。我说「你为什么在顶尖医学院做完全过时的手术？」他说「查理，那手术太美妙了……」

**Warren Buffett**（01:53:32）：对，每当听到一个理论被形容为「优雅」时，要当心。（笑）

**Charlie Munger**（01:53:41）：没错。

To be successful today, does it still just fall back to chapter eight of "The Intelligent Investor?" And I'd like to challenge you to a round of bridge tomorrow. (Laughter)

**Charlie Munger**（01:45:41）：Well, he was talk — what do we think about —

**Warren Buffett**（01:45:43）：Yeah, business schools and all that.

**Charlie Munger**（01:45:45）：— business schools and all that.

**Warren Buffett**（01:45:46）：I didn't catch the last —

**Charlie Munger**（01:45:48）：They're better —

**Warren Buffett**（01:45:49）：Oh, he's challenged me to a round of bridge. OK. The — (Laughter) I went to three business schools, and at each I found a teacher or two. I went to one specifically to get a given teacher. But at each one of them I found a teacher or two that I really got a lot out of. The — so we're not anti-business school here at all. We do think that the priesthood, say, 30 years ago, for example, in terms of — or 40 years ago — in terms of efficient market theory and everything. They strayed pretty far, in our view, from the reality of investing. And I would rather have a person — if I could hire somebody among the top five graduates of number one, two or three of the business schools, and my choice was somebody that had — was bright — but had chapter eight of "The Intelligent Investor" absolutely — it just was natural to them — they had it in their bones, basically — I'd take the person from chapter eight. This is not — what we do is not a complicated business. It's got to be a disciplined business, but it is — it does not require a super IQ or anything of the sort. And there are a few fundamentals that are incredibly important, and you do have to understand accounting. And it helps to get out and talk to consumers and start thinking like a consumer in many ways in certain — and all of that. But it just doesn't require advanced learning. And — I — I certainly — you know, I didn't want to go to college, so I don't know whether I would have done better or worse if I'd just quit after high school, you know, and read the books I read and all of that. I think that if you run into a few great teachers, and they really change the way you see the world to some degree, you know, you're lucky. And you can find them in — you can find them in academia and you can find them in ordinary life. And I've been extraordinarily lucky in having great teachers, including Charlie. I mean, Charlie's been a wonderful teacher. And, you know — Anyplace you can find somebody that gives you insights into things you didn't understand before, maybe makes you a better person than you would have been before, you know, you get — that's very lucky, and you want to make the most of it. If you can find it in academia, make the most of it. And if you can find it in the rest of your life, make the most of it. Charlie?

**Charlie Munger**（01:49:08）：Well, when you found Ben Graham he was unconventional, and he was very smart. And of course, that was very attractive to you. And then when you found out it worked and you could make a lot of money while you're sitting on your ass — (laughter) — of course you were an instant convert. And so —

**Warren Buffett**（01:49:47）：It still appeals to me, actually. I mean — (Laughter)

**Charlie Munger**（01:49:53）：But the world changed. Before he died, Bill Graham — I mean, Ben Graham — recognized that the exact way he sought undervalued companies wouldn't necessarily work for all times under all conditions. And that's certainly the way it worked for us. We gradually morphed into trying to buy the better companies when they were underpriced, instead of the lousy companies when they were underpriced. And of course, that worked pretty well for us. And Ben Graham, he outlived the game that he played personally most of the time. He lived to see most of it fade away. I mean, just to find some company that's selling for one third of its working capital, and figure out it could easily be liquidated and distribute $3 for every dollar of market price. Lots of luck if you can find those in the present market. And if you can find them, they're so small that Berkshire wouldn't find them of any use anyway. So we've had to learn a different game. **Warren Buffett**（01:50:58）：Yeah.

**Charlie Munger**（01:50:59）：What you formerly knew is never enough. So if you don't learn to constantly revise your earlier conclusions and get better (inaudible), you are — I always use the same metaphor. You're like a one-legged man in an ass-kicking contest. (Laughter)

**Warren Buffett**（01:51:22）：If anybody has suggestions for another metaphor, send them to me. (Laughter) Graham, incidentally, one point, important point. Graham was not scalable. Now, you can make adjustments for inflation and everything. But it was just a tiny amount. It wasn't really scalable. And the truth is that Graham didn't care, because he really wasn't interested in making a lot of money for himself. So he had no reason to want to find something that could go on and on, become larger and larger.
