## 问题

加密货币市值接近伯克希尔和苹果。什么因素让您说它是泡沫？

## 回答要点

1. 非生产性资产——黄金从耶稣时代算复利只有百分之零点几
2. 「它自己产出什么？」——支票和电汇才是有用的发明
3. 芒格：「我对加密货币的厌恶比你还深——这就是痴呆。」
4. 「别人在交易大便，你决定不能错过。」（芒格）
5. 巴菲特：「希望有些话翻译得不要太好。」

## 关键引述

> **“it's just dementia”**
>
> 「这就是痴呆」

## 中文全文（逐段翻译）

**Warren Buffett**（00:52:00）：贝基？

**Becky Quick**（00:52:06）：这个问题来自乌克兰的弗拉德·科普捷夫（音）。他说：「加密货币的市值去年已经接近伯克希尔和苹果。加密货币背后的理念显然会影响伯克希尔持股的传统银行。您总说您没有深入了解加密货币。是什么因素让您说它是泡沫？」

**Warren Buffett**（00:52:37）：总的来说，非生产性资产就是另一种东西。如果你在耶稣诞生那年买下黄金，算一下复合收益率，大概是百分之零点几。它本质上不会带来任何东西，除了所谓的稀缺性——因为能挖出来的就那么多。可那又怎样？它自己产出什么？

支票是了不起的发明。想象一下，如果没有支票、没有电汇，世界会怎样。但这并不意味着支票本身就值很多钱。如果你说不能用一个叫「支票」的小纸片，人们会想出别的办法转账。

我认为，你买任何非生产性资产时，都是在指望以后有人从你手里买走它，因为那个人认为可以再卖更高的价。郁金香试过，历史上各种东西都试过。结局都不好。

拿土地说，你很难讲清楚——路易斯安那购地大概花了 1,500 万美元，买下 80 万平方英里左右的土地。其实你就坐在当年那笔购地涵盖的土地上。我们付了多少？每平方英里 20 美元，一平方英里 640 英亩，算下来是 3 美分左右。这笔「非生产性资产」买得相当不错。但这要靠判断，而且非常难。

你也可以买邮票。比尔·格罗斯收藏了一整套极好的邮票，最后卖了很多钱。但那取决于别人想要买、希望之后再卖更高价，如此而已。

归根结底，你赚钱靠的是生产性资产。如果你买一座农场，你会估算每亩能产多少大豆或玉米、要付给代耕的农民多少钱、税负多少等等，然后根据这块资产长期能产出什么来做结论。那才叫投资。

当你买一样东西，只是因为希望明天早上醒来价格更高，那你唯一需要的就是进来的人比离开的人多。这能成立，会自我强化一段时间，有时很久，有时到惊人的数字。但最终——结局都不好。加密货币也会以糟糕的结局收场。

除了这项资产本身不产生任何价值之外，还有一个问题：它会吸引大量江湖骗子，去搭建各种交易所之类的东西。品格不怎么样的人在这里看到了机会，去收割那些想变富的人——因为邻居买这些东西发了财，而两人其实都不懂。它会有糟糕的结局。查理？

**Charlie Munger**（00:56:20）：嗯，我对加密货币的厌恶比你还深。（笑）对我来说这就是痴呆。我认为那些专业交易员去做加密货币交易，令人作呕。就像别人在交易大便，而你决定「我不能错过。」（笑·掌声）

**Warren Buffett**（00:56:51）：我们正在向全球网络直播，我希望有些话翻译得不要太好。（笑）

## 英文原文（CNBC 官方逐字稿）

**Warren Buffett**（00:52:00）：Becky?

**Becky Quick**（00:52:06）：This question comes from Vlad Koptev (PH) in Ukraine. He says, "Capitalization of cryptocurrency has approached that of Berkshire and Apple last year. And clearly the idea behind crypto will affect conventional banking groups where Berkshire is a shareholder. You always say you didn't go into too much detail to obtain an understanding on cryptocurrencies. So what factors caused you to say that it's a bubble?"

**Warren Buffett**（00:52:37）：Well, generally non-productive assets remain not only — if you'd bought gold at the time of Christ and you figured the compound rate on it, you know, it may be a couple tenths of 1 percent. It essentially is not going to deliver anything other than supposed scarcity, you know, because they'll only — you can only mine so many. But so what? I mean, what does it produce itself? You know, the check is a wonderful idea. Just imagine how the world would be without being able to write checks or have wire transfer of funds. But it doesn't make the check intrinsically itself worth a lot of money. And if you said you can't use something called "check" with a little piece of paper you'd do something else to transfer money. I think that anytime you buy a nonproductive asset you are counting on somebody else later on to buy a nonproductive asset because they think they can sell it to somebody for more money. And it's been tried with tulips and it's been tried. It's been tried with various things over time. And it does come to a bad ending. I mean, having — you have a hard time. You can think of raw land. I mean, the Louisiana Purchase was, say, $15 million for 800,000 or so square miles of land. In fact, you're sitting on land that came with the Louisiana Purchase. And so what'd we pay? We paid 20 bucks a square mile. And, you know, 640 acres in a square mile. And you're down to three cents or something. So that was a pretty good purchase of what was then a nonproductive property. But it'd depend — but it's very hard. You can buy stamps. Bill Gross got, you know, collected a wonderful stamp collection. It sold for more money in the end. But it's dependent on somebody else wanting to buy, hoping they will sell it for more money and so on. And in the end you make your money on productive assets. If you buy a farm, you try to estimate what the crops, what amount per acre of soybeans or corn or whatever may be raised, and how much you have to pay the farmer that farms it for you, and what your taxes will be, and various things. And you make a conclusion based on what the asset itself will produce over time. And that's an investment. When you buy something because you're hoping tomorrow morning you're going to wake up, you know, and the price will be higher, the only reason, you know, you need more people coming into it than are leaving. And you can get that. And it will feed on itself for a while, and sometimes for a long while, and sometimes to extraordinary numbers. But in the end — but they come to bad endings. And cryptocurrencies will come to bad endings. And along with the fact that there's nothing being produced in the way of value from the asset that you also have the problem that it draws in a lot of charlatans and that sort of thing who are trying to create various sorts of exchanges or whatever it may be. You know, it's something where people who are of less than stellar character see an opportunity to clip people who are trying to get rich because their neighbor's getting rich buying this stuff that neither one of them understands. It will come to a bad ending. Charlie?

**Charlie Munger**（00:56:20）：Well, I like cryptocurrencies a lot less than you do. (Laughter) And so to me it's just dementia. And I think the people who are professional traders that go into trading cryptocurrencies, it's just disgusting. It's like somebody else is trading turds and you decide, "I can't be left out." (Laughter and applause)

**Warren Buffett**（00:56:51）：To the extent that this — we are being webcast around the world, I hope some of our stuff doesn't translate very well, actually. (Laughter)
