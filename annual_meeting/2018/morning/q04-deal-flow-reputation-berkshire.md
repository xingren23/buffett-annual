## 问题

股东问：与巴菲特和芒格做交易的信任和声望让伯克希尔拿到「老乡折扣」。是否应让更多管理者走向台前，让未来的企业家继续把交易机会带给伯克希尔？

## 回答要点

1. 巴菲特引托尼·奥赖利的玩笑：CEO 的首要任务是找到接班人然后开掉他
2. 伯克希尔作为企业好归宿的声誉「属于伯克希尔」，不依赖巴菲特或芒格
3. 子公司层面已在自行完成大量收购，甚至多于总部
4. 芒格：99% 的上市公司控制权变更通过投行拍卖完成，买家加满杠杆，资金来自慈善基金和养老基金，「迟早会出问题」
5. 一位企业主说：「不是因为你多了不起，而是因为你是唯一剩下的选择。」

## 关键引述

> **“the reputation belongs to Berkshire now.”**
>
> 「声誉现在属于伯克希尔。」

> **“you're the only one left”**
>
> 「你是唯一剩下的选择。」

**Warren Buffett**（00:34:59）：好，贝基·奎克。（掌声）

**Becky Quick**（00:35:09）：这个问题来自柯克·汤普森。他说：「沃伦，在今年的致股东信里，您提到廉价债务和其他公司愿意加杠杆，这些都是收购竞争加剧的例子。看起来，跟沃伦·巴菲特和查理·芒格做交易的信任和声望，让伯克希尔能拿到『老乡折扣』，胜过那些可能出更高价的买家。您有没有考虑让伯克希尔的其他管理者更多地走向台前，好让未来几代成功的企业家像过去几十年那样继续把交易机会带给伯克希尔？」

**Warren Buffett**（00:35:49）：这让我想起——是谁来着？托尼·奥赖利有一次谈到 CEO 的职责。他说 CEO 的第一要务就是在组织里找到那个有主动性、有头脑、有决心、具备一切接班素质的人，然后把这人开掉。（笑）毫无疑问。我认为伯克希尔作为企业好归宿的声誉——尤其是对私人企业——并不依赖于我或查理。接班人可能需要一小段考验期。但基本上，我们有钱做交易，以后也会有。人们能看到我们的子公司未来怎么运营。事实上，我觉得其他一些高管的知名度在上升。但我要告诉你，如果情况变得足够糟，你不用担心——不管怎样他们都会打电话来的。（笑）所以我不担心所谓的「交易流」——我讨厌这个词。我认为这依赖的是伯克希尔，不是我。而且说实话，我的电话也没被好交易打爆过。看来这个什么强大人格魅力没给你们带来什么。（笑）所以下一个人可能接到更多电话。声誉现在属于伯克希尔。对于那些在乎自己和父母、也许祖父母花了几十年心血打造的企业的人——如果他们出于某种原因不想或不能把企业留在家族内部，又在乎它最终的归宿——我们绝对是第一选择。无论接电话的是查理、我还是别人，我们都会继续是第一选择。查理？

**Charlie Munger**（00:38:41）：嗯，很多子公司长期以来一直在跟他们认识而我们不认识的人做各种收购。这已经在发生了。事实上，在总部层面发生的还没那么多——

**Warren Buffett**（00:39:00）：别告诉他们，查理。

**Charlie Munger**（00:39:02）：你如愿了。（笑）奇怪的是，大约 99% 控制权易手的上市公司，都是在投资银行主持的拍卖中转手的。买家通常加满杠杆，等业绩稍有起色就再加一轮杠杆。而这些钱来自慈善捐赠基金和养老基金，它们以极高价格对这些易手公司做高杠杆投资。长此以往，这不可能不出问题。

**Warren Buffett**（00:39:43）：对。

**Charlie Munger**（00:39:44）：会出一次不愉快的事。我认为到那时我们还在，而且状况不错。

**Warren Buffett**（00:39:51）：多年前有个人来找我。他有一家很好的企业。他一直忧心忡忡，因为他看到一个朋友去世后，管理者们某种程度上试图占遗孀的便宜，结果一团糟。他说前一年想了很多，决定不把企业卖给竞争对手——那是最自然的买家——因为他们会裁掉他所有的人。留下的 CFO、上上下下都会变成收购方的人。他不想这样对他的员工。然后他又想，也不想卖给私募股权，因为他觉得他们会加满杠杆——他从来不喜欢那么多杠杆——然后再转手卖掉，完全脱离他的掌控。他想自己继续经营。所以他说：「沃伦，不是因为你多了不起，而是因为你是唯一剩下的选择。」（笑）在很多情况下，伯克希尔将继续是那个唯一的选择。

## 英文原文（CNBC 官方逐字稿）

**Warren Buffett**（00:34:59）：OK, Becky Quick. (Applause)

**Becky Quick**（00:35:09）：This question comes from Kirk Thompson. He says, "Warren, in this year's annual letter to shareholders, you referenced both cheap debt and a willingness by other companies to leverage themselves as competitive examples as to why it's hard to get more acquisition deals done. "It seems like the trust in — and prestige of doing a deal with Warren Buffett and Charlie Munger allow Berkshire to get a hometown discount and beat out other firms that might pay a little more to a prospective seller. "Have you given thought to having other Berkshire managers have more public exposure, so future generations of successful business owners continue to bring deal opportunities to Berkshire like they have in prior decades?"

**Warren Buffett**（00:35:49）：Yeah, that sort of reminds me of — who was it? Tony O'Reilly remarked one time about the responsibility of a CEO. That the very first job of the CEO was to search through his organization and find that person who had the initiative and the brains, the determination, all of the qualities to be his logical successor, and then fire the guy. (Laughter) The — there's no question. I think the reputation of Berkshire as being a very good home for companies — particularly private companies — but a good home for companies, I don't think that reputation is dependent on me or Charlie. It may take a little, you know, there'll be a little testing period for whoever takes over, in that respect. But, you know, basically we've got the money to do the deals. We'll have the money to do the deals subsequently. People can see how our subsidiaries operate in the future. And the truth is that, I think some of the other executives are going — are getting better known. But there will be a — you know, I'll tell you this, if things get bad enough, you don't have to worry. They'll be calling us no matter what. (Laughs) So I do not worry about the so-called "deal flow," which is a term I hate. But I don’t think there’s — I think that's dependent on Berkshire and not dependent on me. And, you know, as I've mentioned, my phone isn't ringing off the hook with good deals. So apparently this big winning personality or something is not delivering for you. (Laughter) So it may be the next person will be even more — get even more calls. Berkshire — the reputation belongs to Berkshire now. And we are, for somebody that cares about a business that they and their parents and maybe their grandparents lovingly built over decades — if they care about where that business ends up being after, for one reason or another, they don't want to keep it or can't keep it in the family, we absolutely are the first call. And we will continue to be the first call, whether Charlie or I answer the phone or somebody else does. Charlie?

**Charlie Munger**（00:38:41）：Well, a lot of the subsidiaries have for a long time already been making all kinds of acquisitions with people they know and we don't. So it's already happening. And, in fact, it's happening more there than it is at headquarters, so —

**Warren Buffett**（00:39:00）：Don't tell them, Charlie.

**Charlie Munger**（00:39:02）：You're getting your wish. (Laughter) And it is weird that about 99 percent of the public companies that change hands, in terms of control, change hands in a sort of auction presided over by an investment banker. And the people that buy are usually just leverage it to the gills, and when it starts doing a little better, they re-leverage it. And that money is coming out of the charitable endowments and pension plans who are making these highly-leveraged investments in all these companies changing hands at very high prices. Sooner or later, this is not going to work perfectly.

**Warren Buffett**（00:39:43）：Yeah.

**Charlie Munger**（00:39:44）：And it's going to have an unpleasant episode. And I think we'll be around and in good shape at that time.

**Warren Buffett**（00:39:51）：There was one fellow who came to me many years ago. And he had a wonderful business. And he had been worried because he had seen a friend of his die. And the problems that arose later when the managers, to some extent, tried to take advantage of the widow. And it became a disaster. So he said he thought about it a lot the previous year. And he decided he didn't want to sell the business to a competitor, who would be a logical buyer, because they would fire all of his people. And the CFO that would remain, and, you know, all up and down the line, they'd all be the acquirer's people. He didn't want to do that to his people. And then he thought, and he didn't want to sell it to a private equity firm, because he thought they'd leverage it up. He never liked to leverage that much, and then they'd just resell it later on to somebody, so it would be totally out of control of what he wanted to do. And he wanted to keep running it himself. So he said, "Warren," he said, "It isn't that you're such a great guy," he says, "It's you're the only one left." So — (Laughter) — Berkshire will continue to be the only one left in many cases.
