## 问题

致股东信提到 4000 亿美元巨灾的可能性。网络风险量级相当，可能波及多个行业。伯克希尔如何思考和防范网络「大事件」？

## 回答要点

1. 巴菲特将网络风险纳入 4000 亿美元超级巨灾的 2% 年概率估算中
2. 「我们或其他任何人在承保网络险时都不知道自己在干什么」——领域太早期
3. 攻击方始终领先于防守方；不做网络险敞口的前三名
4. 巨灾或达 4000 亿时伯克希尔敞口不超 3%；120 亿损失下营业利润仍可能为正
5. 芒格：不允许计算机做自动大额证券交易；大股东全部身家押一只股票，比人员流动频繁的公司管得更精心

## 关键引述

> **“We don't know what we're doing in cyber”**
>
> 「网络险我们不知道自己在干什么」

> **“we don't want to be a pioneer on this”**
>
> 「我们不想在这个领域当先锋。」

## 中文全文（逐段翻译）

> 与下方「英文原文」逐段对应，按发言顺序排列；术语与专名首次出现时保留英文。

**Warren Buffett**（00:41:11）：加里·兰塞姆。

**Gary Ransom**（00:41:14）：早上好。沃伦，您在致股东信里提到过一次 4000 亿美元巨灾的可能性——那是损失分布尾部的事件。我能想到另一个量级相当的风险，那就是网络风险。我相信您的管理者们都做了防范，但在网络风险分布的尾部，一次事件可能波及很多行业、很多伯克希尔的公司。您怎么思考和防范网络领域的『大事件』？

**Warren Buffett**（00:41:55）：对。顺便说一下，我在年报里写的那段——关于大约——没人知道确切答案。我可以填个数字，保险界比我聪明的人会填另一个数字。但我认为发生我所说的 4000 亿美元超级巨灾的概率大约是每年 2%。而网络风险就在这个等式里。不只是地震之类。坦率地说，我认为我们或其他任何人在承保网络险时都不知道自己在干什么。这个领域实在太早期了。我们不知道保单条款会怎么解释，不知道哪些事件会形成关联——我们现在不觉得它们关联，或者还没想到。每年我去听 CIA 或其他机构的人来讲，他们都说攻击方领先于防守方，而且会一直如此。我能设想很多网络事件，但不会在这里细说，因为心思歪的人可能——他们想的招可能比我多得多——我不想喂他们灵感。但对于加州地震的概率、三级或四级飓风袭击佛罗里达的概率之类，我们有相当好的把握。网络险我们不知道自己在干什么，而且不想当先锋。我们在伯克希尔·哈撒韦专业保险（Berkshire Hathaway Specialty）做一些这方面的业务。如果是出于竞争原因做某件事——我可以接受——但如果人们告诉我这是竞争必需品，我们就不想在风险敞口上排第一、第二或第三。我相信我们在网络险上不是。但任何现在告诉你他们用精算方法知道未来一般经验会怎样、或者最坏情况会怎样的人，我认为都是在骗自己。这也是我说 4000 亿美元事件大约有 2% 年概率的原因之一。网络是未知领域，而且会越来越糟，不会越来越好。问题在于，如果外面有一大堆 250 亿美元的商业保额，是否存在我们没预见到的累积效应，或者法院对保单的解释不同——法院通常会倾向于保护被保险人。所以你指出这是一个非常重大的风险，10 年、15 年前不存在，而且今后会愈演愈烈。我只能告诉你加里，它在我的 4000 亿和 2% 里。如果你有不同猜测，你的和我的一样可能对。查理？

**Charlie Munger**（00:46:00）：是的，跟网络风险很像的是，你有计算机程序帮你做证券交易，计算机因为某个错误发了疯。这种事至少已经发生过一次——有人早上还好好的，到下午就破产了，因为计算机出了问题。我们不允许任何计算机做大额自动证券交易。总体来说，伯克希尔比大多数地方更不容易犯那种愚蠢的疏忽。

**Warren Buffett**（00:46:38）：我确实认为，如果网络出现巨灾——比如 4000 亿——我不认为我们的敞口会超过 3%——

**Charlie Munger**（00:46:43）：不，不——

**Warren Buffett**（00:46:44）：——敞口。

**Charlie Munger**（00:46:45）：不，不，我们该摊的份额还是会摊到。

**Warren Buffett**（00:46:53）：但它——你知道——会毁掉很多公司。如果真有 120 亿美元的损失，我觉得——撇开新会计准则不谈——从我所说的营业利润来看，我们那年大概仍然会有合理利润。我们在应对真正超级巨灾的能力上，跟我所知的世界上任何一家保险公司都不一样。好，2 号站台的股东。

**Charlie Munger**（00:47:26）：我想指出，我右边这位大股东几乎全部身家都在一只股票上。那大概比某些人员流动频繁的上市公司管得更精心。

**Warren Buffett**（00:47:40）：对，你不会想要一个 64 岁、65 岁就要退休的人。很多决策你真的不想让他或她来做。（笑）

## 英文原文（CNBC 官方逐字稿）

**Warren Buffett**（00:41:11）：Gary Ransom.

**Gary Ransom**（00:41:14）：Good morning. Warren, in your annual letter, you wrote about a potential for a $400 billion natural catastrophe event, something out in the tail of the loss distribution. I can think of another risk that could have a similar order of magnitude, and that would be cyberrisk. I'm sure all your managers have taken steps against that potential, but in — out in the tail of the cyberrisk distribution, it could hit a lot of industries, a lot of your companies. So how do you think about and prepare for the big one in cyber?

**Warren Buffett**（00:41:55）：Yeah. Well, I include, incidentally, in my — that part I wrote in the annual report where I said that roughly — nobody knows the answer on this. I mean, I could stick down two, and somebody else much smarter in insurance would stick down a different figure. But I think it's about a 2 percent risk of what I call a 400 billion super-cat of all time. And — But cyber is in that equation. I mean, that's not just earthquakes and that sort of thing. And frankly, I don't think we, or anybody else, really knows what they're doing when writing cyber. I mean, we — it is just very, very, very early in the game. And we don't know what the interpretations of the policies, necessarily, will be. We don't know the degree to which they'll be what — there'll be correlated incidents, which we don't really think are correlated now or haven't had the imagination to come up with. We know that every year when I go and hear these people from the CIA or wherever it may be, they tell me that the offense is ahead of the defense, and will continue that way. And I can dream of a lot of cyber incidents, which I'm not going to spell out here, because people that have twisted minds may be — they've probably got more — way more — ideas than I've got, but I don't believe in feeding them any. But it's a business where we don’t — we have a pretty good idea of the probabilities of a quake in California, or the probabilities of a three or a four hurricane hitting Florida, or whatever it may be. We don't know what we're doing in cyber, and we try to keep — we don't want to be a pioneer on this. We do some business in that arena in Berkshire Hathaway Specialty. But if you're doing something for competitive reasons — which I'm OK with — but when I'm doing something where I — that people tell me is a competitive necessity, we are going to try not to have — we don't want to be number one or number two or number three in exposures on it. And I don’t — and I am sure we are not in cyber. But I don’t — I think anybody that tells you now that they think they know in some actuarial way, either what general experience is likely to be in the future, or what the worst case would be, I think, is kidding themselves. And that's one of the reasons that I say that a $400 billion event has a — I think has roughly a 2 percent probability per year of happening. Cyber's uncharted territory, and it's going to get worse, not better. And then the question is whether, if we have a whole bunch of $25 billion commercial limits out there, whether there's some aggregation that we didn't foresee or that the courts interpret those policies differently, then you know — they are generally going to give the benefit of the doubt to the insured. So you're right in pointing that out as a very material risk, which didn't exist 10 or 15 years ago and that — and will be much more intense as the years go along. And all I can tell you, Gary, is that, that's part of my 400 billion and my 2 percent. But if you've got a different guess, it's just as likely that yours is right than mine on that. Charlie?

**Charlie Munger**（00:46:00）：Yeah, well, something that's very much like cyberrisk is, you've got computers programmed to do your security trading and your computer goes a little wild from some error. And that's already happened at least once where somebody just was fine one morning and by the afternoon they were broke because some computer went crazy. We don't have any computers we allot — we allow to do big, automatically trading securities. I think, generally, Berkshire is less likely than most other places to be careless in some really stupid way.

**Warren Buffett**（00:46:38）：I do think if there's a mega-cat from cyber, and let's say it hits 400 billion, I do not think we'll have more than a 3 percent —

**Charlie Munger**（00:46:43）：No, no —

**Warren Buffett**（00:46:44）：— exposure.

**Charlie Munger**（00:46:45）：No, no, we'll get our share.

**Warren Buffett**（00:46:53）：And but it, you know, it will destroy — what will destroy a lot of companies — that we will actually, if we had a $12 billion loss, I would think, except for the new accounting rule, but I believe from what I call operating earnings, we would probably still have a reasonable profit that year. I mean, we are in a different position than any insurance company I know of in the world, in our ability to handle the really — really super, super-cat. OK, shareholder from station 2.

**Charlie Munger**（00:47:26）：May I point out that the main shareholder to my right here has almost all his net worth in one security. That's likely to be more carefully managed than some public place with people just passing through.

**Warren Buffett**（00:47:40）：Yeah, you don't want a guy that's 64 and is going to retire at 65. And a lot of decisions you really don't want him or her to be making. (Laughter)
