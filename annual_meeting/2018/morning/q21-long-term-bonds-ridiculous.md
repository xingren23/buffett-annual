## 问题

国债供应增加将如何影响收益率和利率？

## 回答要点

1. 「不知道。好消息是其他人也不知道。」
2. 长期债券在当前利率下是糟糕投资；等待配置的资金全在短期国库券
3. 超长期债券收益率仅比 3% 略高，而美联储目标通胀 2%——「几乎荒谬」
4. 芒格：降息对储蓄老人不公平，但对在座各位极有利——推高了资产价格
5. 1942 年战争债券 18.75 换 25、年化 2.9%——11 岁的孩子也知道不是好投资

## 关键引述

> **“long-term bonds are a terrible investment”**
>
> 「长期债券是糟糕的投资」

## 中文全文（逐段翻译）

> 与下方「英文原文」逐段对应，按发言顺序排列；术语与专名首次出现时保留英文。

**Warren Buffett**（02:00:07）：好，7 号站台。

**Audience Member**（02:00:10）：早上好。我有一个关于债券市场——美国国债市场——的问题。我叫奥拉·拉尔森（音），住在旧金山湾区。我从未在金融行业工作过。最初在温哥华证券交易所买几分钱的矿业股。几十年后结了婚，妻子说服我买了伯克希尔的股票。那大概是个好决定。（笑）我的问题是，我读到关于美联储和通胀数据的新闻，国债供应量应该会增加、要拍卖。这会如何影响收益率或利率？

**Warren Buffett**（02:01:17）：是的。答案是：我不知道。好消息是，别人也不知道，包括美联储的人，所有人——这里变量太多。

有一件事我们确实知道：我们认为长期债券是糟糕的投资，在当前利率或接近当前利率的水平上都是。所以我们钱袋里等着部署的资金，基本都放在国库券里，平均到期期限大概四个月，最多如此。

这些国库券的利率最近涨了，所以我猜 2018 年我们的税前收益会比去年拿国库券时至少多 5 亿美元。但这不是因为我们想持有它们，我们在等别的机会。

长期债券在这些利率水平上，想想几乎荒谬。因为美联储明确告诉你，它要每年 2% 的通胀；而超长期债券的收益率也就 3% 出头。当然，个人投资者还要缴税，扣掉所得税，税后回报可能降到 2.5%。也就是说，美联储在告诉你：它会尽一切力量，确保你的通胀调整后收益不超过 0.5%。在我看来这非常——我不至于回头去炒仙股——但我想我远远更愿意持有生产性企业，或其他某些生产性资产。

至于债券市场明年怎么走——市面上有数万亿美元在人们手里，他们都在猜哪个期限最值得持有。我们在这场游戏里没有任何优势可言。查理？

**Charlie Munger**（02:03:55）：嗯，货币当局把储蓄利率降到那么低——主要影响的是有储蓄账户的老人——确实不太公平。但为了对抗大衰退可能不得不这么做。但确实不公平。情况很奇怪。我一辈子只遇到过一次利率降得这么低、低了这么久。对很多人很不公平。但极大地造福了在座各位——它推高了资产价格，包括伯克希尔的股价。所以我们都是些不配的人（笑）我希望继续如此。（笑）

**Warren Buffett**（02:04:49）：1942 年这份报纸出版时，政府正在号召所有人的爱国心。我们小时候上学买储蓄邮票，先是叫「美国战争债券」，后来叫「美国国防债券」，再后来叫「美国储蓄债券」。（笑）当时叫战争债券。你付 18.75 美元，十年后拿回 25 美元。

我就是那时算明白的：十年赚这 4 美元，年复合是 2.9%。当时这得用小字印。连一个 11 岁的孩子都看得懂：十年 2.9% 的复合，不是什么好投资。但我们还是都买了，那基本上是战争努力的一部分。

而且政府知道——你也知道——从二战时期的财政状况看，显著的通胀要来了。我们其实在搞一场大规模的凯恩斯式行为，不是因为我们选择信奉凯恩斯，而是战争迫使我们背上巨额赤字，把债务推到 GDP 的 120%。那是有史以来最伟大的凯恩斯实验，我们是被动走进去的，而它把我们送上了一波从未见过的繁荣。所以有时会有意外的好处。

但美国政府当时是在请每个公民把钱投进一个十年期、2.9% 复合的固定美元投资。从那以后，我认为国债基本都不具吸引力（笑）除了 1980 年代初。那时候真的是机会：你可以买零息国债，等于给自己锁定 30 年、年化 14% 左右的复合回报。所以每隔一段时间，市场就会出现真正奇怪的事，诀窍是不仅要准备好，还要在它发生时出手。

查理，你买过战争债券吗？

**Charlie Munger**（02:07:20）：不。不。我从来没买过战争债券。

**Warren Buffett**（02:07:24）：不。以前好像带我去——

**Charlie Munger**（02:07:25）：打仗的时候我根本没钱。（笑）

**Warren Buffett**（02:07:27）：那是不买的好理由。（笑）

**Audience Member**（02:00:10）：Good morning. And I have a question related to the bond market — U.S. Treasury bond market. And my name is Ola Larsson (PH). I live in the San Francisco bay area. And I never worked in the financial industry. I started out buying penny mining stocks on the Vancouver Stock Exchange. And then decades later, I got married. And my wife convinced me to buy Berkshire shares. That was probably a good decision. (Laughter) So my question is, I read the newspapers about the Federal Reserve and the inflation numbers. And there must be an increase supply of Treasury bonds that must go to auction. And my question is how would — what do you expect that to impact yield or interest rate?

**Warren Buffett**（02:01:17）：Yeah. The answer is, I don't know. And the good news is, nobody else knows, including members of the Federal Reserve and everyone — There are a lot of variables in the picture. And the one thing we know is we think that long-term bonds are a terrible investment, and we — at current rates or anything close to current rates. So basically all of our money that is waiting to be placed is in Treasury bills that, I think, have an average maturity of four months, or something like that, at most. The rates on those have gone up lately, so that in 2018, my guess is we'll have at least $500 million more of pretax income than we would've had in the bills last year. But they still — it's not because we want to hold them. We're waiting to do something else. But long-term bonds — they're basically, at these rates — it's almost ridiculous when you think about it. Because here the Federal Reserve Board is telling you we want 2 percent a year inflation. And the very long bond is not much more than 3 percent. And of course, if you're an individual, then you pay tax on it. You're going to have some income taxes to pay. And let's say it brings your after-tax return down to 2 1/2 percent. So the Federal Reserve is telling you that they're going to do whatever's in their power to make sure that you don't get more than a half a percent a year of inflation-adjusted income. And that seems to me, a very — I wouldn't go back to penny stocks — but I think I would stick with productive businesses, or productive — certain other productive assets — by far. But what the bond market does in the next year, you know — you've got trillions of dollars in the hands of people that are trying to guess which maturity would be the best to own and all that sort of thing. And we do not bring anything to that game that would allow us to think that we've got an edge. Charlie?

**Charlie Munger**（02:03:55）：Well, it really wasn't fair for our monetary authorities to reduce the savings rates, paid mostly to our old people with savings accounts, as much as they did. But they probably had to do it to fight the Great Recession, appropriately. But it clearly wasn't fair. And the conditions were weird. In my whole lifetime, it's only happened once that interest rates went down so low and stayed low for a long time. And it was quite unfair to a lot of people. And it benefited the people in this room enormously because it drove asset prices up, including the price of Berkshire Hathaway stock. So we're all a bunch of undeserving people — (laughter) — and I hope that we continue to be so. (Laughter)

**Warren Buffett**（02:04:49）：At the time this newspaper came out in 1942, it was — the government was appealing to the patriotism of everybody. As kids, we went to school and we bought Savings Stamps to put in — well, they first called them U.S. War Bonds, then they called them U.S. Defense Bonds, then they called them U.S. Savings Bonds. (Laughs) But they were called war bonds then. And you put up $18.75 and you got back $25 in ten years. And that's when I learned that that $4 for three — in ten years — was 2.9 percent compounded. They had to put it in small print then. And even an 11-year-old could understand that 2.9 percent compounded for ten years was not a good investment. But we all bought them. It was — you know, it was part of the war effort, basically. And the government knew — I mean, you knew that significant inflation was coming from what was taking place in finance, in World War II. We actually were on a massive Keynesian-type behavior, not because we elected to follow Keynes, but because war forced us to have this huge deficit in our finances, which took our debt up to 120 percent of GDP. And it was the great Keynesian experiment of all time, and we backed into it, and it sent us on a wave of prosperity like we've never seen. So you get some accidental benefits sometimes. But the United States government (inaudible) every citizen to put their money into a fixed-dollar investment at 2.9 percent compounded for ten years. And I think Treasury bonds have been unattractive ever since — (laughs) — with the exception of the early '80s. That was something at that time. I mean, you really had a chance to buy — you had a chance to invest your money by buying zero-coupon Treasury bonds, and in effect, guarantee yourself that for 30 years you would get a compounded return, you know, something like 14 percent for 30 years of your lifetime. So every now and then, something really strange happens in markets and the trick is to not only be prepared but to take action when it happens. Charlie, did you ever buy any war bonds?

**Charlie Munger**（02:07:20）：No. No. I never bought war bonds.

**Warren Buffett**（02:07:24）：No. Used to be like take me —

**Charlie Munger**（02:07:25）：I didn't have any money when I was in the war. (Laughter)
