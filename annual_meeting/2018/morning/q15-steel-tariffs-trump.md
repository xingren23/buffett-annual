## 问题

特朗普的关税会如何影响伯克希尔的制造业？

## 回答要点

1. 钢材成本有所上升，但不认为任何一国会陷入真正的贸易战
2. 芒格：「即使是特朗普，有些事情也是对的」——钢铁行业当时处境极其艰难
3. 贸易好处不可见、代价极显性——「路杀」人群需要政策照顾
4. 总统需要当好「首席教育者」，像罗斯福的炉边谈话那样解释贸易
5. 缅因州鞋业、新贝德福德纺织、扬斯敦炼钢——自由贸易对个体的代价真实而痛苦

## 关键引述

> **“even Donald Trump can be right on some of this stuff”**
>
> 「即使是特朗普，有些事情也是对的。」

**Warren Buffett**（01:34:36）：好，5 号站台。

**Audience Member**（01:34:42）：早上好，巴菲特先生和芒格先生。我是来自内布拉斯加州奥马哈的伊桑·穆波萨（音）。我的问题是，特朗普的关税会如何影响伯克希尔的制造业？

**Warren Buffett**（01:35:20）：到目前为止——（掌声）——钢材成本有所上升。但如我之前所说，美国和中国——会有来回博弈，有些事会让一些人不高兴，但我不认为任何一国会陷入引发和持续真正贸易战的局面。我们过去经历过几次，也吸取了教训。我们的贸易政策会惹恼别人，别人的也会惹恼我们，会有一来一回。但最终不会出一个糟糕的结果。查理——

**Charlie Munger**（01:36:21）：嗯，钢材——情况一度对美国钢铁行业来说几乎难以置信地不利。你知道，即使是特朗普，有些事情也是对的。（笑·掌声）

**Warren Buffett**（01:36:48）：关于贸易——我一直说总统需要当好「首席教育者」，就像罗斯福在大萧条时期那样。所以才有了炉边谈话，他向人民传达需要做什么、周围发生了什么，这非常重要。贸易尤其困难，因为贸易的好处基本是看不见的。你不知道如果规定所有衣服必须在美国制造，你今天穿的衣服会贵多少，电视机会贵多少。没有人每天买东西时会想到这些好处。而坏处——确实有——非常明显、非常痛苦。如果你被裁员了——像我们在缅因州的鞋业（Dexter Shoes）那样——你是一个非常好的工人，为你做的事感到骄傲，也许你父母也做过这一行，突然之间你发现美国制造的鞋子跟国外的比没有竞争力。你可以大谈亚当·斯密或大卫·李嘉图，解释自由贸易和比较优势的好处，但那毫无意义。如果你已经 55、60 岁了，谈再培训什么的——那又怎样？所以，在政治上，隐性的好处和对部分选民来说非常显性的代价，是很难卖的。你需要做两件事。你知道什么对国家好。所以你必须善于解释，这对在纺织厂工作的人是真真切切的伤害——比如我们在新贝德福德的工厂，一半工人只说葡萄牙语。突然他们就没工作了。他们干了好多年、干得很好。你得做两件事。第一，你得理解这就是个人为集体利益付出的代价。第二，你得照顾那些因年龄等原因再培训等于笑话的人。你得照顾那些在对国家整体有利的事情中沦为「路杀」的人。这需要社会通过代表来制定政策，既取得正确的集体成果，又不至于在过程中在经济上毁掉太多人。这些年我们在不同领域做过这样的事。有生产力的人帮助照顾太老和太小的。每次美国出生一个婴儿，我们就承担了 12 年的教育义务。现在要花 15 万美元。我们有一个把壮年人和老幼联系在一起的体系。它在变好，远非完美，但在变好。我相信贸易，如果解释得当、并有照顾「路杀」人群的政策，对国家是有利的，也是可以解释清楚的。但对在缅因州德克斯特做鞋、在新贝德福德操作织机、在俄亥俄州扬斯敦炼钢的人来说，这很难——非常非常难卖。（掌声）

## 英文原文（CNBC 官方逐字稿）

**Warren Buffett**（01:34:36）：OK. Station five.

**Audience Member**（01:34:42）：Good morning, Warren Buffett and Charlie Munger. My name is Ethan Mupposa (PH), and I am from Omaha, Nebraska. My question is, how will Donald Trump's tariffs affect the manufacturing business of Berkshire Hathaway?

**Warren Buffett**（01:35:20）：Well, to date — (applause) — steel costs — we've seen steel costs increase somewhat. But as I said earlier, I don't think the United States or China — there'll be some jockeying back and forth, and there will be something that leaves some people unhappy and — but I don't think — I don't think either country will dig themselves into something that precipitates and continues any kind of real trade war in this country. We — we've had that in the past a few times. And I think we've learned a general lesson on it. But there will — there will be some things about our trade policies that irritate others. And there will be some from others that irritate us. And there will be some back and forth. But in the end, I don't think we'll come out with a terrible answer on it. Charlie, I'll let you —

**Charlie Munger**（01:36:21）：Well, steel has — it reached — the conditions in steel were almost unbelievably adverse to the American steel industry. You know, even Donald Trump can be right on some of this stuff. (Laughter and applause)

**Warren Buffett**（01:36:48）：The — the thing about trade — you know, I've always said that the president, whether it's president — any president — needs to be an educator-in-chief, which [Franklin] Roosevelt was in the Depression. That's why he had those Fireside Chats, and it was very important that he communicated to the people what needed to be done and what was happening around them, and — Trade is particularly difficult, because the benefits of trade are basically not visible, you know. You don't know what you would be paying for the clothes you're wearing today if we'd had a rule they all had to be manufactured in the United States, or what you'd be paying for your television set, or whatever it may be. No one thinks about the benefits day-by-day as they walk around buying things and carrying on their own business. The negatives, and there are negatives, are very apparent and very painful. And if you're laid off — like happened in our shoe business [Dexter Shoes] in Maine — and you know you are — been a very, very, very good worker, and you were proud of what you did, and maybe your parents did it before you, and all of a sudden you find out that American shoes — shoes manufactured in America — are not competitive with shoes made outside the United States. You know, you can talk all you want about Adam Smith or David Ricardo or something and explain the benefits of free trade and comparative advantage and all that sort of thing, and that doesn't make any difference. And if you're 55 or 60 years old, to talk about retraining or something like that, you know, so what? So, I — it is tough in politics where you have a hidden benefit and a very visible cost to a certain percentage of a — of your constituency. And you need to do two things under those circumstances, if you have that situation. You know what's good for the country. So, you have to be very good at explaining how it does really hurt, in a real way, somebody that works in a textile mill, like we had in New Bedford, where you only spoke Portuguese — half our workers only spoke Portuguese. And suddenly, they have no job. And they've been doing their job well for years. You've got to do two things. You can — you'll have to — you have to understand that that's the price individuals pay for what's good for the collective good. And secondly, you've got to take care of the people that are — that — where retraining is a joke because of their age, or whatever it may be. And you've got to take care of the people that become the roadkill in something that is collectively good for us as a country. And — That takes society acting through its representatives to develop the policies that will get us the right collective result, and not kill too many people economically in the process. And you know, we've done that in various arenas over the years. The people in their productive years do help take care of the people that are too old, and too young. I mean, every time a baby is born in the United States, you know, we take on an obligation of educating them for 12 years. It'll cost $150,000 now, you know? It — We have a system that has a bond between the people in their productive years and the ones in the young and old. And it gets better over time. It's far from perfect now. But it has gotten better over time. And I believe that trade, properly explained, and with policies that take care of the people that are roadkill, is good for our country and can be explained. But I think it's a tough — it's been a tough, tough sell to a guy that made shoes in Dexter, Maine or worked on a loom in New Bedford, Mass, or works in the steel mill in Youngstown, Ohio. (Applause)
