## 问题

与亚马逊和摩根大通合作做医疗后，如何用认知偏差理论理解互联网公司？如何判断是否真正理解一门生意？

## 回答要点

1. 1960 年美国医疗占 GDP 5%，如今近 18%；人均支出从 170 美元涨到 1 万多
2. 医疗成本是「美国企业竞争力的绦虫」；人均医生/病床/护士反而少于支出低的国家
3. 3.3 万亿美元流向千千万万参与者，「每一块钱都有一个利益群体」
4. 不为利润，目标是让员工以更低成本获得更好医疗；CEO 人选是关键
5. 洛克菲勒用私人资金大幅改善美国医疗——「我在三个方面模仿他」

## 关键引述

> **“It is a tapeworm, in terms of American business and its competitiveness.”**
>
> 「就美国企业竞争力而言，这是一条绦虫。」

## 中文全文（逐段翻译）

> 与下方「英文原文」逐段对应，按发言顺序排列；术语与专名首次出现时保留英文。

**Warren Buffett**（01:18:53）：好。（笑）4 号站台。

**Audience Member**（01:19:00）：早上好，查理和沃伦。我知道这个顺序有点奇怪，但我是你们的忠实粉丝——主要是查理的 25 个认知偏差。我来自华盛顿州西雅图，经营一家一人数字营销公司，专做 Facebook 广告和邮件营销。我经常用这些。你对可口可乐的分析非常扎实，我用它来理解客户产品的机制和推广方式。所以我相当确定你的认知偏差理论适用于互联网公司。现在你们跟亚马逊和摩根大通合作做医疗，我好奇你们有没有开始理解怎么把这些偏差应用到互联网公司？或者你们有另一套工具来判断是否真正理解一门生意？因为你们常说不投资自己不理解的企业。

**Warren Buffett**（01:20:01）：嗯，医疗是一个——我们不打算开医疗公司，也不一定要做保险什么的。我们只是有三家组织、三位我钦佩和信任的领导者。三方彼此信任。我们希望能做点什么——查理大概会说这几乎不可能——来改变一个 1960 年占 GDP 5%、如今接近 18% 的体系。美国企业的医疗成本与世界上任何国家相比都高得离谱。我们 5% 的时候有些国家也在 5% 左右，但我们涨到了 18%，他们没有超过 11%。1960 年美国人均医疗支出 170 美元，现在超过 1 万美元。一美元只有 100 美分。所以有成本问题。就美国企业竞争力而言，这是一条绦虫。我们的人均医生数更少、人均病床更少、人均护士也比一些支出远低于我们的国家少。这个体系输送着 3.3 万亿美元——几乎等于联邦政府的全部收入——流入千千万万参与其中的人。每一块钱都有一个利益群体。就像政治一样。我们能不能找到 CEO——正在找，应该不久能宣布——这是关键。那个人是否有想象力和支持力、能否在一个大家都同意成本失控但都认为是别人错的体系里做出实质改善——我们拭目以待。不容易。但我们的动机不是利润。我们想让员工以更低成本获得更好的医疗服务。我们不会推出让他们获得的服务不如现状的方案。但我们认为可能有办法做出真正有意义的改变。阻力会大得难以想象。如果失败了，至少我们试过了。不是说我读几本医学杂志就能做出什么突破（笑）——去改变一个如此根深蒂固的体系。而是这三家组织——雇了超过 100 万人——宣布之后我们收到大量想加入的电话，但现在还没有东西可以加入。如果我们想出有用的东西，他们会加入。调动资源、找到人。CEO 非常关键。然后支持那个人。想办法让美国人继续获得更好的医疗服务，而那个 18% 不至于在我们有生之年变成 20% 或 22%——因为一美元只有 100 美分。我们拭目以待。如果你是阿吉特做精算，你不会赌我们赢。但我认为我们有机会做点什么。有机会——没人能量化——做出实质性的改变。我们比大多数人的位置更好。我们有合适的伙伴。所以我们会试一试。查理？（掌声）

**Charlie Munger**（01:25:38）：这个公共服务领域有成功的先例。几十年前，约翰·D·洛克菲勒用自己的钱极大地改善了美国医疗。规模惊人。事实上从那以后没有任何个人做出过能与之相比的改善。所以沃伦在某方面模仿了洛克菲勒，现在换一种方式再试。也许会成功。

**Warren Buffett**（01:26:09）：顺便说一下，洛克菲勒活得非常长。所以我其实在三个方面模仿他。（笑）我们看看会怎样。但我们——进展很大。我想几个月内大概能找到 CEO。但如果没有合适的人，我们不会为了赶进度随便选一个。我们有这么好的伙伴。我们之间没有合伙协议。有人在法务部起草了一份，CEO 直接叫停了。资源是有的。虽然我们各有各的官僚主义，但如果有真正合理的事，我们可以穿透它。我们会得到支持——也会遇到很多阻力。但如果我们想出合理的东西，会得到很多美国企业的支持。如果容易的话，早就有人做了。这毫无疑问。

**Charlie Munger**（01:27:09）：不容易。

**Warren Buffett**（01:27:13）：对。（笑）但值得试。

## 英文原文（CNBC 官方逐字稿）

**Warren Buffett**（01:18:53）：OK. (Laughter) Station 4.

**Audience Member**（01:19:00）：Good morning, Charlie and Warren. I know that seems a little bit out of order, but I'm a huge fan of yours, Charlie, mostly for your 25 Cognitive Biases. I'm from Seattle, Washington. I run a one-person digital marketing firm that specializes in Facebook ads and email marketing. I use these a lot. I — your breakdown of Coca-Cola was really, really solid. And I use that as reference when looking to how to understand the mechanics of my clients' products and how to promote them. So I'm fairly certain that your cognitive biases work for internet-related companies. Now that you're partnering with Amazon [and JPMorgan] on health care, I'm curious, have you started to understand how to apply these biases to internet-related companies? Or is there another set of tools you use to decide if you understand a business? Because you guys talk a lot about not investing in businesses that you don't understand.

**Warren Buffett**（01:20:01）：Well, health care is a — we don't plan to start health care companies or, necessarily, insurers or anything. We simply have three organizations with leaders that I admire and trust. And we — mutually goes around all three. And we hope to do something which Charlie correctly would probably say is almost impossible to change in some way a system which is — was taking 5 percent of GDP in 1960, and now is taking close to 18 percent. And we have a hugely noncompetitive medical cost in American business, relating to any country in the world. The countries that — there were some countries that were around our 5 percent when we were at 5 percent. But we've managed to get to 18 without them going beyond 11 or so. Literally, in 1960, we were spending $170 per capita on medical costs in the United States. And now we're spending over 10,000. And, you know, every dollar only has a hundred cents. So there is a cost problem. It is a tapeworm, in terms of American business and its competitiveness. We don't — we have fewer doctors per capita. We have fewer hospital beds per capita, fewer nurses per capita, than some of the other countries that are well below us. And you've got a system that is delivering $3.3 trillion — that's almost as much as the federal government raises — it's delivering 3.3 trillion, or some number like that, to millions and millions and millions of people who are involved in the system. And every dollar has a constituency. It's just like politics. And whether we can find the chief executive, which we're working on now, and which I would expect we would — we would be able to announce before too long — that — but that's a key part of it. And whether that person will have the imagination and support of people that will enable us to make any kinds of significant improvements in a system which everybody agrees is sort of out of control on cost, but what — but — but they all think it's the other guy's fault, generally — we'll find out. It won't be — it won't be easy. But it is not a — the motivations are not primarily profit-making. They're — we want to deliver — we want our employees to get better medical service at a lower cost. We're not going to — we're certainly not going to come up with something where we think the service that they receive is inferior to what they're getting now. But we do think that there may be ways to make a real — significant changes — that could have an effect. And we know that the resistance will be unbelievable. And if we fail, we've at least tried. And — but they — the idea is not that I will be able to contribute anything to, you know, in some breakthrough moment, by reading a few medical journals or something — (laughs) — changing something that is as embedded as the medical system. But the idea is that maybe the three organizations, which employ over a million people and which, after we announced it, we had a flood of calls from people that wanted to join in, but there isn't anything to join into now. But they will if we have — come up with any ideas that are useful. Whether we can — bring the resources, bring the person. And the CEO is terribly important. And then bring the person, support that person. And somehow, figure out a better way for people to continue to receive better medical care in the United States without that 8 percent — 18 percent — going to 20 or 22 percent, you know, in the lifetime of, you know, our children or something of the sort — because there are only a hundred cents in the dollar. And we will see what happens. It's — you know — if you were Ajit [Jain], actuarially figuring, it would not — you would not bet on us. But — I think there is some chance we will do something. There's a chance — nobody can quantify it — that we can do something significant. And we are positioned better than most people to try. And we've certainly got the right partners. So, we will give it a shot and see what happens. Charlie? (Applause)

**Charlie Munger**（01:25:38）：There is some precedent for success in this public service activity. If you go back many decades, John B. Rockefeller I, using his own money, made an enormous improvement in American medical care. Perfectly enormous. In fact, there's never been any similar improvement done by any one man since that rivals it. So Warren, having imitated Rockefeller in one way, is just trying another. And maybe it'll work.

**Warren Buffett**（01:26:09）：Rockefeller, incidentally, lived a very long time. So I actually am trying to imitate him three ways there. (Laughter) We'll see what happens. But we are — we're making a lot of progress. And I think we'll probably have a CEO within a couple of months. But if we don't have one, then we're not going to pick somebody just because we want to meet any deadline or anything like that. We've got these wonderful partners. We don't have a partnership agreement among us. Somebody started drawing up one in a legal department and the CEO just put a stop to it. They — you do have places that have a lot of resources. And while we all have our share of bureaucracy, we can cut through it if we've got something that we really think makes sense. And we will get the support — we'll get — we'll get a lot of resistance, too. But we will get the support of a lot of American business, if we come up with something that makes sense. But if it was easy, it would've already been done. There's no question about that.

**Charlie Munger**（01:27:09）：It's not easy.

**Warren Buffett**（01:27:13）：No. (Laughs) But it should be tried.
