## 问题

股东指出：去年三季度伯克希尔以均价约 207 美元回购了近 10 亿美元 B 类股，此后四个多月股价低于 207 美元，公司却只回购了很少的量，手中还握着 1120 亿美元现金。为何不加大回购？除非当时雷达上真有一笔 800 亿—900 亿美元的收购。

## 回答要点

1. 回购不再挂钩账面价值，旧政策已经过时
2. 原则：回购价必须让留下的股东比回购前的那个瞬间更划算
3. 巴菲特心中的内在价值是一个约 ±10% 的区间，与芒格的区间接近但不相同
4. 一季度已回购 10 亿美元出头，「可以买，但没有馋到流口水的地步」
5. 若股价比内在价值低 25%—30% 且无更好用途，会花非常可观的钱

## 关键引述

> **“We will buy stock when we think it is selling below a conservative estimate of its intrinsic value.”**
>
> 「只有当我们认为股价低于其内在价值的保守估计时，我们才会买。」

> **“Well, I predict that we'll get a little more liberal in repurchasing shares.”**
>
> 「我预测，我们回购股票的尺度会稍微放宽一点。」

**Unknown**（00:16:57）：卡罗尔·卢米斯：很多人——不止一位——写信给我谈股票回购，所以我把它选作我的第一个问题。这个问题来自住在比利时的沃德·库基，今天早上他还在给我发邮件，谈到刚发布的一季报。他问：「我的问题与你们回购伯克希尔股票有关。去年第三季度，你们花了近 10 亿美元买进伯克希尔 B 类股，均价约 207 美元。但从 12 月 26 日到 4 月 11 日，股价在 207 美元下方徘徊了近四个月，而你们只回购了在我看来很有限的一点股票，尽管你们手里握着 1120 亿美元的巨款。我的问题是：为什么你们不回购更多股票？当然，除非有段时间你们的雷达上真有一笔 800 亿到 900 亿美元的收购。」

**Warren Buffett**（00:18:07）：是的。我们手里是 1000 亿还是 2000 亿美元，或者说 500 亿，都不会改变我们回购股票的思路。

我们过去把回购与账面价值挂钩，但那条政策实际上已经过时了。真正要做的是：只有当你认为回购价格能让留下来的股东——在你回购之后的那个瞬间——比你回购之前的那个瞬间更值钱，才去回购。

这就像你在经营一家合伙企业，有三个合伙人，生意值 300 万。其中一位来说：「我想让你按 110 万买回我的份额。」我们说，算了吧。他说 100 万？我们多半还是说算了吧。但如果他说 90 万，我们就买。因为那时剩下的生意还值 210 万，我们是两个所有人，我们的权益价值就从 100 万变成了 105 万。就这么简单的算术。

大多数公司宣布回购计划时只说「我们要花多少钱」。这就好比说「我们要买 XYZ 股票，准备花这么多钱」，「我们要买一家公司，不惜代价」。只有当我们认为股价低于其内在价值的保守估计时，我们才会买。内在价值不是一个精确的点，在我心里大概是一个区间，带宽可能有 10%。查理心里也有个区间，大概也是 10%。两个区间不完全一样，但非常接近。有时他估得比我高一点，有时低一点。

但我们要确定的是：回购之后，那些没有卖股票的人比回购前更划算。就这么简单。今年一季度你们会看到我们买了 10 亿美元出头的股票，这远不到我雄心壮志的水平。这意味着我们觉得可以买，但没有馋到流口水的地步。我们认为一季度回购的股票让留下来的股东比不回购更好，但我们不认为差别有多大。

你们很容易会遇到这样的时期：如果我们觉得股价比内在价值低 25% 或 30%，而我们手头又没有更好的用途，我们会花掉非常可观的资金。但只要不认为这么做能让你们更划算，我们在任何一个季度都不会花一分钱。查理？

**Charlie Munger**（00:21:45）：我预测，我们回购股票的尺度会稍微放宽一点。（笑声）

**Warren Buffett**（00:22:00）：我本来想给你对等的时间，不过……（笑声）

## 英文原文（CNBC 官方逐字稿）

**Unknown**（00:16:57）：CAROL LOOMIS (RETIRED FORTUNE MAGAZINE EDITOR): Many people — a number of people — wrote me about repurchases of stock. And, hence, the question I picked for my first one. The question, this particular question comes from Ward Cookie (PH), who lives in Belgium and who was still emailing me this morning in reference to the first quarter report. And he asked, "My question concerns your repurchase of Berkshire shares. In the third quarter of last year, you spent almost 1 billion buying Berkshire B stock at an average price of $207. "But then you got to a period between December 26th and April 11th when the stock languished for almost four months under 207. And yet, you purchased what I think of as a very limited amount of stock, even as you were sitting on an enormous pile of 112 billion. "My question is why you did not repurchase a lot more stock? Unless, of course, there was for a time an acquisition of, say, 80 billion to 90 billion on your radar."

**Warren Buffett**（00:18:07）：Yeah, the question — whether we had 100 billion or 200 billion would not make a difference — or 50 billion — would not make a difference in our approach to repurchase of shares. We repurchase shares — we used to have a policy of tying it to book value. But that became — really became obsolete. It did not — The real thing is to buy stock — repurchase shares — only when you think you're doing it at a price where the remaining shareholders have had — are worth more the moment after you repurchased it than they were the moment before. It's very much like if you were running a partnership and you had three partners in it and the business was worth 3 million, and one of the partners came and said, "I'd like you to buy back my share of the partnership for a billion" — I started out with millions, so I'll stay with millions — "for $1.1 million?" And we said, "Forget it." And if he said, "1 million?" we'd probably say, "Forget it," unless — and if he said, "900,000," we'd take it because, at that point, the remaining business would be worth 2-million-1, and we'd have two owners, and our interest in value would have gone from a million to a million and fifty-thousand. So, it's very simple arithmetic. Most companies adopt repurchase programs and they just say, "We're going to spend so much." That's like saying, you know, "We're going to buy XYZ stock, and we're going to spend so much here." "We're going to buy a company." "We're going to spend whatever it takes." We will buy stock when we think it is selling below a conservative estimate of its intrinsic value. Now, the intrinsic value is not a specific point, it's probably a range in my mind that might have a band maybe of 10 percent. Charlie would have a band in his mind, and it would probably be 10 percent. And ours would not be identical, but they'd be very close. And sometimes he might figure a bit higher than I do, a bit lower. But we want to be sure, when we repurchase shares, that those people who have not sold shares are better off than they were before we repurchased them. And it's very simple. And in the first quarter of the year, they'll find we bought something over a billion worth of stock, and that's nothing like my ambitions. But it — what that means is that we feel that we're OK buying it, but we don't salivate over buying it. We think that the shares we repurchased in the first quarter leave the shareholders better off than if we hadn't — the remaining shareholders — better off than if we hadn't bought it. But we don't think the difference is dramatic. And you will — you could easily see periods where we would spend very substantial sums if we thought the stock was selling at, say, 25 or 30 percent less than it was worth, and we didn't have something else that was even better. But we have no ambition in any given quarter to spend a dime unless we think you're going to be better off for us having done so. Charlie?

**Charlie Munger**（00:21:45）：Well, I predict that we'll get a little more liberal in repurchasing shares. (Laughter)

**Warren Buffett**（00:22:00）：I was going to give you equal time, but then — (Laughter)
