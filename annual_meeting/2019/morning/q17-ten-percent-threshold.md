## 问题

为何要定期减持银行等持仓、摆脱 10% 以上的监管要求？美联储拟允许持银行股至 25% 而不触发更严监管，若落地，会否放弃自设的 10% 红线？

## 回答要点

1. 短摆利润规则：持股超 10% 后六个月内获利卖出须把利润归还公司，限制反手与改主意
2. 超 10% 后每笔买入须两三个工作日内申报，等于向全世界做广告，执行成本巨大
3. 为富国申请美联储许可花了一年多，子公司被问了无数问题
4. 若美联储放宽规则，会看到伯克希尔在更多公司超过 10%；「喜欢 9.5%，就更喜欢 15%」
5. 芒格：这是「极度富有的又一个尴尬劣势」

## 关键引述

> **“if we like 9.5 percent of a company, we'd like 15 percent better”**
>
> 「如果喜欢一家公司 9.5% 的持股，我们会更喜欢 15%」

**Warren Buffett**（02:01:14）：好，格雷格？

**Gregg Warren**（02:01:08）：格雷格·沃伦：沃伦、查理。我理解伯克希尔每年需要减持富国银行及其他银行持股，把持仓压到美联储要求的 10% 以下，尤其行业回购不断。但看到你们尽可能定期减持、以摆脱 10% 以上持仓附带的监管要求，我还是有些意外——在我看来，这缩小了伯克希尔、至少缩小了巴菲特能长期实质性投资的范围，毕竟 2000 亿美元股票组合的大头由沃伦管理。能否展开谈谈持股超 10% 对伯克希尔的监管影响？另外怎么看待美联储最近允许伯克希尔这类投资者持银行股至 25% 而不触发更严格监管的提案？如果落地，你们会放弃自设的 10% 红线，把钱投进本来就很熟悉的名字吗？

**Warren Buffett**（02:02:27）：10% 这个事，有几个原因——

**Charlie Munger**（02:02:30）：这个回答是对的。嗯。（笑声）

**Warren Buffett**（02:02:35）：对银行还有美联储的要求。但很多人可能不知道：持有某只普通股超过 10%，若在六个月内获利卖出，短摆利润要归还给公司；卖出要与最低买入价配对计算。我印象中卖出后六个月内再买回、价格低于卖出价，同样要把差价交给公司——这条规则我多年没有重读，不太确定。三四十年前，有律师专门扫描我每月收到的 SEC 报告，找无意违反这条规则的人，替公司追讨并抽取报酬。所以它极大限制了反手、改主意的灵活性。

其次，超过 10% 之后，每笔买入都要在两三个工作日内申报，等于向全世界做广告，而世界多少会跟着我们下单，执行成本巨大。这两条都是显著的减分项，一般人还想不到。

达美航空那次越线其实是意外，但我完全不介意。如果美联储改变规则，我们就不必减到线下了。我们不想成为银行控股公司。多年前我们为富国申请过许可，过期后前几年又申请了一次，花了一年多，富国被问了我们无数问题。这确实是个威慑，未来会小一些。短摆规则对我们这样的人比对一般买卖股票的人负担轻，因为我们本来就不怎么倒腾。

**Charlie Munger**（02:05:35）：但如果没有这些破规矩，我们会愉快地买更多，对吧？

**Warren Buffett**（02:05:42）：当然当然。呃，其实每次买我们都挺愉快……是的，你们会看到我们在更多公司超过 10%。美联储若改规则，有些公司会因为回购让我们的持股自然飘过 10%——富国如此，过去一两年一两家航空公司也如此。喜欢 9.5%，就更喜欢 15%。未来你们可能看到我们在这方面表现不同。

**Charlie Munger**（02:06:30）：极度富有的又一个尴尬劣势。

**Warren Buffett**（02:06:33）：是。（笑声）还真是。还有被人跟踪模仿——跟单的问题可能真是个麻烦。

## 英文原文（CNBC 官方逐字稿）

**Warren Buffett**（02:01:14）：OK, Gregg?

**Gregg Warren**（02:01:08）：Warren and Charlie. While I understand Berkshire's need to trim its stake in Wells Fargo and any other banks you hold, each year, in order to bring Berkshire's ownership stake below the 10 percent threshold required by the Federal Reserve for bank holdings, given the ongoing share repurchase activity that's taking place in the industry. I was kind of surprised, though, to see you move to trim all of your holdings, where possible, on a regular basis to eliminate the regulatory requirements that come with ownership levels above 10 percent, which in my view limits the investment universe that Berkshire, or at least Warren, can meaningfully invest in longer term, given that Warren manages a large chunk of Berkshire's $200 billion equity portfolio. Could you elaborate more on the regulatory impact for Berkshire of holding more than 10 percent of any company's stock, as well as how you feel about the Fed's recent proposal to allow investors like Berkshire to own up to 25 percent shares of a bank without triggering more restrictive rules and oversight? Basically, if that proposal were to come to fruition, would you be willing to forego that 10 percent threshold self-imposed that you've done, and put money to work in names that you're already fairly comfortable with?

**Warren Buffett**（02:02:27）：Yeah, the 10 percent, there's a couple reasons —

**Charlie Munger**（02:02:30）：That's the right answer. Yeah. (Laughter)

**Warren Buffett**（02:02:35）：We will — there's two factors beyond in the case of banks. There's the Federal Reserve requirement there. But many people probably don't even — might not know about this, but if you own over 10 percent of a security — common stock — and you sell it within six months at a profit, you give the money over to the company, the short-swing profit that you give them. And you match your — any sale against your lowest purchase. And I think if you sell it and then buy it within six months — I'm not as positive about that, because I haven't reread the rule for a lot of years. But I think if you sell and then buy within six months, and the purchase is below the price at which you made the sale, you owe the money to the company. There used to be lawyers that would scan that monthly SEC report that I used to get 30 or 40 years ago. They would scan it to find people that inadvertently had broken that rule, and they would get paid a fee for recovering it for the company. So, it restricts enormous — it restricts significantly your ability to reverse a position or change your mind or something of the sort. Secondly, I think you have to report within two or three business days every purchase you make once you're in that over 10 percent factor. So, you're advertising to the world, but the world tends to follow us some, so it really — it has a huge execution cost attached to it. Nevertheless — and those are both significant minuses, and they're both things that people generally don't think about. We did go over recently, for example, in Delta Airlines, that was actually an accident, but I don't mind the fact at all that we did. And if the Federal Reserve changes its approach, we won't have to trim down below that. We don't want to become a bank holding company and we don't want to — We went in many years ago and got permission with Wells, but then our permission expired, and we went in again a few — a couple years ago. And we spent a year or so, and there were just a million questions that Wells got asked about us and so on. So, it's been a deterrent. It'll be less of a deterrent in the future, but it does have those two — The short-swing thing is less onerous to us than it would be to most people who buy and sell stocks, because we don't really think in terms of doing much.

**Charlie Munger**（02:05:35）：But if we didn't have all these damn rules, we would cheerfully buy more, wouldn't we?

**Warren Buffett**（02:05:42）：Sure, sure. Well, any time we buy we do it cheerfully, but — Yeah. And we will — you'll probably see us at more than 10 percent in more things. And if the Fed should change its rules, there will be companies where we drift up over 10 percent simply because they're repurchasing their shares. That's been the case with Wells, and it's been the case with an airline or two in the last year or so. So, if we like 9.5 percent of a company, we'd like 15 percent better, and you may see us behave a little differently on that in the future.

**Charlie Munger**（02:06:30）：Well, one more awkward disadvantage of being extremely rich.

**Warren Buffett**（02:06:33）：Yeah. (Laughter) And it really is. Yeah, and people following you. I mean, the followers problem can be a real problem.
