## 问题

如何为暴露于极低概率、极高代价风险的非常规保险合约定价？要做哪些分析与心智检查，确保盈利又不至于过度暴露于灾难性风险？未来的 CEO 会否与首席承保人保持同样紧密的合作？

## 回答要点

1. 贾因：起点是尽量用科学——历史数据；数据不够时做主观判断并「绝对设定敞口上限」
2. 多数时候无从下判断就放弃；偶尔在主观概率上有显著安全边际时出手
3. 「终极测试是我拿起电话打给沃伦」——两人互不通气各报价格
4. 9/11 后全世界只有寥寥数人愿意听、有能力接单；伯克希尔「输得起大钱」
5. 芒格：这些年阿吉特的怪异交易已为资产负债表添了 500 多亿美元；巴菲特：他若不跟我商量是 499 亿
6. 这种才能「基本教不会」，不是什么秘密公式

## 关键引述

**Warren Buffett**（02:21:45）：好，贝基？

**Becky Quick**（02:21:38）：贝基·奎克（CNBC）：沃伦，你刚才提到阿吉特（贾因）和格雷格（阿贝尔）都在现场回答问题，所以我想替西雅图的威尔问这个问题。问题是提给阿吉特·贾因先生和沃伦·巴菲特先生的。「你们说过会就非常规保险合约保持定期沟通——这类合约暴露于极不可能发生但代价极高的风险。我好奇你们如何思考、如何安全地为这些非常规合约定价。你们要做哪些分析、跑哪些心智检查，以确保伯克希尔既能盈利又不至于过度暴露于灾难性风险？另外，巴菲特先生，您是否希望未来的 CEO 与首席承保人保持同样紧密的合作？」

**Warren Buffett**（02:22:29）：我们马上给阿吉特递话筒、打聚光灯。他就在那儿。阿吉特，如果你愿意，先请。

**Ajit Jain**（02:22:50）：阿吉特·贾因：你好。显然，起点在于：这类情形没有足够的数据可以依赖，更像艺术而非科学。我们先尽量用科学——研究与特定风险相关的历史数据，或与之近似的数据。如果历史数据不够，那显然必须做判断：这种事发生的概率有多大？在这类情形下，我们绝对会设定敞口上限。这样，如果坏事情发生或者我们判断错了，我们确切知道自己会亏多少钱，以及这个损失能否在不重创利润表或资产负债表的前提下吸收。

至于艺术的部分，很难。多数时候根本无从下判断，我们就放弃。但偶尔，我们认为能拿到一个价格，使我们对这类事件的主观概率有显著的安全边际，于是觉得这个风险值得承担。

最后，终极测试是我拿起电话打给沃伦：「沃伦，这里有一单，你怎么看？」（笑声）好，该你了，沃伦。

**Warren Buffett**（02:24:13）：好。（掌声）

**Charlie Munger**（02:24:16）：这不容易，你不会想让随便什么人替你干这活。

**Warren Buffett**（02:24:23）：不不。事实上，对我们有时接到的那类业务，我只愿意让阿吉特来做。就这么简单，没有第二个他。

如阿吉特所说，我们会看最坏情形；如果赔率合意我们就愿意接——而且你说的对，这些事查不了资料。阿拉斯加或加州过去一百年发生过多少次 6.0 级以上地震，这可以查；很多东西都有数据可查，有时有用，有时没用。但另一些情形——比如 9/11 之后：接下来几天会不会还有连环袭击？我记得当时国泰航空的航班如果不安排好巨额责任险，下一个周一就落不了香港。世界必须继续运转。西尔斯大厦的按揭持有人突然要求保险覆盖——我记得确实是它——各种求助蜂拥而至：一周前还不在意这些事的人，现在为涉及巨额资金的风险忧心忡忡。当时全世界真正愿意听、而且有能力接单的只有寥寥数人，而且没有书可查。所以其中有很大的判断成分。

阿吉特在这方面比我强一百倍，但我们在这类事情上想法相近。不能太相近，但我们确实相近。我有输得起大钱的意愿。几乎每家保险公司到了更高限额都得靠既有分保条约，只能接那么多——当时全世界都瘫痪了。我们不时接到一些全世界没有别人能做的询价。这有点像我们的投资处境，只是搬到了保险上。我们不靠这个营生，但时机来临时我们准备好了。

阿吉特是全世界任何其他公司都没有的资产。我们也确实「用足了他」。我们很喜欢彼此讨论这类风险：他会让我先想价格应该是多少，我们互不通气，然后我报个数，他会说：「沃伦，你疯了吗？」（笑）然后指出我忽略了什么。这很有趣，也为我们赚了很多钱。伯克希尔的股东极其幸运。像阿吉特这样的人是雇不来的，一辈子遇到一个就是万幸。查理？

**Charlie Munger**（02:28:21）：我想我们没帮上提问者多少忙。这真的很难。

**Warren Buffett**（02:28:25）：会有那样的时刻——我大概赶不上了——就像金融市场一样，保险世界偶尔会出现只有伯克希尔、几乎只有伯克希尔被人求助的时刻。

**Charlie Munger**（02:28:50）：但过去这些年，阿吉特，与你商量这些怪异的交易，已经给伯克希尔的资产负债表添了 500 多亿美元。

**Warren Buffett**（02:28:58）：要是他不跟我商量，大概会是 499 亿。（笑）不过别在家里试这个。

**Charlie Munger**（02:29:12）：是，这不意味着它容易。

**Warren Buffett**（02:29:14）：不容易，而且基本教不会。

**Charlie Munger**（02:29:17）：对，教不会，你说得对。

**Warren Buffett**（02:29:20）：伯克希尔也没有什么藏着掖着的秘密公式。这基本上就是阿吉特身上一种极不寻常的天赋。

**Charlie Munger**（02:29:32）：我们没有保留什么。它就是难。

## 英文原文（CNBC 官方逐字稿）

**Warren Buffett**（02:21:45）：OK, Becky?

**Becky Quick**（02:21:38）：Warren, you mentioned, in response to an earlier question, that Ajit (Jain) and Greg (Abel) are both here to answer questions, and so I thought I'd ask this question that comes from Will in Seattle. He says, his question is for Mr. Ajit Jain and Mr. Warren Buffett. "You have said that you communicate regularly about unconventional insurance contracts that expose the company to extremely unlikely but highly costly events. I'm curious about how you think about and safely price these unconventional insurance contracts. What analyses and mental checks do you run through your head, to make sure that Berkshire Hathaway will profit without being unduly exposed to catastrophic risk? "Furthermore, Mr. Buffett, would you want a future CEO to continue a similarly close collaboration with the chief underwriter?"

**Warren Buffett**（02:22:29）：We will get a microphone to Ajit and a spotlight in just a second. And there he is. Ajit, why don't you answer first, if you'd like to?

**Ajit Jain**（02:22:50）：Hi. Obviously, the starting point, I mean, these situations where there's not enough data to hang your hat on, it's more of an art than a science. We start off with as much science as we can use, looking at historical data that relates to the risk in particular, or something that comes close to relating to the risk that we're looking at. And then beyond that, if there's not enough historical data we can look at, then clearly, we have to make a judgment in terms of, what are the odds of something like that happening? We try — we absolutely, in situations like that, we absolutely make sure we cap our exposure. So, that if something bad happens or we've got something wrong, we absolutely know that how much money we can lose and whether we can absorb that loss without much pain to the income statement or the balance sheet. In terms of art, it's a difficult situation. More often than not, it's impossible to have a point of view, and we end up passing on it. But every now and then, we think we can get a price where the subjective odds we have of something like that happening has a significant margin of safety in it. So, we feel it's a risk that's worth taking. Then finally, the absolute acid test is, I pick up the phone and call Warren. "Warren, here's a deal. What do you think?" (Laughter) OK. Your turn, Warren.

**Warren Buffett**（02:24:13）：OK. (Applause)

**Charlie Munger**（02:24:16）：It's not easy, and you wouldn't want just anybody doing it for you.

**Warren Buffett**（02:24:23）：No, no. In fact, the only one I would want doing it for us on the kind of things we have sometimes received is Ajit. I mean, it's that simple. There isn't anybody like him. And as Ajit said, we'll look at a worst case, but we are willing, if we like the odds, and like you say, there's no way to look these up. We can tell you how many 6.0 or greater earthquakes have happened in the last hundred years in Alaska or California or so on. And there's a lot of things you can look up figures on. Sometimes those are useful, and sometimes they aren't. But there's a lot where you can get a lot of data. And then there's others that — well, after 9/11, you know, was that going to be the first of several other attacks that were going to happen very quickly? There were planes flying that couldn't — well they couldn't land in Hong Kong, as I remember. I think it was Cathay Pacific couldn't land in Hong Kong the following Monday unless they had a big liability coverage placed with somebody. I mean, the world had to go on. The people that held mortgages on the Sears Tower all of a sudden wanted coverage. —I think that actually was one — but they were just pouring in, of people that hadn't been worried about something a week earlier, and now they were worried about things involving huge sums. And there were really only a couple people in the world that would even listen and had the capacity to take on a lot of the deals we were proposed. And there's no book to look up. So, you do — there's a big element of judgment. Ajit and I — I mean, Ajit's a hundred times better at this than I am, but we do tend to think alike on this sort of thing. You don't want to think too much alike, but we think alike. I've got a willingness to lose a lot of money. And most, well, virtually every insurance company if they get up to higher limits, they've got treaties in place, and they can only take this much. So, the world was paralyzed on that. We don't get those, but now obviously. But we do occasionally get inquiries about doing things that really nobody else in the world can do. It's a little like our investment situation, only transferred over to insurance. We don't build a business around it, but we are ready when the time comes. And Ajit is an asset that no other company in the world has. And we work him. And we actually enjoy a lot talking to each other about these kind of risks, because he'll ask me to think about what the price should be. And he'll think about — we don't tell each other ahead of time. And then I'll name it, and then he'll say, "Have you lost your mind, Warren?" (Laughs) And then he'll point something out to me that I've overlooked. And it's a lot of fun, and it's made us a lot of money. And the shareholders of Berkshire Hathaway are extraordinarily lucky. You can't hire people like Ajit. I mean, you get them once in a lifetime. Charlie?

**Charlie Munger**（02:28:21）：I don't think we helped him very much. It's really difficult.

**Warren Buffett**（02:28:25）：There will be a time when — I mean, I probably won't be around then — but there will be a time occasionally, just like in financial markets, when things are happening in the insurance world, and basically, Berkshire will be the only one — virtually the only one — people turn to.

**Charlie Munger**（02:28:50）：But in the past, Ajit, talking to you, has added more than $50 billion to the balance sheet at Berkshire, by making these oddball calls.

**Warren Buffett**（02:28:58）：And if he hadn't talked to me, it'd be probably 49.9 billion, you know? (Laughs) But you don't want to try — don't try this at home.

**Charlie Munger**（02:29:12）：Yeah, that doesn't mean it's easy.

**Warren Buffett**（02:29:14）：No. And it's not very teachable.

**Charlie Munger**（02:29:17）：No, it isn't very teachable, you're right.

**Warren Buffett**（02:29:20）：No, it is not something that Berkshire has some secret formula someplace for it. It basically is a very unusual talent with Ajit, and —

**Charlie Munger**（02:29:32）：We're not holding anything back. It's hard.
