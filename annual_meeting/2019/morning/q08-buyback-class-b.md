## 问题

A 类与 B 类股流动性悬殊，回购机会多半落在 B 类；结合每年捐股给基金会、以及「希望股东忠诚」的表态，能否推断未来回购以 B 类为主？

## 回答要点

1. B 类日均成交约 6.22 亿美元，A 类约 7700 万美元，花在 B 类的钱必然多得多
2. 历史上 A 类偶尔有整块出售（吉野遗产、华盛顿邮报换股）
3. 不希望股票显著低估或显著高估，理想是围绕内在价值波动
4. 「无意为便宜回购而打压股价」

## 关键引述

> **“we don't want the stock to be either significantly underpriced or significantly overpriced.”**
>
> 「我们不希望股票显著低估或显著高估。」

**Warren Buffett**（00:56:53）：格雷格·沃伦（晨星金融服务分析师）：沃伦，我的第一个问题不出意外还是回购。公开市场回购需要买卖双方都愿意。伯克希尔有两类股票，回购时本应更灵活。但两类股票流动性差别巨大——过去五年 A 类股日均成交 313 股，约合每天 7700 万美元；B 类股日均成交 370 万股，约合 6.22 亿美元。伯克希尔回购 B 类股的机会远多于 A 类——去年下半年和 2019 年一季度正是如此。虽然回购 A 类股、注销更多带投票权的股份更理想，但两类股价差很小，而且你每年把 A 类股捐给比尔及梅琳达·盖茨基金会和子女的基金会，B 类股逐年增加。考虑到你最近对《金融时报》说更希望股东名单上是忠诚的长期持有人——328,000 美元一股的 A 类股似乎正是这种门槛——我们是否可以推断，你们未来的回购将主要落在 B 类股上？

**Warren Buffett**（00:58:17）：是的。回购金额可观时，花在 B 类上的钱一定比 A 类多得多，只因 B 类成交量高得多。历史上偶尔有人整块卖给我们——比如吉野（音）遗产的股份，还有当年用华盛顿邮报股票换电视台和邮报股东持有的 A 类股的交易。所以 A 类偶尔有整块出现，B 类也会有。但毫无疑问：如果我们能花 250 亿、500 亿或 1000 亿美元回购，几乎所有钱都会花在 B 类上。这方面没有总体规划，就是价格合意时积极买进。B 类按美元计的成交量就是比 A 类大得多。查理？

**Charlie Munger**（00:59:47）：我不太在意买哪一类。

**Warren Buffett**（00:59:50）：（笑声）说真的，理想状态下——如果我们规模很小——一年就定一次价，像私人公司那样操作：价格公允，想退出的人能退出；别人想接手也好；没人接手而我们认为价格公允，就由公司回购。我们做不到。但我们不希望股票显著低估或显著高估。高估那半句上我们大概是独一无二的——我们就是不希望。我不想让股票卖到价值两倍的价格，因为那会让人失望。股价远超价值时，没有魔法能让它物有所值。商业上讲，股价很便宜时，我们回购自然高兴。但理想状态是股价大致围绕内在企业价值波动。我们无意炒作，也无意为便宜回购而打压。市场的本性就是高估与低估交替出现——低估时我们就占便宜。

## 英文原文（CNBC 官方逐字稿）

**Warren Buffett**（00:56:53）：OK, Gregg Warren. GREGG WARREN (FINANCIAL SERVICES ANALYST, MORNINGSTAR RESEARCH SERVICES): Warren, my first question, not surprisingly, is on share repurchases. Stock buybacks in the open market are a function of both willing buyers and sellers. With Berkshire having two shares of classes, you should have more flexibility when buying back stock. But given the liquidity difference that exists between the two share classes — with an average of 313 Class A shares exchanging hands daily the past five years, equivalent to around $77 million a day, and an average of 3.7 million Class B shares doing the same, equivalent to around 622 million — Berkshire's likely to have more opportunities to buy back Class B shares than Class A, which is exactly what we saw during the back half of last year and the first quarter of 2019. While it might be more ideal for Berkshire to buy back Class A shares, allowing you to retire shares with far greater voting rights, given that there's relatively little arbitrage between the two share classes and the number of Class B shares increase every year as you gift your Class A shares to the Bill and Melinda Gates Foundation and your children's foundations, can we assume that you're likely to be a far greater repurchaser of Class B shares, going forward, especially given your recent comments to the Financial Times about preferring to have loyal individuals on your shareholder list, which a price tag of $328,000 of Class A shares seems to engender?

**Warren Buffett**（00:58:17）：Yeah, we will - when we're repurchasing shares, if we're purchasing substantial amounts, we're going to spend a lot more on the Class B than the Class A, just because the trading volume is considerably higher. We may, from time to time — well, we got offered a couple blocks in history, going back in history from the Yoshi (PH) estate and when we had a transaction exchanging our Washington Post stock for both a television station and shares held — A shares — held by the Washington Post. So, we may see some blocks of A. We may see some blocks of B. But there's no question. If we are able to spend 25, 50, or a hundred billion dollars in repurchasing shares, more of the money is almost certainly going to be spent on the B than the A. There's no master plan on that other than to buy aggressively when we like the price. And as I say, the trading volume in the B is just a lot higher than the A in dollar amounts. Charlie?

**Charlie Munger**（00:59:47）：I don't think we care much which class we buy.

**Warren Buffett**（00:59:50）：Yeah. (Laughter) We would like — we really want the stock — ideally, if we could do it if we were small — once a year we'd have a price and, you know, we'd do it like a private company. And it would be a fair price and people who want to get out could get out. And if other people wanted to buy their interest, fine. And if they didn't, and we thought the price was fair, we'd have the company repurchase it. We can't do that. But that’s — we don't want the stock to be either significantly underpriced or significantly overpriced. And we're probably unique on the overpriced part of it. But we don't want it. I do not want the stock selling at twice what's it worth because I'm going to disappoint people, you know. I mean, we can't make it — there's no magic formula to make a stock worth what it's selling for, if it sells for way too much. From a commercial standpoint, if it's selling very cheap, we have to like it when we repurchase it. But ideally, we would hope the stock would sell in a range that more or less is its intrinsic business value. We have no desire to hype it in any way. And we have no desire to depress it so we can repurchase it cheap. But the nature of markets is that things get overpriced and they get underpriced. And we will — if it's underpriced, we'll take advantage of it.
