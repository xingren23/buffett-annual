## 问题

您是高调的民主党人。若民主党候选人赢得大选，伯克希尔会否面临更多监管、更高公司税甚至拆分呼声？作为受托人，您如何看待自己的政治表达？

## 回答要点

1. 「不把公民身份放进盲信，但也不代表公司发言」
2. 伯克希尔 54 年从未给总统候选人捐款；子公司 PAC 仅求与竞争对手同等的接触机会
3. 自称「持证的资本主义者」，但认为资本主义需要监管、需要照顾被甩下的人
4. 医疗支出占 GDP 从 5% 升至 17%—18%，与联邦政府开支占比相当，故与摩根大通、亚马逊合搞医疗项目
5. 不认为美国会在 2020、2040 或 2060 年走向社会主义

## 关键引述

> **“I'm a card-carrying capitalist.”**
>
> 「我是一个持证的资本主义者。」

> **“you do not put your citizenship in a blind trust.”**
>
> 「你不会把公民身份放进盲信。」

## 中文全文（逐段翻译）

> 与下方「英文原文」逐段对应，按发言顺序排列；术语与专名首次出现时保留英文。

**Unknown**（00:50:01）：安德鲁·罗斯·索尔金（《纽约时报》/CNBC）：沃伦、查理，这个问题——其实我们收到了好几个同类问题——这个表述最好。沃伦，您一直是高调的民主党人。在民主党总统候选人热议社会主义与资本主义之争的背景下，如果他们赢得大选，您预计伯克希尔会面临更多监管、更高的公司税，甚至旗下公司被呼吁拆分吗？作为公司的受托人，同时作为说过「身居商界领袖之位并不等于把公民身份放进盲信」的人，您如何看待自己的政治立场？

**Warren Buffett**（00:50:38）：是的，我说过，你不会把公民身份放进盲信。但你也不能代表公司发言。你发言时是以公民身份发言，所以必须小心时机，因为人们会默认你在代表公司说话。54 年来，伯克希尔·哈撒韦从来没有、也永远不会给总统候选人捐款。我想我们没给任何政治候选人捐过款——但我不想替这 54 年打包票。（掌声）现在我们不这么做。

我们经营着几个受监管的行业。我们的铁路和公用事业，实际上必须在华盛顿和所在州的立法机构有存在感。所以有几家政治行动委员会（PAC）——那是收购子公司时就存在的。毫无疑问，它们会做一些捐款，只是为了获得和竞争对手同样的接触机会。卡车行业要游说，铁路行业当然也要游说。但总的原则是：人们不能用你们的钱追逐自己的政治利益。这些年有一两个经理人，用向他们的供应商之类募捐的方式搞政治筹款。我一旦发现，立即叫停。

我在伯克希尔的职位，不会被用来推进我个人的政治信念。但当一场竞选足够重要时，我可以以个人身份、而不是以伯克希尔代表的身份表达我的政治信念。我尽量少这么做。不过谁都知道，上次大选我为候选人筹过款。我不会给 PAC 捐钱——有次无意捐过一次，不知道那是 PAC，此后就不做了。但大额筹款是有的。我不喜欢金钱在政治中的用法。我在《纽约时报》写过评论文章，谈金钱对政治的影响。很多年前，在《麦凯恩—法因戈尔德法案》出台之前，我花时间与约翰·麦凯恩探讨过限制的办法。但世界后来走了另一条路。

**Warren Buffett**（00:54:03）：关于你问的这个话题——我只想说，我是一个持证的资本主义者。（掌声）我们今天能坐在这里，靠的就是市场体系、法治和这个国家的一些根本制度，所以不必担心我在这方面改弦易辙。但我也认为资本主义离不开监管，离不开照顾被甩下的人——尤其是当国家极度繁荣的时候。除此之外，我没有为任何主张站台的伯克希尔讲台。查理？

**Charlie Munger**（00:54:40）：在我们这样繁荣的国家，我们所有人都赞成要有某种政府社会保障网。我们很多人不喜欢的，是政府管理这张保障网某些部分时的巨大愚蠢。（掌声）如果我们做得更明智，会好得多。不过做得更大方些，也许也会更好。

**Warren Buffett**（00:55:10）：是的。我们和摩根大通、亚马逊一起搞医疗这个项目，原因之一是：美国花在医疗上的钱有 3.3 万亿或 3.4 万亿美元，和联邦政府的全部开支差不多。医疗占 GDP 的比重从 5% 升到 17% 或 18%，而联邦政府开支的占比基本停在 17% 上下。所以我们希望私营部门能带来重大改进，因为我通常认为多数事情私营部门干得比公共部门好。但我也认为，如果私营部门不作为，答案就会是另一种样子。我愿意相信在这个问题上，私营部门能拿出比公共部门更好的方案。

看谁被提名吧——这些年我投过不少共和党人。1960 年我还竞选过共和党全国代表大会的代表人。不过，我不认为这个国家会在 2020 年、2040 年或 2060 年变成社会主义。

## 英文原文（CNBC 官方逐字稿）

**Unknown**（00:50:01）：ANDREW ROSS SORKIN (NEW YORK TIMES/CNBC): Warren and Charlie, this is a question — actually, we got a handful of questions on this topic. This is probably the best formulation of it. Warren, you have been a long-time, outspoken Democrat. With all the talk about socialism versus capitalism taking place among Democratic presidential candidates, do you anticipate an impact on Berkshire in the form of more regulations, higher corporate taxes, or even calls for breakups among the many companies we own if they were to win? And how do you think about your own politics as a fiduciary of our company, and at the same time, as someone who has said that simply being a business leader doesn't mean you’ve put your citizenship in a blind trust?

**Warren Buffett**（00:50:38）：Yeah. I have said that you do not put your citizenship in a blind trust. But you also don't speak on behalf of your company. You do speak as a citizen if you speak. And therefore, you have to be careful about when you do speak, because it's going to be assumed you're speaking on behalf of your company. Berkshire Hathaway certainly, in 54 years, has never — and will never — made a contribution to a presidential candidate. I don't think we've made a contribution to any political candidate. But I don't want to say, for 54 years, that — (Applause) We don't do it now. We operate in several regulated industries. And our railroad and our utility, as a practical matter, they have to have a presence in Washington or in the state legislatures in which they operate. So, we have some — a few — I don't know how many — political action committees which existed when we bought it — when we bought the companies at subsidiaries. And I think, unquestionably, they make some contributions simply to achieve the same access as their competitors. I mean, if the trucking industry is going to lobby, I'm sure the railroad industry's going to lobby. But — the general — well, the rule is, I mean, that people do not pursue their own political interests with your money here. We've had one or two managers over the years, for example, that would do some fundraising where they were fundraising from people who were suppliers of them or something of the sort. And if I ever find out about it, that ends promptly. My position, at Berkshire, is not to be used to further my own political beliefs. But my own political beliefs can be expressed as a person, not as a representative of Berkshire, when a campaign is important. I try to minimize it. But it's no secret that in the last election, for example, I raised money. I won't give money to PACs. I accidentally did it one time. I didn't know it was a PAC. But I don't do it. But I've raised substantial sums. I don't like the way money is used in politics. I've written op-ed pieces for the New York Times in the past on the influence of money in politics. I spent some time with John McCain many years ago before McCain-Feingold, on ways to try to limit it. But the world has developed in a different way.

**Warren Buffett**（00:54:03）：On your question about the — I will just say I'm a card-carrying capitalist. (Applause) But I — and I believe we wouldn't be sitting here except for the market system and the rule of law and some things that are embodied in this country. So, you don't have to worry about me changing in that manner. But I also think that capitalism does involve regulation. It involves taking care of people who are left behind, particularly when the country gets enormously prosperous. But beyond that, I have no Berkshire podium for pushing anything. Charlie?

**Charlie Munger**（00:54:40）：Well, I think we're all in favor of some kind of a government social safety net in a country as prosperous as ours. What a lot of us don't like is the vast stupidity with which parts of that social safety net are managed by the government. It'd be much better if — (applause) — we could do it more wisely. But I think it also might be better if we did it more liberally.

**Warren Buffett**（00:55:10）：Yeah, one of the reasons we're involved in this effort along with J.P. Morgan and Amazon — with (J.P. Morgan CEO) Jamie Dimon and (Amazon CEO) Jeff Bezos — on the medical question, is we do have as much money going — 3.3 or 3.4 trillion — we have as much money going to medical care as we have funding the federal government. And it's gone from 5 to 17 percent — or 18 percent — while actually the amount going to the federal government has stayed about the same at 17 percent. So, we hope there's some major improvements from the private sector because I generally think the private sector does a better job than the public sector in most things. But I also think that if the private sector doesn't do something, you'll get a different sort of answer. And I'd like to think that the private sector can come up with a better answer than the public sector in that respect. I will probably — it depends who's nominated — but I voted for plenty of Republicans over the years. I even ran for delegate to the Republican National Convention in 1960. But — we are not — I don't think the country will go into socialism in 2020 or in 2040 or 2060.
