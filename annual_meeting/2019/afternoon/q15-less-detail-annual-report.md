## 问题

年报信对经营业务与财务表现的细节越来越少：不再披露金融板块细节、制造服务零售板块资产负债表、按单位的浮存金拆分。为什么投资者拿到的信息变少了？

## 回答要点

1. 「我不认为信息变少了，只是呈现形式逐年不同」
2. 年报的假想读者是把大部分净资产放在伯克希尔、不懂行话的「两个妹妹」
3. 「300 页的报告信息量反而不如 50 页，还会把读者弄丢」
4. 「综合成本率」「运营比率」是行话，不如直接叫利润率
5. 细节对分析师有趣，但股东要判断的是谁在管钱、长期怎么管
6. 芒格：细节对竞争对手有利，对股东没有好处

## 关键引述

> **“you can write a 300-page report that gives a whole lot less information than a 50-page report.”**
>
> 「你可以写一份 300 页的报告，信息量反而不如 50 页。」

## 中文全文（逐段翻译）

> 与下方「英文原文」逐段对应，按发言顺序排列；术语与专名首次出现时保留英文。

**Warren Buffett**（01:13:17）：杰伊？

**Jay Gelb**（01:13:20）：杰伊·盖尔布（巴克莱保险分析师）：过去几年，伯克希尔年报信对经营业务与财务表现的细节越来越少。例如：不再提供金融与金融产品板块的细节，不再提供制造、服务与零售板块的资产负债表，不再按单位拆分保险浮存金。对伯克希尔这么庞大而多元的公司，为什么投资者拿到的信息比过去少了？

**Warren Buffett**（01:13:53）：我不认为我们提供的信息变少了，只是呈现形式逐年不同。比如今年，我照例在心里从「亲爱的多丽丝和伯蒂」（我的两个妹妹）开始写信：告诉那些把相当大比例净资产放在伯克希尔、聪明但不熟悉各行业行话、又愿意读很多字的人。只要我解释得体，他们就能听懂。我用的是让上百万股东里相当一部分人能理解的语言，因为他们的会计水平千差万别。我写的是我自己站在另一边想听的信息。

假如我是竞争对手，想知道某家家具店赚多少钱？我当然乐意知道，但那其实无关紧要。对一个 5000 亿美元的组织，通过三四五页纸就能让你看懂我们的保险业务全貌。实际上 SEC 要求我们披露一大堆损失准备发展之类的材料。我认为你可以写一份 300 页的报告，信息量反而不如 50 页，还会把读者弄丢。

所以我试着（就像我说的，在心里是对妹妹说话）告诉他们：如果我们合伙经营一门私人生意，每人持三分之一，他们每年想听什么。他们不知道综合成本率是什么意思，那本来就是个大家都用的愚蠢术语；重要的是叫「利润率」。他们不知道铁路的「运营比率」是什么，那是个过时的术语，叫利润率更好。

至于行话：我们不是写给分析师的，是写给股东的。让他们既能看清我们拥有什么，也能理解我们如何思考经营、长期想做什么。每年我们都尽力做到最好。对细节特别着迷的人，其实错过了整幅图画：1965 年你可以知道纺织业务的每一个细节：衬里赚多少、手帕赚多少。但那是另一个世界。重要的是我们如何看待管钱、长期会如何处理事情。1980 或 1985 年你若细读喜诗糖果东进的每处细节，反而会被误导——我们可以告诉你，整体上东进失败了。

细节对分析师可能很有趣，但股东要决定的是：谁在管他们的钱、怎么管、长期做过什么、未来想做什么、如何衡量。我们写给的是个人。查理？

**Charlie Munger**（01:17:51）：按说我应该盯着每家小公司盯到最后一枚硬币，但我不这么干，也不想要那么多细节。竞争对手会喜欢那些细节，但那对股东没有好处。所以我们会照现在的样子继续报。

**Warren Buffett**（01:18:06）：你们看我们这里多么灵活。（笑声）

## 英文原文（CNBC 官方逐字稿）

**Warren Buffett**（01:13:17）：Jay?

**Jay Gelb**（01:13:20）：For the past several years, in Berkshire's annual shareholder letter, there's been increasingly less detail provided on its operating businesses and financial performance. For example, the company is no longer providing details on the finance and financial product segment, or a balance sheet for the manufacturing, service, and retail segment, or a breakdown of float by unit in the insurance business. For a company as large and diversified as Berkshire, why are investors being provided less information than previously?

**Warren Buffett**（01:13:53）：Well, I don't think we actually provide less information. We may present it in a somewhat different form from year to year, just — and then this year, for example, you know, I started my letter, as usual, in my mind as saying, "Dear Doris and Birdie," my sisters, to tell them what I would tell anybody that had a very significant proportion of their net worth in Berkshire, who is intelligent, did not know all the lingo of our various businesses, that would read a lot of words, because they did have a large investment. So, if I explained anything, and did a decent job, that they would understand what I was talking about. And I tell them that in the language that I think will be understandable to a significant percentage of a million-plus people who have all kinds of different understanding of accounting and all that sort of thing. I tell them the information I would want to hear on the other side. Now, if I was a competitor, and I wanted to know what one of our furniture stores was earning or something of the sort, you know, I might love it, but it doesn't really make any difference. If you're talking about a $500 billion organization, if you understand our insurance business, in terms of giving you the picture, I think, in three or four or five pages — you know, actually we've got a whole bunch of stuff required by the SEC about loss reserve development. I think you can write a 300-page report that gives a whole lot less information than a 50-page report. And you lose people. So, I try to tell them — like I say, in my mind, it's my sisters — I try to tell them what I would tell them if we had a private business and they owned a third of it each, and I owned a third, and once a year, they like to get filled in. And they don't know what a combined ratio means because it's a dumb term that everybody uses. And the important thing is to call it a profit margin. They don't know what the operating ratio is in the railroad business, and it's an obsolete term. It'd be better to call that a profit margin. But the lingo — we're not writing it for analysts. We're writing it for shareholders, and we're trying to tell them something so they can make a — they can not only get the picture as to what we own now, but how we think about the operation, what we're trying to do over time. And we try to do the best job we can every year. And I don't think it — I think if somebody is terribly interested in the details, they really are missing the whole picture. Because you could have known every detail of our textile business in 1965, and we could give you the information as to how much we made from linings and how much we made from handkerchiefs, and you'd be in a different world. I mean, the important thing was how we looked at running money and what we would do about things over time. And it just — you could have gotten very misled — if you'd read it in 1980 or '85 and you looked for great detail on how See's candy was doing because they moved eastward, you know, we'll tell you that overall that failed, in terms of moving out the territory. But going into a whole lot of detail that might be very interesting to an analyst, but really for the shareholder, they've got to make a decision as to who's running their money, and how they're running it, and what they've done over time, and what they hope to do in the future, and how to measure that. And again, we're writing it for the individual. Charlie?

**Charlie Munger**（01:17:51）：Well, I suppose I should be watching every tiny little business down to the last nickel, but I don't. And I don't want that much detail. And I think our competitors would like it, and it wouldn't do our shareholders any good. So, we'll probably just keep reporting the way we do.

**Warren Buffett**（01:18:06）：You can see how flexible we are here. (Laughter)
