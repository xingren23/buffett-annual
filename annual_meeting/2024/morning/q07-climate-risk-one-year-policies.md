## 问题

气候变化正严重冲击保险业，加州等地因野火、洪水风险和赔付上升，已出现大型机构撤出。贾因先生如何看待这一风险向其他地区扩散？气候因素又如何改变了保险投资的逻辑？

## 回答要点

1. 伯克希尔的再保险合约多数只锁定一年，每年都能重新定价，甚至可以彻底退出，因此反而敢长期留在市场。
2. 气候风险上升意味着价格必须大幅上调，但涨幅难以精确测算，监管又限制提价和撤出某些地区。
3. 包括伯克希尔在内的多家保险公司已退出部分州，监管正变得务实，行业近两年重新回到创纪录盈利。
4. 巴菲特强调气候风险会做大保险业务，但定价错误同样会让公司破产，所以坚持一年一签。
5. 大西洋水温与飓风的路径、强度、频率关系并不确定，不需要一千人研究洋流，只需要一个极聪明的承保人。
6. GEICO 1950 年约 17.5 万张保单、每车约 40 美元，如今每车超过 2000 美元，规模从 700 万美元增至 400 亿美元，通胀是朋友。

## 中文全文（逐段翻译）

> 与下方「英文原文」逐段对应，按发言顺序排列；术语与专名首次出现时保留英文。

**Warren Buffett**（01:05:42）：贝基？

**Becky Quick**（01:05:42）：这个问题给阿吉特，来自 Meher Bharucha。「气候变化似乎在重创保险业：主要玩家因为山火和洪水风险退出加州等市场，赔付同时还在上升。贾因先生怎么看这种风险向其他地区扩散？因为这件事，对保险投资的判断发生了什么变化？」

**Ajit Jain**（01:06:13）：气候变化、气候风险，最近确实以一种非常大的方式成了焦点。对我们来说，缓和这个问题的一个因素——尤其在我们做的部分再保险业务里——是合同责任在多数情况下只覆盖一年。所以一年到期，我们就有机会重新定价，包括干脆退出这项业务，如果我们不喜欢那个定价。正因为每次只押一年，我们反而能比原本更长期地留在这一行。

因为气候变化，价格显然必须上涨。但涨多少，很难做得非常科学。需要涨很多，我们一直在提价，希望跑在曲线前面，但不是所有情况下都能做到。监管机构也不帮忙：他们捆住我们的手脚，让我们难以从某些地区撤出，也难以对某些产品大幅调价。结果就是不少保险公司——包括我们——决定在某些州不做了。我认为监管方现在稍微现实了一点，开始意识到保险公司需要有回报、体面的回报，才会继续投放资本。这是来回的拉锯，最近几年一直是资本提供方吃亏，但我觉得正在回到合理区间。看保险公司最近公布的业绩，大家都在赚创纪录的利润。这显然不会持久，但至少未来几个月，尽管有气候变化、有火灾和洪水风险上升，保险业还是个不错的地方。

**Warren Buffett**（01:08:25）：对，气候变化会抬高风险。说到底，长期看它会把我们的生意做大——前提是我们定价正确，定错了我们一样会破产。而我们绝大多数业务都是一年一签。

我想说的是：评估这件事，我宁可用一个阿吉特，也不要全世界一千个承保人或保险经理。这些因素……拿大西洋飓风来说，那大概是我们最大的风险。大西洋水温当然可以测，暖水对飓风的影响你也知道，但你不一定知道那是好是坏，因为它可能让飓风转向更快，可能改变路径，也改变损失的强度和频率。

我们就一年一签，让阿吉特来承保。我们不必告诉你五年后、十年后会怎样。那些没有保险分析头脑的人对这个题目发的议论，实际上并没有增加我们的知识。（笑）我们收到很多信，写信的人智商肯定不低，但他们确实不懂保险生意。而且我不认为他们在气候变化上是错的。可是如果没有风险，就没有保险业。我们做的就是评估风险，一年一年地评。有些例外，你的决定会延伸到很久以后，那种我们尽量避开。再说一遍，你不需要一千个人去分析洋流之类的东西，你需要一个非常非常非常聪明的人，而我们有。（笑）好。

**Ajit Jain**（01:11:00）：我只补一句：气候变化很像通胀，做对了，它可以成为风险承担者的朋友。

**Warren Buffett**（01:11:12）：对我们来说确实如此。看 GEICO，1950 年大约有 17.5 万张保单，每辆车收 40 美元左右，总量 700 万美元。现在我们每辆车收 2000 多美元。技术进步等等这一切——如果我们死守当年 40 美元那套公式，这门生意会很糟。实际上，车变得更安全，也变得更贵、更难修，加上通胀，一堆事都发生了。所以当年我上门推销时那 700 万美元的生意，现在是 400 亿美元。如果我们活在一个没有通胀的世界里，GEICO 不会是家 400 亿美元的公司。

**Warren Buffett**（01:05:42）：Becky?

**Becky Quick**（01:05:42）：This question's for Ajit. It comes from Meher Bharucha. "Climate change seems to be impacting the insurance industry heavily, with major players pulling out of markets like California because of wildfire and flooding risks, combined with payouts increasing. How does Mr. Jain see this risk expanding to other regions, and how has the thesis on insurance investments changed because of it?"

**Ajit Jain**（01:06:13）：Climate change, climate risk, is certainly a factor that has become has come into focus in a very, very big way more recently. Now, the one thing that mitigates the problem for us, especially in some of the reinsurance operations we are in, is our contractual liabilities are limited to a year in most cases. So as a result of which, at the end of a year, we get the opportunity to reprice, including the decision to get out of the business altogether if we don't like the pricing of the business. But the fact that we are making bets that tie us down to one year at a time certainly makes it possible for us to stay in the business longer-term than we might have otherwise. Because of climate change, clearly, prices need to go up. It is difficult to be very scientific about how much the prices need to go up. They need to go up a lot, and we keep increasing prices and hope we stay above the ahead of the curve. But that doesn't happen in all cases. The regulators don't make it any easier by tying our feet to the ground and making it difficult for us to withdraw from certain territories or to make dramatic changes in the pricing of certain products. As a result of which, a number of insurance carriers, included ourselves, have decided to not write business in certain states. I think the regulators are getting a little more realistic about the and they are waking up to the fact that the insurance carriers need to make some kind of a return, a decent return, for us to keep deploying our capital. It's a constant battle back and forth. It's been against the capital providers these last few years, but I think we're coming back into bounds. If you look at the results that have been recently announced by the insurance carriers, everyone's now making record profits. Obviously, that will not last, but certainly for the next several months I think the insurance industry, in spite of climate change and in spite of increased risk of fires and flooding, it's going to be an OK place to be in.

**Warren Buffett**（01:08:25）：Yeah, climate climate change increases risk. And, you know, it in the end it makes our business bigger over time, but not if we if we misprice them, we'll also go broke. But we do it one year at a time, overwhelmingly. And I would say this: I would rather have Ajit assessing this than any thousand underwriters or insurance managers in the world. I mean, the factors aren't you know, well, we'll take Atlantic hurricanes that which would be probably our biggest risk. You know, there's no question that you can measure the temperature of the water in the Atlantic, and you know what warm water does to hurricanes, but you don't know necessarily whether that's good or bad because it may cause them to turn faster. You know, and it may change the path as well as the intensity and frequency of losses. But we'll write it one year at a time and we'll have Ajit underwriting it and, you know, we don't have to tell you what's going to happen five years from now or ten years from now. And people who don't have sort of analytical insurance minds that comment on this subject really don't expand our knowledge. (Laughs) Now, it's we get a lot of letters from people that I'm sure have good IQs, but they don't really know they don't understand the insurance business. And they're not wrong, I don't think in my mind, about climate change. But if there was no risk there'd be no insurance business. And we're in the business of evaluating it, and we do it one year at a time. And there's some exceptions where you can't do it, where your your decisions extend for a long time in the future. We try to avoid those. But again, you don't need a thousand people analyzing water currents or anything. You need one very, very, very smart guy, and we've got him. (Laughs) OK.

**Ajit Jain**（01:11:00）：Yeah, the only thing I'd add is that climate change, much like inflation, done right, can be a friend of the risk bearer.

**Warren Buffett**（01:11:12）：And it has been for us. If you look at GEICO, it had 175,000 policies, roughly, in 1950, and it was getting roughly 40 bucks a car. So that was 7 million of volume. And, you know, now we have we're getting over two thousand dollars. Well, all the advances in technology and everything like that if we had been wedded to some formula, what we did with 40 dollars, we'd have had a terrible business. But in effect, by making the cars much safer they've also made them much more expensive to repair and a whole bunch of things have happened, including inflation. So now we have a 40-billion-dollar business from something that was 7 million back when I called on it. So, if we'd operated in a non-inflationary world, GEICO would not be a 40-billion-dollar company.
